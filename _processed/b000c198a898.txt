General Background
In 1936, Congress enacted the Randolph Sheppard Act for the purposes of providing blind persons with remunerative employment, enlarging the economic opportunities of the blind, and stimulating the blind to greater efforts in striving to make themselves self-supporting. Under the Act, blind persons are granted priority to operate vending facilities in government buildings. 20 U.S.C. §§ 107(a), (b). Responsibility for the program is divided between state and federal agencies. At the federal level, the Secretary of Education interprets and enforces the provisions of the Act and designates state licensing agencies. 20 U.S.C. §§ 107a(a)(5) and 107b; 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.5, 395.8. At the state level, state licensing agencies (SLAs) implement the program. State participation in the program is voluntary.
A person seeking a position as a blind vendor applies to the SLA and is licensed by that agency. The state agency then applies to the federal government for placement of the licensee on government property. 20 U.S.C. § 107b. Once the state and federal government have agreed on an appropriate location for the vending facility, the SLA is responsible for equipping the facility and furnishing the initial stock and inventory. 20 U.S.C. § 107(b)(2). The blind vendor thereafter operates as a sole proprietor who is entitled to the profits of the vending facility and is responsible for the facility's losses. Among the conditions of state participation in the Randolph Sheppard program is the state's agreement to provide any blind licensee dissatisfied with any action arising from the operation or administration of the vending facility program an opportunity for a hearing before the state agency, 20 U.S.C. § 107b(6). If the hearing does not resolve the matter, the licensee must be given an opportunity to submit any unresolved grievances to an arbitration panel convened by the Secretary of Education. Id. Lynn Kneip is such an aggrieved licensee who petitioned for arbitration.
A hearing was held in this matter on October 27 and 28, 2021, at the offices of the Idaho Department of Labor in Idaho Falls, Idaho. The hearing was transcribed. Both parties had the opportunity to present evidence and make arguments. At the end of the hearing, the parties submitted the matter on oral argument, whereupon the record was closed. Based upon all the evidence presented and arguments made, the Arbitration Panel renders this Opinion and Award.
Issue
The parties and the arbitration panel participated in a virtual pre-hearing conference on September 24, 2021. At that time, the parties stipulated that the issue to be decided is: Whether the Idaho Commission for the Blind and Visually Impaired has made payment to Mr. Kneip in accordance with the prior arbitration award?  If not, what additional monies are owed to Mr. Kneip?
Findings of Fact
The Idaho Commission for the Blind and Visually Impaired (hereinafter "Commission" or "ICBVI") is an administrative agency of the State of Idaho. Pursuant to Idaho Code § 67-5411, the Commission was designated as the SLA for the State of Idaho under the provisions of the Randolph Sheppard Act. Pursuant to that statutory authority, the Commission established the Business Enterprise Program (hereinafter "BEP").
The BEP contracts with various governmental agencies for vending, snack bar, and cafeteria services in government buildings and facilities. The BEP then subcontracts the operation of the vending, snack bar and cafeteria services in those facilities to licensed blind vendors.  The BEP provides training and assistance to the blind vendors, and the BEP purchases and services the vending machines and other equipment used by the blind vendors who work under contract with the BEP.
Pursuant to federal law, the Commission has priority over vending services in most federal buildings in Idaho. When the federal government selects the Commission to provide services to a building through the Randolph Sheppard program, it issues the Commission a permit to provide specific services for a specific building or location, but the location can cancel the permit if the vending, snack bar, or cafeteria facilities are not serviced and operated in accordance with the terms of the permit.
Mr. Lynn Kneip entered the BEP as a trainee in November, 2006. He initially operated under a temporary contract but this was converted to a long term location Agreement on September 1, 2007. He operated a snack bar and vending machines at the TSA/TSB facility. Over time, there were numerous issues and complaints regarding Mr. Kneip's operation of the facility, culminating in a Notice of Termination of Operator's License and Suspension and Termination of Agreement dated March 22, 2012.
Mr. Kneip promptly filed a request for administrative review of the decision, followed by a request for an evidentiary hearing which affirmed the termination. The Administrator entered a Final Order adopting the hearing officer's decision as her own. Mr. Kneip then timely filed a complaint with the United States Department of Education requesting arbitration pursuant to the Randolph Sheppard Act.
The arbitration panel rendered its decision on June 22, 2015. The panel found that the Commission's decision to terminate Mr. Kneip's SEP license and his Agreement to operate the TSA/TSB facility was not supported by substantial evidence because the ICBVI failed to provide Mr. Kneip with the post-license training that he was legally entitled to receive under Idaho's administrative regulations and the Randolph Sheppard Act. The panel stated:
the Arbitration Panel orders the Commission to reinstate Mr. Kneip's license. Within two weeks of the reinstatement of Mr. Kneip's license, the panel orders the Commission to enter into a discussion with Mr. Kneip to determine whether he wishes to reenter the BEP program or whether he is interested in pursuing other employment. Assuming Mr. Kneip wishes to return to the program, the Panel further orders the Commission to provide Mr. Kneip with adequate training on accounting and other financial operations so that he can manage his cash flow properly. At the same time, the Commission must provide Mr. Kneip with any other additional training required to bring him back up to speed as a licensed blind vendor since he has been out of the Program now for over three years.
Finally, as required by the Commission's regulations, "the Program shall restore all rights and benefits to the vendor including compensation for the period of termination calculated at a weekly rate determined by averaging the net income for the facility for the prior federal fiscal year." IDAPA 15.02.30.40.04.
Mr. Kneip's license was reinstated on August 10, 2015. On the same day, the Acting Administrator of the lCBVI wrote to Mr. Kneip's attorney and provided information stating that Mr. Kneip's location earned a total net income of $16, 575.65 in FY 2014 for a weekly average of $318.76. In addition, each vendor received $1,725 in benefits for FY 2014. Since, according to the Acting Administrator, Mr. Kneip's license was terminated for a period of 176 weeks, the total compensation due was $61,941.44.
During Mr. Kneip's period of termination, his location was operated by Travis Beck, another blind vendor who was a member of the Commission and who also operated an additional location. As a result thereof, Mr. Beck incurred labor costs to operate the TSA/TSB location which were subtracted from the gross profit to determine the net income for each month of operation.
Through his attorney, Mr. Kneip objected to the deduction of the labor costs in the calculation of net income, contending that had he been running the facility he would not have incurred such costs and that the net income for the location should include the cost of labor. He contended that he was due $127,610.56 based on the FY 2014 operation of the location.
Thereafter, through his attorney, on September 30, 2015 Mr. Kneip requested an administrative review of the decision with respect to the monies owed to him.
This request resulted in the issuance of a Declaratory Ruling by the Commission on November 3, 2015, in which it found that the ICBVI interpretation of the rule is correct and that the compensation had been correctly calculated. Through his attorney, Mr. Kneip objected to this course of action by the Commission. On November 5, 2015, a request for a full evidentiary hearing was made on Mr. Kneips's behalf.
By letter dated November 12, 2015, the Commission denied the request. Mr. Kneip filed a Petition for Judicial Review and asked that the ICBVI be ordered to hold an evidentiary hearing on the dispute.
During the pendency of the Court action, Mr. Kneip and the Commission, in order for the Commission to use budgeted funds before the end of the fiscal year, entered into a Disbursement Agreement in May, 2016 whereby $62,145,55 was disbursed to Mr. Kneip ($17,000 of which went to his attorney), without either party relinquishing its claims with regard to the total amount due to Mr. Kneip.
On June 27, 2016, the District Court of the Seventh Judicial District of the State of Idaho, in and for the County of Bonneville, entered an Order remanding the matter for a review and evidentiary hearing. An evidentiary hearing was held on November 28, 2016, with a Report to the Administrator filed on March 10, 2017. The hearing officer found the compensation owed to Mr. Kneip to be $61,941.44, based on 175 weeks with an average compensation per week of $351.94.
On April 5, 2017, the Administrator adopted the findings of the hearing examiner and determined that the compensation owed to Mr. Kneip was $351.94 per week for total compensation of $61,589.50. (The Administrator's Final Order indicated that the Commission had overpaid Mr. Kneip for a week, but that it would not seek reimbursement.)
On November 30, 2015, a complaint was filed with the Department of Education, pursuant to the Randolph Sheppard Act, seeking to arbitrate the dispute regarding the amount owed to Mr. Kneip.
The last full fiscal year that Mr. Kneip operated the TSA/TSB facility was FY 2011. During that year, his net profit was $29,069.98 and he received $2,175 in benefits.
175 weeks passed between the date of Mr. Kneip's termination and his reinstatement as a blind vendor.
Discussion
The issue presented is whether the Commission has complied with the Order of the prior Arbitration Panel and paid Mr. Kneip all that he is entitled. The Complainant contends that the ICBVI has not fully complied with that Order in that it deducted labor costs from the net income of the TSA{TSB facility, costs that would not have been incurred had he, rather than Travis Beck, been operating the facility.  Mr. Kneip also argues that because the Commission lost at the arbitration hearing it does not have the right to decide what he is owed: he is entitled to the net income plus labor costs, not simply net income during the 175 weeks that he was not allowed to operate the vending site.
The Arbitration Panel ordered that "the Program shall restore all rights and benefits to the vendor including compensation for the period of termination calculated at a weekly rate determined by averaging the net income for the facility for the prior federal fiscal year." IDAPA 15.02.30.40.04 The parties focused on a disagreement as to the meaning of "net income." This arbitration panel has also focused on the issue of what constitutes the "prior federal fiscal year."
It is Mr. Kneip's contention that net income is something different than net profit. ICBVI argues that the terms are synonymous. According to Mr. Kneip, net income does not include labor costs. It is his position that he was taught this in a training module offered by the Hadley School for the Blind. He was not, however, able to produce documentary evidence or testimony to support this contention.
A review of the monthly Business Enterprise Program Location Reports prepared by Mr. Kneip for the months of August and September, 2011, reveals that he subtracted labor costs from gross income to determine gross profit. The net profit was then determined by subtracting a 10% set-aside from the gross profit. The report form does not provide a separate category for net income. The Idaho Commission for the Blind and Visually Impaired Allowable Costs Performance Standards Manual, Business Enterprise Program, defines net profit as "the amount remaining after deducting the set aside (presently 10% of the gross profit) from gross profit". It also defines gross profit as "the amount remaining from the sale of merchandise of a facility, all vending machine income, and other income accruing to the vendor from the facility after deducting allowable expenses of sales, but excluding set-aside charges required to be paid by the vendor". The formula used to calculate the gross profit on the set aside report is as follows: Gross Income - Allowable Expenses = Gross Profit. The allowable expenses include food or product purchases, labor expenses (wages, benefits, and employment taxes) and Miscellaneous General Expenses, including accounting fees, license fees, insurance, laundry, mileage, permits, telephone, advertising, paper, office supplies and other pre-approved expenses.
To complicate the matter further, the Code of Federal Regulations, at Title 34, Part 395, Vending Facility Program for the Blind on Federal and Other Property, in subpart A - Definitions (k), defines net proceeds as "the amount remaining from the sale of articles or services of vending facilities, and any vending machine or other income accruing to blind vendors after deducting the cost of such sale and other
expenses (excluding set-aside charges required to be paid by such blind vendors)." That is, it appears that net proceeds and net profit are the same. This Panel finds that they are also the same as net income.
Accordingly, we find that the Commission did not err in the manner in which it calculated the amounts due to Mr. Kneip, and that labor costs were properly deducted from gross profit in order to determine net income. We, however, find that the ICBVI erred in basing its calculations on FY 2014. In order for the Commission to comply with IDAPA 15.02.30.40.04, the calculations should have been based on the average weekly earnings of the facility in FY 2011. The ambiguity in the meaning of the "prior federal fiscal year" was raised sua spante by members of this arbitration panel. Mr. Kneip had no opinion as to the correctness of the panel's inquiry as to the proper year. The Commission, however, argued that it had made the decision to use FY 2014 in good faith and that Mr. Kneip's prior attorney had utilized the same year in her calculations of the sums owed to Mr. Kneip. His attorney argued that labor costs should have been included in the net income since Mr. Kneip had incurred virtually no labor costs and "the Commission's interpretation could mean that the Commission could operate the vending facility so that
it showed NO "net income" whatsoever, which would nullify the effect of having the regulation in the first place."
It is precisely because the Commission could have operated the vending facility so that there was no net income, or could have closed the facility altogether, that the undersigned panel members find that FY 2011 is the appropriate year from which to make the calculation of what is owed to Mr. Kneip. It is clearly the intent of the Order of the prior Arbitration Panel and of the administrative rule to return an improperly terminated vendor into the position he would have been had he not been wrongfully terminated. That is, he should not be harmed by the termination as clearly has happened in the use of FY 2014 income that was less than Mr. Kneip had historically earned. Similarly, he should not be unjustly enriched if the facility were to have been operated by a much more successful vendor.
The Commission contends that it's interpretation of the rule and use of FY 2014 is reasonable and that deference should be given to the agency's interpretation. The ICBVI asked that we follow the rationale utilized in the Report to the Administrator as a result of Mr. Kneip's request for a full evidentiary hearing, a prerequisite for filing a complaint with the Department of Education. The Hearing Officer included an expansive discussion of Idaho Law governing the interpretation of agency rules.
Agencies are generally given deference in their interpretation of administrative rules when the agency is responsible for administration of the rule, the agency's construction is reasonable, the language of the rule does not expressly treat the matter at issue, and any of the rationales underlying the rule of agency deference are present. The Idaho courts have found that an agency interpretation of a rule is unreasonable when it "is so obscure or doubtful that is entitled to no weight or consideration." Generally, agency interpretations are considered to be reasonable unless the agency relied on erroneous facts of law in its determination.
There is no question that the ICBVI is responsible for the administration of the rule. However, it's interpretation thereof is not reasonable. The purpose of the administrative rule is to "restore all rights and benefits to the vendor including compensation for the period of termination..." Restoration of compensation for the period of termination requires that the fiscal year prior to the termination be the basis of the calculation of compensation. Doing anything else does not comply with the intent of the rule.
The Commission interpretation that the "prior federal fiscal year" is the year prior to the issuance of the arbitration award, while made in good faith, is entitled to no weight or consideration. In this case, more than three years elapsed between Mr. Kneip's termination and the issuance of the Award. There are too many variables, including the possible closing of the facility in the interim, to conclude that the year prior to the Award is the proper basis for determining compensation.
The record is silent as to whether the Commission has ever interpreted this rule in the past, whether any other vendor has been reinstated and had compensation awarded to him or her. Accordingly, this panel finds no basis upon which to defer to the agency's interpretation. Upon questioning as to why FY 2014 had been used, Administrator Beth Cunningham (who was not at the agency when the prior Award was issued) stated that it had been decided prior to her arrival and she didn't question it. She did not offer an opinion as to whether she thought it was the correct year to consider.
Mr. Kneip is entitled to additional compensation based on the weekly rate determined by averaging the net income for fiscal year 2011. That amount is $43,005.83. (Computation is attached as Exhibit A). As further relief in this matter, Mr. Kneip has asked for attorney's fees, damages, and pre-judgment interest. Although Mr. Kneip retained an attorney for mediation purposes, this panel has decided to follow the original Arbitration Panel and declines to award attorney's fees. Additionally, as both parties prevailed in part, attorneys' fees are not indicated. The record is devoid of the nature of additional damages sought by Mr. Kneip, and, accordingly no additional damages will be awarded.
The original Arbitration Award was issued on June 22, 2015. More than seven years have passed since Mr. Kneip raised his objections to the manner in which the compensation had been calculated. The Commission initially refused to provide him with an evidentiary hearing and issued a Declaratory Ruling denying Mr. Kneip an evidentiary hearing. It forced him to go to Circuit Court for an Order requiring the ICBVI to provide the evidentiary hearing. The results thereof were unsatisfactory, forcing Mr. Kneip to again seek arbitration pursuant to the Randolph Sheppard Act. This Panel concludes that this matter is appropriate for an award of pre-judgment interest from the date of the initial arbitration award. A review of prior arbitration awards under the Randolph Sheppard Act demonstrates that there is precedent for so doing.
The Commission has participated in this arbitration proceeding, and has thereby waived sovereign immunity and agreed to abide by the decision of this panel. Although the Idaho pre-judgment statute does not apply to this matter, ICBVI is an SLA under the Randolph Sheppard Act.  § 107d-l(a) states that if a blind licensee is dissatisfied with "any action" taken or decision rendered as a result of the full evidentiary hearing they may file a complaint with the Secretary of Education who shall convene a panel to arbitrate "the dispute" without any limitation on the arbitrator's remedial authority.
In determining whether a pre-judgment interest award is warranted, we consider the need to fully compensate Mr. Kneip for actual damages suffered, consideration f fairness and the relative equities of the award. An award of interest from the date of the initial arbitration award is appropriate in this case because the purpose of the Randolph Sheppard Act is to benefit the blind vendor, and because the action of the Commission in issuing a Declaratory Ruling disallowing an evidentiary hearing was clearly erroneous and contrary to law.
In federal question cases, the rate of pre-judgment interest is left to the court's discretion. The Panel concludes that the post-judgment rate established by 28 U.S.C. § 1961 is an appropriate rate because the claim arises under federal law. The current § 1961rate is .17% per annum, and the Panel will award pre-judgment interest at that rate from June 22, 2015 to the date of this award.  That amount is $469.86.
Mr. Kneip  presented  testimony  and  documentary  evidence  regarding  issues  at  the  Idaho Commission for the Blind and Visually Impaired. He produced an audit of the Commission for the years 2013, 2014 and 2015 which was issued in 2017. The audit made three findings:
Financial accountability of the Business Enterprise Program (BEP) is not being monitored in accordance with rules and regulations.
Conflicts of interest existed within the Business Enterprise Program (BEP) and the Commission did not have an adequate process in place to address them and ensure fair and consistent treatment for all vendors.
Receipt transactions were not reviewed for accuracy on a consistent basis,
This evidence was adduced to support Mr. Kneip's allegation that the appointment of Travis Beck to operate his facility during the period of his termination constituted a conflict of interest. This may, or may not, be the case. It is not relevant to the issue before this panel of whether Mr. Kneip is owed additional compensation. We have found that he is.
Based on the above and the record in its entirely, the undersigned panel issues the following.
AWARD
The Arbitration Pane finds that the Idaho Commission for the Blind and Visually Impaired has not fully complied with the Arbitration Aware issued in June, 2015. As a remedy, the Commission is ordered to immediately pay to Mr. Kneip the sum of $43,005.83 plus pre-judgment interest in the amount of S469.86.
Post-judgment interest shall accrue at the rate of 0.17% per anrurn from the date of this Award until payment in full is made.
