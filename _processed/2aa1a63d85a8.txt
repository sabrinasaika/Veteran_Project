I. PROCEEDINGS/JURISDICTION
In July, 2019 John Bechtel (Claimant) applied to the Business Enterprise Program (SEP) of the Washington State Department of Services for the Blind (DSB) to be considered for contract to provide vendor services at a Washington State Department of Labor and Industries (L&I) cafeteria in Tumwater, WA. DSB Ex 2. His application was denied and, as a licensee under WAC 67-35-490, Claimant sought Administrative Review, per WAC 67-35-470. DSB Ex 3. The DSB Deputy Director, Michael MacKillop, subsequently supported the BEP decision. Thereafter, on August 13, 2019, Claimant sought a Full Evidentiary hearing, which was in turn convened on September 12, 2019, under the auspices of the state Office of Administrative Hearings. In an Initial Order of September 25, 2019, Administrative Law Judge Dan Gerard affirmed the DSB/BEP decision to deny Claimant's vendor application. DSB Ex 7.
On October 14, 2019, Claimant then filed a complaint with the Washington State Licensing Agency against the DSB alleging a violation of the federal Randolph-Sheppard Act (the Act) and its implementing regulations (20 U.S.C §§ 107d(a) and 107d-2, and 34 CF.R. § 395.13). On October 17, 2019, the BSD forwarded the associated petition to convene an Ad Hoc Arbitration panel to its Randolph-Sheppard Act Liaison at the US Department of Education Rehabilitative Services Administration.
In letter dated April 8, 2020, the Department of Education, through its Office of Special Education and Rehabilitative Services (USDE/RSA), convened and empowered an ad-hoc arbitration panel to hear and render decisions on the issues raised by Claimant against the DSB. The panel was authorized to proceed under relevant provisions of the Act, guided by the Policies and Procedures for Convening and Conducting an Arbitration (under the authority of 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2). Program Specialist James McCarthy was designated the USDE/RSA coordinating contacting person. He thereafter served as the central organizing and administrative authority for the subsequent process by which the instant tripartite Arbitration Panel (Panel) was selected by the parties. The Claimant chose and appointed Joel Parsons as Panel Member. The DSB chose and appointed Sheila Huber as Panel Member. The parties then jointly selected FMCS-listed Michael G. Merrill, professional neutral and Labor Arbitrator, and appointed him to serve as Panel Chair.
Panel Chair Merrill accepted his appointment on or about September 15, 2021, and executed a USDE contract for services on case R-S/20-02 on or about November 4, 2021. The Neutral Panel Chair Letter was then forwarded by McCarthy on December 21, 2021. This letter included a packet of procedural history documents as well as specific instructions for hearing procedures (including special considerations in use during the COVID era) and, finally, it included the contact information for fellow Panel Members Parsons and Huber.
Subsequently, in jointly-shared electronic mail exchanges the full panel and the parties mutually agreed to their choice of hearing procedures and timing. A hearing date of February 3, 2022, was chosen. A Settlement Conference preceded the hearing on that date. The hearing was held remotely, with shared video and audio connections (via MS Teams) coordinated by Official Reporter Jacqueline Denlinger of Heritage Reporting Corp of Washington, DC.
Appearing before the Panel representing the DSB was Lianne Malloy, Senior Counsel for the Attorney General of the State of Washington. Claimant John Bechtel represented himself. Witnesses were sworn and testimony and evidence was received as the hearing proceeded in orderly fashion under the auspices of the Panel Chair, as assisted by fellow Panel Members. The hearing was concluded on February 3, after the parties offered closing arguments on the record verbally. The Official Transcript was received on or about February 17, 2022. The Panel Members then followed their own agreed-upon format for deliberation, which included multiple exchanges (also via remote electronic means). The instant Decision results.

Ill. SUMMARY STATEMENT /FINDING OF CASE FACTS
The Department of Services for the Blind (DSB) is a Washington state agency created to provide services to eligible blind persons. RCW 74.18.030. The Department manages the Business Enterprise Program (SEP), a "unique" program operated under the authority of the Randolph­ Sheppard Act (Act). TR 12. The BEP offers opportunities for eligible blind adults to become business owners and operators of cafeterias, coffee stands, and such, in government buildings throughout the state of Washington. TR 13.
John Bechtel (Claimant) is legally blind, and states he has been involved in cooking and business operations for most, if not all, of his adult life. DSB Ex 2; TR 106; 118. In 2016 he trained to become and was accepted as a licensee under the BEP, and as such became eligible to apply to gain contracts for business opportunities under the program. DSB Ex 2; TR 106. This training included an overview of good business financial practices, specifically including training on the importance of paying taxes. TR 46; 48; 106. In July 2017 Claimant was awarded a DSB contract to operate an espresso bar at a state office. DSB Ex. 8. Under terms of the Operating Agreement, Claimant agreed "to comply with all city, county, state and federal laws, including...payment of all taxes " Id.
Claimant ceased operations under this contract in April 2018. DSB Ex 2. Claimant states his accountant at the time, who he believed was DSB-required, told him $5,000 in business taxes remained due and owing. TR 91-92; 103. Claimant reports he paid that amount. Id. Claimant testified he then moved to Oregon. TR 92.
In February, 2019, Claimant admits being upset to learn he owed a sum of at least $7000 in taxes to the Washington State Department of Revenue (DOR). Id. This debt remained unpaid at the time of hearing. TR 106. A Warrant for Unpaid Taxes was filed in Thurston County Superior Court on August 22, 2018. DSB Ex 5. The amount listed is $7660.35. Id. Since that date, the amount has increased due to interest and penalty charges, and exceeded $17,000 at time of hearing. TR 92.
In the summer of 2019 an opportunity came up to operate a cafeteria at the Washington State Department of Labor and Industries (L&I). TR 58. The contract was open to eligible vendors and licensees under the DSB and was managed by the BEC. TR 31-32; 58. BEC sent a notice announcing the opportunity to all program licensees, including the Claimant (who remained an active licensee at the time, even though he held no active contracts). TR 13.
On July 1, 2019, DSB/BEP contract specialist Liz Tunison advised in the notice that the deadline for submitting a licensee bid application for the contract would be set at 4:30 p.m., July 16, 2019. Id. Normally, according to Tunison, this open application period is ten days, but due to the intervening Independence Day holiday an internal decision was made to extend the period.  Id.
Claimant received this notice, including the deadline notice. DSB Ex 1; TR 13.
In an email on July 8, 2019, Claimant informed Tunison that he intended to apply for the vendor contract and requested information about the specific operation. BSD Ex 1. Tunison replied the next morning regarding the possible information sources, but then sent a second email the same day which stated in material part, "...before you submit a bid on the facility, we want to make sure all your taxes are update (sic) with Department of Revenue as this is a requirement." Id.; TR 18-20.
DSB's rules governing the BEP program set out certain eligibility requirements to bid on a vendor location. DSB Ex 2; 3. One of those requirements states that the bidder must "[b]e current in the payment of all federal and state taxes, Social Security taxes, unemployment taxes, and worker's compensation." Washington Administrative Code (WAC) 67-3 070(7); DSB Ex 4, TR 20-21. The purpose of this rule is to ensure that vendors in the BEP program are practicing good business principles and to protect both the vendor and the BEP program from DOR freezing business assets or seizing business property - including property leased by DSB to the vendor. TR 54-55.
Claimant responded to Tunison's July 9 email that same day, informing her that he would apply for the L&I contract under an LLC and not as a sole proprietor. DSB Ex 1. He indicated he did not believe that an LLC would be liable for any tax debt owed by him as a sole proprietor. Id. Tunison responded by return email that the contract would be awarded to an individual "applicant" and that under the DSB rules for the vendor operation program, the applicant must be current in payment of all federal and state taxes. Id. Tunison then sent a copy of the rule CvVAC 67-35-070) setting out eligibility requirements to all licensees. Id.; TR 18-21.
On July 10, 2019, Claimant signed a Declaration of Intent to submit a bid on the L&I contract. DSB Ex 2. The Declaration was sent to DSB before the July 16, 2019, deadline. The Declaration states, in part:
(1) I am current in my payment of taxes, debts to suppliers, wages to employees, and all other expenses, according to accepted business practices.
(2) I meet all of the requirements of an "eligible vendor" as outlined in WACs 67-35-040, 67-35-045 and 67-35-070.
DSB Ex 2; TR 101-102; 22-23.
When processing applications or bids, DSB staff try to assist interested licensees in meeting the eligibility requirements. DSB Ex 1. If it appears a licensee may not initially meet the requirements, DSB attempts to help them resolve issues so that their bid applications can be timely submitted and considered. TR 14; DSB Ex 1.
While reviewing Claimant's application for eligibility, Tunison learned of the tax debt owed to DOR. In addition to her earlier emails, she emailed Claimant again on July 19, 2019, to let him know that DSB would need verification that his tax payments were up to date before they could move forward with his application. BSD Ex 1. She asked him to provide proof that DOR had been paid in full on the August 2018 tax warrant; she provided the source of the warrant, its case number, and the exact amount shown. Id. Claimant responded on July 22, 2019, saying he did not owe L&I any money. Because of Claimant's apparent confusion, Tunison began efforts of her own to check with L&I on any money due to that agency, while reiterating in a response email to Claimant that DSB must have proof from the DOR that the taxes were paid or would be cleared. Id.
Later that same day Tunison wrote Claimant again, reporting her independent contacts with L&I on Claimant's behalf had confirmed L&I had no charges owing from him; at the same time, she provided the telephone numbers for DOR. Id. The next day, Tunison wrote yet another email, gave two separate DOR contact numbers again, and suggested Claimant "[e]xplain to them you have a business opportunity that hinges upon having a clear tax record and I'm sure they'd be happy to resolve it. Especially if you can pay them immediately." DSB Ex 1; TR 23-26.
For his part, Claimant called DOR during this time, but did not ask them about a resolution. Instead, he asked only for the total amount owed. TR 103-104. In an email dated July 23, 2019, he advised Tunison that he was willing to write a check to DOR in an amount up to $25,000, if necessary, however, "I'm not going to write the check [unless] I get the position." DSB Ex 1; TR 103- 104.
Early the next day, on July 24, 2019, Tunison reports she received a voicemail from Claimant. Her email of that date indicates she heard Claimant repeat the position he took in his email the day before ("I understand per your voicemail that you are willing to pay the Department of Revenue the debt if you were to win the bid..."). DSB Ex. 1. Tunison wrote she would forward Claimant's message to the "team." Id. She then repeated the BEP licensee rule about being free of any tax debt when bidding on a facility. Id. She stated that she was just looking at the rules and making sure that all licensees applying for the L&I facility were qualified. Finally, Tunison advised Claimant that she had heard his request to be allowed to move to the interview stage the follow Monday, and that the BEP team would discuss what steps to take next. Id. She forwarded his voicemail on to Jim Hemmen, DSB's new BEP program manager. DSB Ex 1; TR 26.
The "team" referenced by Tunison included Hemmen, as well as representatives from the State Committee of Blind Vendors. TR 61. The committee is a representative group of blind vendors elected by the vendors themselves, and its existence and use is required both by WAC code and the Act. TR 78. The committee agreed with DSB that Claimant was not eligible to apply for the L&I position. TR 61.
On Wednesday, July 24, 2019, Hemmen wrote to Claimant informing him that DSB would give him until 4:00 p.m. Friday, July 26, 2019, to provide proof, by way of a certified letter, that his DOR account was paid in full. DSB Ex. 4. The 4:00 p.m. deadline was to give Mr. Hemmen time to provide all eligible bids to the interviewers by the end of the business day on Friday, July 26. TR 76. The interviewing panel was scheduled to begin interviews at 8:00 a.m. on Monday, July 29, 2019. Id. (In the event, Hemmen was unable to meet his deadline and did not provide the bids until just before the interviews began. TR 76-77.) The letter from Mr. Hemmen to Mr. Bechtel was sent by email at 3:59 p.m. on July 24, 2019, and again at 8:35 a.m. on July 25, 2019. DSB Ex. 4; TR 60-62.
As per what he described was his usual practice with DSB/BEP participants, Hemmen explained he sent this message in multiple formats (including an email containing an accessible word document) to allow persons with limited visual acuity to read the documents in a choice of methods. TR 62. There is no evidence in the record that this Wednesday (and Thursday) message from Hemmen, laying out the timing deadline coming at close of business Friday, was not received by the Claimant.
Claimant did not provide proof that he had paid his tax obligation by the deadline set by DSB and his application to bid on the opening at L&I was withdrawn. TR 66. Tunison described the application review process as including an "Applicant Eligibility Worksheet." TR 34. This two-sided single-page document lists items of review and uses check-off boxes to indicate their "Yes/No" findings. Id. The form includes the item: "Be current in all Federal & state taxes." Id.; DSB Ex. 3. As the record amply shows, a "Yes" is required here. When the DSB team reviewed the Claimant's application, "No" was circled, and the handwritten note added: "DOR $7660.35". Id. The application was not forwarded to the interviewers, Claimant was not permitted to interview for the opening, and the subsequent dispute led to the instant Arbitration.
IV. CENTRAL ISSUE
Whether the DSB violated the Act and implementing regulations by not including Claimant's bid among those that received consideration, including an interview, for the available position at issue.
V. SUMMARY POSITIONS OF THE PARTIES
DSB
The Randolph-Sheppard Act expressly authorizes state business enterprise programs to adopt laws and regulations sufficient to run the program. A state is required to apply controls that assure the program will operate effectively, and that will ensure the program complies with all relevant federal and state laws.
Washington state law requires payment of applicable taxes, and the BEP requires that any licensee applying to bid for a contract must be current in all tax payments. This requirement is set forth on the face of the bid application itself. The applicant must sign the document to confirm they do not owe any back taxes. While Claimant did sign that document in July, 2019, his confirmation was not true. Claimant now admits that he knew even in February of 2019 that he had a significant tax debt owed to the Department of Revenue.
When this fact became known to BEP, its representatives worked with Claimant to bring him into compliance. Claimant was told of the liability, and its impact. Claimant was given a deadline to become current in his taxes and was told the consequences of failure. The email records show this process at work.
The first deadline passed on July 16 with no change. The deadline was extended to July 26. Still, there was no change on that date, a Friday. Instead, Claimant now says that he would have complied if allowed to on July 29, the Monday that the interviews were to take place for successful applicants. This claim differs from the email record (which records Claimant as saying that the would write the necessary check, but not unless he got the position he was applying for). But, this change of position does not matter, as the fact remains that the deadline was on July 26, and on that date Claimant was not current on his taxes and was not eligible for consideration.
Payment of taxes is not a little thing. Paying taxes is an important part of running a successful business. Paid taxes reasonably indicate the business is profitable, and thus will be sustainable. Further, a vendor with tax debt is vulnerable to frozen accounts by the Department of Revenue. A vendor with a frozen account could be locked out their business, and the BEP would be responsible for paying the debt in order to preserve the business site in order to allow another vendor to apply and take it over. If a vendor is behind on taxes and is shut down, it puts the entire enterprise effort at risk. Requiring all applicants to be current on taxes is reasonable and necessary.
The record is very clear. The facts are not complicated and the law is not complicated. Claimant failed to comply. His application did not meet a reasonable and important requirement that he was fully aware of, and he was given time to fix the situation. Yet, as of the deadline on July 26, he was not eligible for the interviews of eligible applicants on July 29, so he was not interviewed. This decision must be affirmed.
Claimant
The record shows that Claimant chose not to present an oral argument at the close of the evidentiary portion of the hearing.   Instead, he elected to stand on the record he made in presenting his case as a witness. TR 123; 126. That record, and the argument it very clearly contained, may be summarized here with fidelity to the manner in which it was delivered. We are able do so because when Claimant testified under his own form of examination, he offered the argument-sourcing narratives that follow, differing here in order and structure, but not in substance.
Claimant, a man of 56 years, has been a cook for most of his adult life. He has operated businesses for some 30 years. As a legally blind person, in roughly 2015 he associated with the DSB/BEP and began on-the-job training and an internship as he worked to become a licensee in the program. He succeeded in two or three years and earned a bid as a BEP vendor operating a cafe roughly between 2017 and 2018. He believed he paid all taxes when that tenure ended, writing a check for a bill of some $5000. Claimant was, in fact, relying on an accountant he believed he was required to use by the BEP itself, who told him that amount cleared his liability.
For that reason, Claimant was surprised (and angered) to learn in February of 2019 that a debt of at least $7,000 was owed. The debt grew over time after discovery, and after many months become a debt of many thousands more because of added penalties and interest. After his cafe closed in 2018 Claimant had moved entirely out of state while those charges and fees were being added. He soon ceased travel because of COVID, and he had no presence in Washington until the events at issue much later.
By Friday July 26, 2019, Claimant was fully aware of the debt, and fully able to pay it even though it stood at approximately $25,000 at that point. However, his debit card limit is far below this amount, and Claimant knew an in-person payment was now required He had been trying to reach Jim Hemmen, the BSE person in charge, to tell him he was willing and able to come up and pay it in order to get on the interview list for the following Monday, July 29. When he finally got his call back from Hemmen, it was at 4:45 p.m. Friday afternoon. When Hemmen told him he would have to have a certified letter to his office by 5:00 p.m. confirming the payment, Claimant knew that was impossible. Claimant was in Oregon, as Hemmen knew.
Claimant therefore pleaded with Hemmen at that late hour to be allowed to fly in on the weekend, pay the taxes Monday morning, then interview later that day. This was the only way Claimant knew to remedy the situation, as he also knew a "certified letter" via the post office would be impossible to get to Hemmen by Monday, much less by 5:00 or even 5:30 that same day. In fact, that demand was almost literally impossible because Hemmen surely had to know, as Claimant did, that his office uses a PO Box and does not even take mail at his office address.
Claimant discovered for the first time during this arbitration hearing that the "certified" proof Hemmen wanted was not so much a letter, but instead a receipt that could be proven as certified in the sense of proven reliability as documented payment. With that understanding, Claimant is certain he could have succeeded and met the BEP requirements in full. All would have been satisfied if BEP would have simply accepted the certified, date-stamped, receipt from the Department of Revenue obtained by Claimant at 8:00 a.m. Monday and delivered before the start of any interviews at 9:00 a.m.
As for the DSB's claim that Claimant had given contrary understandings and put conditions on his willingness to pay, those are the product of misunderstanding. He did not mean to put out a statement that if he did not get the final bid award he would not pay the taxes. What he was trying to communicate was that he did not want to buy a flight to Washington to pay the taxes in person unless he was in fact going to get an interview. An interview was all that he needed to be assured of; since he would be flying up for that, it allowed the in-person payment at the same time.
Some of the confusion in reading Claimant's emails is explained by the visual limits he must deal with. Claimant has to use a form of dictation software that ends up with things the software types that are not necessarily what he, as speaker, said. There is often a difference - that a sight impaired person cannot see - between what the software comprehends him saying and what he meant to put forward. The emails in the record as exhibits show this problem clearly in multiple places.
Finally, even the limited and demanding timing Hemmen required wasn't real.
Hemmen insisted that he had to have the certified evidence of payment by the end of his workday on Friday. But, he admitted that he didn't even turn in the finished application packets to the interview group until that following Monday morning. This is further proof that the offer Claimant made to show up with the documented payment early Monday would have put him in the same position as all other applicants ready to be interviewed later that morning.
There is no reason why DSB should have denied Claimant's application. They made unreasonable timing demands, even practically impossible demands, and rejected Claimant's reasonable and practical offers of ways to satisfy their demands. Claimant was willing to clear his debt as necessary to obtain the interview he applied for and deserved.
VI. DISCUSSION, ANALYSIS, and CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
The Arbitration Panel's Authority and the Burden of Proof
The jurisdiction and authority of the Arbitration Panel is fully established. The record presented to the Panel Chair at time of appointment, and discussed in the "Proceedings/Jurisdiction" section hereinabove, satisfies any question as to the propriety of the Panel's formation and authority. Indeed, there has been no challenge to the jurisdiction from the parties.
The parties have also not argued matters relating to the burden of proof or persuasion. Their stated positions did not include reference to same. As such, and especially where (as will be seen) such considerations do not impact the ultimate finding, the Panel need not take it upon itself to belabor the proceeding with sua sponte efforts to determine where such burdens may, or may not, lie.
In reaching the remaining conclusions of law, the following portion of this discussion and analysis will be divided into two sections that cover the essential areas of concern on the merits of the issues presented. Those are, generally speaking, (1) did the DSB properly apply the Act, and its enabling legislation, both state and federal, and (2) when exercising discretion in its application, did DSB act reasonably with sound judgment and without abusing that discretion?
The Controlling Policy, Law, and Regulation
Washington's Business Enterprise Program for blind vendors was established by the legislature in 1983, which states in its enacting legislation:
The purposes of the business enterprises program are to implement the Randolph-Sheppard Act [20 U.S.C. § 107, et seq.] and thereby give priority to qualified blind persons in operating vending facilities on federal property, to make similar provisions for vending facilities in public buildings in the state of Washington and thereby increase employment opportunities for blind persons, and to encourage blind persons to become successful, independent business persons.
Laws of 1983, ch. 194 §§ 20-23; codified, as amended, at RCW 74.18.200-.230.
RCW 74.18.060(2) is the enabling provision that gives DSB authority to create rules necessary to carry out the legislation relating to programs for the blind.
The application and bidding process to operate vending facilities under DSB's Business Enterprise Program is contained in the agency's program rules. WAC Chapter 67-35. The rules define who is eligible to bid for operating vending facilities and describe the notification and selection process. WAC 67-35-070.
The rules require DSB to send a "notice of availability" to all vendors and licensees whenever a primary location vending facility becomes available. The notice must state a closing time and a date for submitting bid applications. WAC 67-35-060; WAC 67-35-070. "Bids received after the closing time and date will not be considered and the bids will be returned to the bidder." WAC 67-35-070.
In this case, on or about July 1, 2019, DSB sent a notice of availability of a primary vending location at the Washington Department of Labor and Industries to all vendors and licensees. DSB Ex
DSB set a closing time and date of Tuesday, July 16, 2019, at 4:30 p.m. Id.
In order to be eligible to bid on a contract for vendor services at a primary location, the licensee must meet a number of requirements. The applicable requirement here is that the licensee must "[b]e current in the payment of all federal and state taxes, Social Security taxes, unemployment taxes, and worker's compensation." WAC 67-35-070(7).
By at least July 9 or 10, 2019, Claimant was aware of the requirement regarding taxes. He had knowledge of the requirement from DSB-sponsored training sessions, his prior BEP contract in 2017, the bid application form, and communications exchanged with Tunison. DSB Ex. 1, 2, 8; TR 45-48; 118; 124-125. In fact, there is nothing in the record to indicate at any point Claimant has denied knowing about the existence of this requirement. TR.
As for the debt, Claimant knew as early as February 2019 that he owed taxes to the State of Washington DOR. TR 91-92. Although he was upset with his accountant for allegedly providing an incorrect tax figure, Claimant did not dispute that he owed the tax debt. Id. During the time of the bidding process, Tunison repeatedly and vehemently encouraged him to work with DOR to get the tax issue resolved in order to be eligible to bid on the L&I contract, but he failed to take that action and has continued to fail to pay his tax debt. DSB Ex. 1; TR 68; 92.
On July 16, 2019, at 4:30 p.m. - the closing time and date of the bid applications on the L&I location - Claimant was not eligible to bid on the contract because he was not current in his payment of state taxes, WAC 67-35-070(7), and DSB had authority to reject his application at that time.
This, however, did not end the matter. While the impact of the applicable law, regulation and policy is undeniably clear, the timing of the imposition of consequences to Claimant under that law, regulation, and policy is significant to our discussion and analysis here and requires additional attention. As will be evident, while the DSB has leeway in this regard, its decisions must be reasonable under all the circumstances.
The Discretionary Timing Decisions
In consultation with the State Committee of Blind Vendors, DSB, acting within its discretion, provided an exception to its rules and granted Claimant a 10-day extension of time (from July 16 to July 26, 2019) to demonstrate he was eligible to submit a bid application. The extension and new deadline came after several earlier consultations about Claimant's ineligibility due to unpaid taxes. He did not take advantage of the consultations and he did not take advantage of the additional time. He did not pay his tax debt and did not contact DOR to attempt to resolve the outstanding tax debt. When the business day ended at DSB on July 26, 2019, opportunities for repair ended and the record reflects Claimant's application was finally rejected.
Claimant comes to hearing arguing DSB's timing demands were unreasonable. He does not contest the fact of the applicable law requiring applicants to be free of tax debt. He does not contest the fact that he carried a tax debt at the time of his application. Rather, he contests the discretionary timing decisions made by DSB. He submits that in addition to the 10-day extension, he should have been given an additional three days, and allowed to bring himself within the applicable law by making his tax payment on the morning of the successful-applicant interviews on July 29, 2019. TR 92-93. The question becomes whether DSB abused the discretion it holds by extending the time limits to July 26, but not to July 29.
The proper scope of review is found at 5 U.S.C. § 706, and is known as the "arbitrary and capricious" standard of the Administrative Procedure Act. Marsh v. Or. Natural Res. Council, 490 U.S. 360, 109 S. Ct. 1851, 104 L. Ed. 20 377, 29 ERC 1508 (1989), Court Opinion. This provides, in material part:
To the extent necessary to decision and when presented, the reviewing court shall decide all relevant questions of law, interpret constitutional and statutory provisions, and determine the meaning or applicability of the terms of an agency action. The reviewing court shall -
compel agency action unlawfully held or unreasonably delayed; and hold unlawful and set aside agency action, findings, and conclusions found to be -
arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law
5 U.S.C. § 706.
The total record in this case provides a ready answer to the charge that DSB set its chosen deadlines arbitrarily and capriciously, or otherwise abused its discretion. The record, largely through the Hemmen testimony and evidence, is replete with information on how and why the timing decisions were made. First, Hemmen consulted with the Committee of Blind Vendors before taking any action. Mr. Hemmen also considered Claimant's stated ability to pay his taxes (first communicated to Tunison), his delays in paying, and his reluctance to pay.
But, and importantly, Hemmen also considered the fact that the interviews were scheduled to begin first thing on Monday morning, July 29, 2019, and that DSB planned to provide the eligible applicants' information to the interviewers late Friday afternoon, July 26. TR 76-77. This consideration is of import because it reflects the fact that other parties, other licensee applicants as well as the L&I parties on the other side of the contracting and decision-making process, were involved here. They too had interests and needs to be served by the DSB, notably to (1) have the process move with predictability and expediency, and (2) to have as far in advance as reasonably possible the information needed to make sound choices between and among applicants.
Hence, the period for applicants to satisfy known eligibility requirements could not be open­ ended; it could not be extended at the will or request of a single applicant without impact to others. The record reflects the July 29 date for interviews had been long set, and parties on all sides had been given the date to rely upon and prepare for. Based on all the varied interests at stake and considering the documented history of Claimant's application, Hemmen reasonably chose the Friday before the Monday morning interview process as the last possible date for submitting a qualified application.
The Claimant makes much of his offer to fly as necessary to appear on Monday morning, make his payment in person, and remedy the problem with his application. He emphasizes the "impossible" nature of moving a "certified letter'' overnight, or even over a weekend, to arrive at a PO Box. These observations on known postal timing (while correct) do not militate in Claimant's favor. This is because they ignore his decisions and actions, or lack thereof, in the time prior to the point he emphasizes. Whether that final offer to Hemmen occurred Friday the 26th, or, as Hemmen recalls and as his letter tends to indicate, on Wednesday the 24th (or even the 25th) is of no matter. What matters here are the days between July 9 and that point.
July 9 is the date that Tunison first wrote directly to Claimant asking about his tax debt status.
Of course, since Claimant admits knowing about his tax debt for months prior, and has never claimed he was unaware of his duty to pay it once he found it, this period could extend much further out. But, assuming arguendo he was unaware of the duty to clear this known debt to meet eligibility requirements until Tunison raised this issue, the analysis will proceed from that point.
Between July 9 to the first July 16 deadline the Claimant had seven calendar days to address the problem effectively. From July 16 and July 26 were at least ten more. Claimant at first denied the debt and attributed it to an error at L&I. He then protested the debt by attributing it to poor advice from an accountant. He floated an idea to hide the debt, in effect, by operating his business "as an LLC" instead of as a sole proprietor. Finally, the first offer to pay came conditioned (clearly unsatisfactorily) upon a successful win of the bid.  By the time Claimant chose to move his plan to the "fly and make an in-person payment", the time was indeed extremely short. But this was entirely due to the Claimant's own prior choices. After delaying payment plans for well over two weeks (if not months), for the Claimant to expect yet another personal and individual extension beyond July 24 is in itself an unreasonable expectation.
The time limits set by the DSB do not represent an error in judgment at any level or of any kind. The timing decisions were based upon articulable, well-considered, and reasonable operational and business needs. Considering all the factors and circumstances. and in view of the entire record, there is no evidence to support a claim that DSB abused its discretion by setting time limits here in arbitrary or capricious fashion, or in any other unreasonable means or manner.
VII. ARBITRATION PANEL MAJORITY DECISION
Following careful consideration and within the bounds of its authority, and based on the above analysis of the entire record and the conclusions of fact and law accordingly made, on a majority basis the Panel decides:
By not including the bid of Claimant John Bechtel among those that received consideration, including an interview for the available position at issue in this matter, the Washington State Department of Services for the Blind did NOT violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act or its implementing regulations, or Washington State law or regulation.
