INTRODUCTION
Pursuant to 20 U.S.C. § 107(d)-l(b), this dispute was placed before this panel of arbitrators (hereinafter "Panel") for a decision in the dispute between the General Services Administration (hereinafter "GSA") and the Oregon Commission for the Blind (hereinafter "OCB") pursuant to the requirements of the Department of Education, Office of Special Education and Rehabilitation Services ("DOE"). Both parties had the opportunity to present evidence, call and cross examine witnesses, and argue their cases. Accordingly, the Panel shall render a decision based upon the Randolph-Sheppard Act (hereinafter "R-S Act"), as well as the testimony and exhibits offered at hearing or as stipulated between the parties. The decision of this Panel shall be the final and binding upon the parties, subject to appeal and review pursuant to chapter 7, title 5 of the United States Code.
As the present dispute involves OCB's challenge to GSA's application interpretation of the RSA to the alleged detriment to the OCB, the Panel invited OCB to be the first to present their case in chief, while assigning OCB the burden of proof by a preponderance of the evidence.
It is further recognized that this burden of proof can shift during the course of the proceedings if the party with the burden of persuasion presents sufficient evidence to justify a finding in their favor, thereby placing the burden to rebut such evidence upon the other party. Likewise a party wishing to assert an affirmative defense shall carry the burden of persuasion in such matters.
The Panel further notes for the record that the hearing was recorded by a court reporting service, and as such, an official transcript of the hearing was made available to the Panel and the parties. The parties agreed to submit a post-hearing brief to the Panel, in addition to closing arguments.
The following issues are before the Panel.
ISSUES
Did OCB waive its right to challenge GSA's awarding of a food service contract in the Edith Green/Wendell Wyatt (EGWW) Federal Building when it failed to file a timely objection? If not, then the Panel shall proceed to answer the next question.
What is the food service facility at the EGWW Federal Building, a cafeteria or vending facility? If not a cafeteria, what is the remedy?
BACKGROUND
This dispute arises out of GSA's decision and subsequent action to award the food service facility in the 18 story Federal office building located in Portland, Oregon by standard competitive contract and not by permit to OCB as the state licensing agent (SLA) under the R-S Act. Prior to 2009, the EG/WW Federal Building (hereinafter "building") included a large cafeteria in the basement operated by a private contractor, as well as a dry stand and vending machines operated by a licensed blind vendor. In 2009 the building underwent an extensive remodel and was reopened in 2013. During the remodel the large cafeteria was significantly decreased in size and scope and relocated to the main floor.
In August of 2012, the GSA issued Solicitation No. GS-10P-12-C-0024 for cafeteria concessions services. In October of 2012, following the closing of the solicitation and receipt of all proposals, but prior to an award of the contract, OCB through the Oregon Department of Justice wrote a letter to GSA indicating that the award was without priority to licensed blind vendors and was in violation of R-S Act. (See GSA Exhibit 3). GSA cancelled this solicitation shortly thereafter and issued a second one in May of 2013. (See GSA Exhibit 6). In October of 2012, GSA informed OCB that it was resoliciting and inviting them to submit their proposal. The letter did not encourage the submission of a permit as allowed under the R-S Act.
In May of 2013, the GSA again invited OCB to submit a proposal for cafeteria services in the building and made it clear that the facility would be operated by contractual agreement and not by permit. On June 18, 2013, OCB rejected the GSA request for proposal and submitted a permit application instead.  (See GSA Exhibit 8).  In September of 2013, OCB made contact with the GSA headquarters in Washington, D.C. questioning whether the food service facility in the building was designed as a cafeteria or some other level of service. On February 11, 2014, GSA awarded a contract for the food service facility to LunchStop, Inc. a California corporation. On August 13, 2014, OCB through the Oregon Department of Justice initiated the subject arbitration with the Department of Education.
POSITION OF THE PARTIES
OCB's Position
OCB contends that GSA's solicitation for a food service facility within the building should not have been awarded by contract but by permit as the design and functionality of such facility is something other than a "cafeteria." OCB argues that both vending machines and operation of the food service area should be awarded on the basis of a permit as what GSA is calling a cafeteria is really a "grab and go" snack bar with a limited menu.
GSA's Position
GSA argues that OCB waived its right to challenge its contract award as it failed to request arbitration in a timely manner. GSA further contends that the food service facility within the building is in fact a cafeteria according to the definition found within the R-S Act. And finally, GSA believes that the procurement process is a matter of administration for the agency and is not an appropriate subject matter for arbitration here. For the record, GSA had mistakenly provided the Panel with a second copy of its Post Arbitration Brief when its Reply Briefs was due on February 1, 2016. This error was corrected on February 10, 2016, and the Panel accepted GSA's Reply Brief at that time.
FINDINGS OF FACT
The Panel will first review the procedural defenses presented by GSA, and if necessary, address the question of whether or not the food service facility within the building is in fact a "cafeteria" according to the R-S Act. The Panel will review the arguments of the parties in light of the burden of persuasion required of the respective parties.
TIMELINESS
GSA contends that OCB failed to request arbitration in a timely manner and therefore OCB has waived its right to challenge GSA's contract award. GSA notes that there are a number of problems associated with OCB's complaint or request for arbitration.
Legal Precedent
GSA sites a number of court decisions involving alleged improprieties with agency contract solicitations that stand for the proposition that a protest of an agency's procurement process must be filed in a timely manner or the protesting party will not be afforded the remedy they seek. At a minimum, a number of these cases would require the protest to be initiated prior to the close of the bidding process. GSA quotes Panel Member Gashel in an article she prepared in 2012 referring to a 2002 Federal Court of Claims case where she was encouraging others to file a protest either prior to the date for receipt of proposals or prior to contract award. GSA further notes that this Panel's ultimate decision is subject to appeal which would be heard at the Federal Court of Claims and hence this Panel should adhere to the precedent that is before it.
What appears to be missing from GSA's thorough case analysis is the question of what constitutes a "protest" when challenging an agency involved in a contract procurement matter. According to GSA's own record of events, it is clear that OCB through the Oregon Department of Justice did initiate a form of protest by letter dated October 10, 2012, (GSA Exhibit 3) which in tum resulted in the agency cancelling their initial solicitation. This solicitation had closed, and proposals had been received, but the award for the contract had not yet been issued. The Oregon Department of Justice letter clearly noted that any contract to award "restaurant, and/or other food service" in the building as had been solicited by GSA would violate the R-S Act.
There is no question that GSA was put on notice as to OCB's perceived problems with the alleged violations of the R-S Act and that it was their clear intention to "take steps necessary if food vendors are awarded contracts within this building in violation of the Act."
The Panel was not directed to any statutory language under the R-S Act that would define the type of "protest" that would otherwise be required to challenge such contract process or the timing for any such protest or even request for arbitration. What is also lacking is any indication that GSA officials met with or discussed their proposed food service facility design with the affected SLA, their solicitation for contract bid with OCB once they learned of concerns surrounding the R-S Act, or anytime after OCB's formal protest. There is also no evidence to suggest that the regional office of the GSA invoked any clarification from its central office or that of the Secretary of Education. Since the design and footprint of the proposed food service facility was so greatly different from the previous cafeteria and was part of the new era for food service facilities, it only stands to reason that a thorough discussion with the possible end users or the Secretary of Education responsible to carry out the purposes of the R-S Act, would have been well advised. In addition to the earlier expressions of concern and threat to take action, we know that OCB submitted a letter of protest and a corresponding application for permit almost 8 months in advance of GSA issuing its contract award.
It should be noted that in recent history between these parties OCB challenged GSA's original solicitation with such challenge occurring after the close of the solicitation process, but prior to an award of the contract. This challenge did not come in the form of a request for arbitration through the DOE, but rather by letter to GSA, which resulted in the cancelling of such solicitation.
For the foregoing reasons, the Panel does not find GSA to have presented legal precedent sufficient to preclude OCB from pursuing the subject arbitration.
Deference to Procurement Agency Interpretation
GSA contends that the Secretary of Education is required to defer to the soliciting agency's definition of a "cafeteria." GSA cites Chevron, USA, Inc. v. Natural  Resources Defense Council, Inc. 467 US 837 (1984) as authority granting an agency's interpretation of a silent or ambiguous statute provided such interpretation is reasonable. Specifically, GSA argues that the term "wide variety" as found within the R-S Act, is silent as to what this term means. The Panel may beg to differ on whether or not these two words require further clarification other than their natural meaning. However, further assistance is provided the Panel when reading these two words in context within the sentence which reads," 'Cafeteria' means a food dispensing facility capable of providing a broad variety of prepared foods and beverages (including hot meals) primarily through the use of a line ... " Miriam-Webster tells us that something is "ambiguous" if it is able to be misunderstood in more than one way. The Panel fully understands the meaning of the statutory language, but realizes how the parties could differ on its application. It is the application of this language and not its interpretation that would otherwise require notice and discussion between the responsible parties to assure the rightful conclusion relative to the type of food service identified under the R-S Act. GSA has clarified this term with greater specificity in a document of their own which will be addressed later.
GSA witness Paul Gallegos testified that the "Act" was ambiguous for a reason. He believed that this would "allow for, you know, you to be able to have some flexibility  in the way you implement." Earlier Mr. Gallegos testified that it made no difference from his point of view whether there was a blind vendor or not as long as the essential service was being provided to the building.   Mr. Gallegos also testified that he could define a "line" to mean, "one inch or one mile," referring to the definition of a "cafeteria" in the R-S Act.  It appears to the Panel that GSA would like to have its proverbial cake and eat it too, as GSA contends that the ambiguous language will allow it to make whatever decision it likes. Since GSA has refined its understandings through documents of their own as noted earlier, we have no need to quibble over one inch versus a mile as it relates to the interpretation given the R-S Act.
Consequently, if the Panel were to defer to GSA's interpretation of what constitutes a "broad variety of prepared foods and beverages" and "line," such deferral would be tantamount to negating the exclusive remedy for any claims of violations of the R-S Act as there could never be an effective challenge to the agency. This Panel is unable to recognize any such limitation to its charge under the R-S Act.
CAFETERIA VERSUS VENDING FACILITY
The question then is whether or not the building currently houses a "cafeteria" or a "vending facility." If it is the former, then the OCB challenge will be dismissed. If it is the latter than the Panel will need to decide what remedy is to be imposed in order to satisfy the R-S Act.
A visit to the actual site confirmed for the Panel Chair that the food service facility in the building is greatly different than what he had personally experienced prior to the 2009 renovation. It is the Panel Chair's opinion that the former food service facility would easily fit the traditional view of a "cafeteria" whereas the current facility is clearly something much different with a greatly reduced footprint. Mr. Smith and Mr. Gallegos both confirmed in their testimony that today's food service facility in the building was a significant variation from the old facility. Mr. Smith also reported that in his conversations with GSA's Melissa Walker, that she noted that. "they made it very clear that, you know, the cafeteria concept was dying, that it, you know, was antiquated, you know, and that they could not imagine a scenario where they would ever go out on the market again for a cafeteria solicitation."
In the instant case the Panel has to look no further than GSA's own determination as to what is the reasonable application for this food service facility. OCB offered Exhibit A6 which includes recent correspondence and an accompanying document between GSA's Ms. Walker and OCB's primary witness Terry Smith. The document is entitled The General Services Administration's Levels of Food Service Operations in Federal Facilities. GSA's witness Paul Gellagos testified that he was unsure when and how this document was prepared though he did acknowledge its existence. Mr. Smith testified that he had requested a copy of this document from GSA's Ms. Walker and that GSA has treated levels 1 to 6 as being conveyed by permit to blind vendors while only level 7 would be let by competitive bid. This statement is confirmed by GSA's Ms. Walker as found in such electronic correspondence with Mr. Smith. Level 7 is entitled "Cafeteria," whereas Level 6 is entitled "Cafe." When comparing the two levels relative to food selection we see that Level 6 may include "a limited line of prepared hot vegetables and entrees each day." (Emphasis added). Level 7 includes those products offered in cafes and "may include a wide range and selection of hot and cold foods each day." (Emphasis added). With these entree distinctions and the fact that there is no cafeteria-style "line," it is clear to the Panel that at the food service facility within the building is something less than a Level 7 operation. As the determination between Level 5 and Level 6 does not affect the outcome here, there is no need to identify the applicable level here.
Comparing this facility with other food service facilities within federal buildings across the country, Mr. Smith testified that he has seen more complex operations within other federal buildings that have been issued permits to operate with blind vendors. Mr. Smith specifically cited the Denver Federal Courthouse as a recent example under GSA control which "has the same variety."
GSA also noted that a contrary interpretation to their "cafeteria" designation would be "unfair to the woman owned small business that successfully bid on the revised contract solicitation. Aside from the fact that the successful bidder is actually a fairly large corporation out of the neighboring state of California, the Panel is charged with a review of the application of the R-S Act, and not the impact that may follow as a result of an agency's disregard for such application.  The time to have considered such matters has long since passed since a decision was made to proceed with the "cafeteria" contract solicitation in spite of OCB's stated concerns.
CONCLUSION
For the reasons noted above, the Panel finds that GSA was not justified in labeling the new food service facility within the EG/WW Federal Building a "Cafeteria," when the facility should have been considered a "Vending Facility" pursuant to the R-S Act. Accordingly, GSA was in error when it refused OCB's application for a permit to operate the food service facility with a blind vendor.
AWARD
The Panel has determined that the food service facility within the EG/WW Federal Building, in Portland, Oregon is a Vending Facility within the R-S Act, and that OCB is entitled to a permit to operate the facility with a blind vendor. This award includes the following remedy:
OCB is to be awarded an operational permit consistent with the R-S Act effective February 11, 2014.
OCB is to be granted lost income from the date the permit is awarded to the date in which OCB has access to fully operate the food service facility within the EGWW Building. Lost income is to be based upon an average of 90% of the realized profit experienced by current vendor, LunchShop, Inc. while managing the facility. The income award will cease when OCB has full access to the facility even if OCB is unable to provide service. GSA may retain current vendor in place until OCB is fully able to perform, or longer should GSA elect to continue with vendor while resuming the lost income damage award. In no event will GSA renew current vendor ' s contract beyond the initial 60 month contract period.
Parties are to negotiate a date when OCB will have access to operate the facility.
OCB will only be required to purchase equipment, tools and signage similar to or comparable with current vendor, with possibility of purchasing current vendor' s equipment at their purchase price less depreciation.
OCB is to be awarded reasonable attorney fees.
