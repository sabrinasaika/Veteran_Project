SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (Section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to 
Section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by Arizona Rehabilitation 
Services Administration (AZRSA). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the process 
by which RSA carried out its activities from February 27, 2024, through February 29, 2024, was 
defined by information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, 
documents, and data submitted by AZRSA, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, 
and technical assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
● Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities;   
● Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

quality employment outcomes; 
● Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities,

● Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported

● Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and

● Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic

funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops 
strategies to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds to 
achieve quality employment outcomes.  
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE  
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by AZRSA on the Case Service Report (RSA-
911) and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 
2021, and 2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and 
requested documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR 
services, quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as 
discussions with AZRSA of potential factors influencing program performance informed the 
analysis and helped to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the 
performance and management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from 
related sources, and discussions with AZRSA during the review, RSA identified various factors, 
which may be contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s performance 
leading to employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of 
Federal funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to 
AZRSA’s requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by the VR agency 
as it develops strategic actions to ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to 
maximize the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with 
disabilities in achieving quality employment outcomes. 
 
Applicant, Eligible Individual, and Participant Attrition 
 
The attrition of applicants, eligible individuals, and participants prior to or after receiving 
services plays a key role in evaluating the performance of the VR agency. Premature or untimely 
exit of individuals with disabilities during the VR process can, among other things, impact

4 
AZRSA’s ability to expend funds to fully support participants with the necessary services to 
achieve their employment goals. When attrition is high, VR agencies should examine the reasons 
for exit and make the necessary adjustments to their operations, policies, and practices to sustain 
engagement and remove unnecessary barriers or contributing factors. AZRSA experienced a high 
rate of attrition during the period under review. The percentage of individuals who exited after 
application prior to being determined eligible averaged 3.73 percent over the three-year review 
period. The percentage of individuals who exited after eligibility but prior to a signed 
individualized plan for employment (IPE) was 20.0 percent in PY 2020, 14.5 percent in PY 
2021, and 15.4 percent in PY 2022. The percentage of participants who exited after IPE, without 
an employment outcome increased from 43.3 percent in PY 2020 to 51.2 percent in PY 2022. 
The reasons most reported for exit during the period of the review included “no longer interested 
in receiving services” (ranging from 35.4 percent in PY 2020 to 29.4 percent in PY 2022) or 
“unable to locate” (ranging from 22.8 percent in PY 2020 to 34.4 percent in PY 2022). These 
reasons suggest issues related to sustained engagement with eligible individuals and participants 
or services that are not sufficiently meeting the needs of participants. 
AZRSA communicated that the attrition of counselors, high vacancy rates, and staff training 
needs all contributed to the untimely processing of applications, eligibility determinations, IPE 
development, and service delays. In the VR services portion of PY 2024-2027 Unified State 
Plan, AZRSA reported that it employed 176 of the necessary 212 VR counselors. With a 16.9 
percent vacancy rate, the ratio of qualified VR counselors to clients was 1:78. The VR program 
shared that the COVID-19 pandemic also affected the participation of individuals in services for 
various reasons, including vendor closures and reluctance to return to in-person employment. 
During the on-site review, AZRSA discussed realigning staff to maximize resources available to 
increase participant engagement. Specifically, AZRSA shared its plans to use rehabilitation 
technicians to engage participants more frequently to provide job readiness training, soft skills, 
and interview skills training. AZRSA plans to implement the use of customer service 
representatives to serve as a point of contact during the rehabilitation process to improve 
communication and connections with VR participants. To expedite the processing of 
applications, AZRSA sought clarification regarding the statutory and regulatory requirements for 
applications and signatures.

Impediments and Practices Affecting the Accurate Reporting and Provision of Pre-
Employment Transition Services 
 
The Rehabilitation Act requires VR agencies to provide or arrange for the provision of pre-
employment transition services to a “student with a disability” as defined in Section 7(37). Early 
engagement and career exploration services expose students to the VR program and services 
available to support their career goals. Although the number of reported students decreased 
during the three-year review period, the total number of students with disabilities (both eligible 
and potentially eligible for the VR program) in receipt of pre-employment transition services 
increased from PY 2020 through PY 2022. Only 24.2 percent of the 8,898 students reported in 
PY 2022 received one or more pre-employment transition service.  
 
AZRSA communicated that the decrease in students reported and the small percentage of 
students in receipt of pre-employment transition services was due, in part, to the closure of 
schools and service providers as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. AZRSA’s practice of 
closing the cases of students who were potentially eligible for VR services after all five required 
activities were provided, rather than after reaching the age at which they no longer met the 
definition of a “student with a disability” also affected the accurate reporting of students with 
disabilities and the provision of pre-employment transition services. The agency’s policy section 
11.1G includes a statement that pre-employment transition services may not be provided if the 
services have been completed and the individual chooses not to apply for VR services. Following 
the provision of all five required activities, the contractor bills AZRSA and the agency sends the 
student a letter to respond within 15 days requesting that the student apply for VR services if the 
student would like to receive VR services or resume receiving pre-employment transition

6 
services. AZRSA communicated that this practice helps staff to manage active cases and reduces 
the burden associated with keeping a student's case open the entire time they meet the definition 
of a “student with a disability” while not actively receiving services. During FFYs 2019 through 
2021, the percentage of funds reserved and expended for pre-employment transition services 
decreased from 13.5 percent to 8.5 percent, respectively. AZRSA communicated that it is not 
able to accurately report all pre-employment transition services provided by VR program staff 
and purchased from vendors, or the service expenditures due to case management system 
limitations. AZRSA anticipates a new case management system to launch in July 2025, which 
will allow AZRSA to report all services being provided.  
 
In July 2022, AZRSA expanded its transition unit to include a pre-employment transition 
services coordinator, two pre-employment transition services specialists, a transition coordinator, 
three transition specialists and a special projects liaison that outreaches to 32 of the 674 school 
districts in the State through its Transition from School to Work (TSW) contracts. AZRSA’s 
central office staff is responsible for tracking and reporting the provision of pre-employment 
transition services to potentially eligible students through case management system case notes. 
RSA and AZRSA discussed the agency’s strategies to improve the provision of pre-employment 
transition services, including maximizing its transition unit and VR counselors as resources to 
directly provide required activities to potentially eligible and eligible students with disabilities, 
and the use of Project SEARCH, paid summer work-based learning experiences, Diverse Ability, 
and the Arizona Youth Leadership Forums, and Arizona Youth Engagement Academy to expand 
services. AZRSA communicated that it is exploring the use of on-line pre-employment transition 
services (i.e., pre-ETS 360 academy curriculum), but has experienced challenges and delays due 
to being required to determine rates of payment with the Arizona Department of Economic 
Security (DES), the umbrella agency over the Division of Employment and Rehabilitation 
Services (DERS), the designated State agency (DSA) and AZRSA, the DSU.  
 
At the time of the review, AZRSA was under a FFY 2021 non-compliance pre-employment 
transition services corrective action plan (CAP) through which RSA was monitoring AZRSA’s 
progress towards expanding the provision of pre-employment transition services and meeting the 
reserve and expenditure requirement. AZRSA achieved compliance with the reserve and 
expenditure requirement in FFYs 2022 and 2023. With AZRSA’s most recent quarterly CAP 
report submission (October 31, 2024) and the FFY 2023 final RSA-17 reports for the agency 
(January 28, 2025), RSA was able to confirm that the State of Arizona exceeded the 15 percent 
reserve and expenditure requirement and resolved the FFY 2021 pre-employment transition 
services non-compliance CAP on February 3, 2025.

Service Provision and Reporting System Limitations 
 
From PY 2020 through PY 2022, the number of applicants and individuals determined eligible 
for VR services increased; however, the number of individuals who received services decreased 
from 13,441 in PY 2020 to 12,639 in PY 2022. Although more applicants are entering the VR 
system and being determined eligible, over half of the individuals exiting the VR program in 
Arizona are leaving after their IPE is developed and without employment; and only a quarter 
(26.9 percent) are exiting with employment.  
 
In accordance with the intent of the Rehabilitation Act, VR agencies are invested in assisting 
individuals with disabilities to reach their maximum potential through the pursuit of careers with 
the opportunity for advancement. An examination of the training, career, and other services data 
that AZRSA reported for PY 2022, revealed the following for training services: 6.3 percent were 
postsecondary education; 3.8 percent were occupational or vocational training; 0.7 percent were 
work-based learning experiences; and data indicated no apprenticeship training, Randolph-
Sheppard entrepreneurial training, or customized training. The largest percentages of services 
provided in PY 2022 were vocational rehabilitation counseling and guidance (91.9 percent); job 
search assistance (25.3 percent); and job readiness training (19.3 percent). Given this 
performance, RSA and AZRSA discussed the need to increase training services and conduct 
labor market analyses to determine the State’s labor market demands and opportunities to 
prepare individuals with disabilities for careers with advancement potential rather than simply 
entry-level employment.  
 
AZRSA reported limited job placement assistance services (less than 2 percent) despite the level 
of job search assistance (25.3 percent) in PY 2022. AZRSA policies bundled job placement with 
miscellaneous training (policy section 10.5), job readiness training (policy section 11.3), and job-
related services (policy section 11.4). The VR agency communicated that case management 
system limitations forced staff to enter bundled services, including job search assistance and job 
placement assistance services. Specifically, at the time of the on-site review, only 28 codes were 
available for VR counselors to choose from a drop-down menu and manually enter on a quarterly 
basis. This programming function impeded AZRSA’s ability to accurately collect and report 
actual services provided, and funds expended. AZRSA clarified that its new case management 
system (to be implemented in July 2025) will correct the inaccurate entry of bundled services.  
 
During the on-site review, AZRSA communicated that some services are not included on the IPE 
and thus, not pulled by counselors and reported on the RSA-911. Specifically, system reporting

9 
limitations require field counselors to manually report services reflected on the IPE and transfer 
them to the RSA-911. RSA instructed AZRSA to require that all individualized VR services be 
included on an IPE for VR participants to receive such services, and to ensure that these services 
are accurately reported on the RSA-911.  
 
RSA and AZRSA also discussed the need to revise policies to address limitations (e.g., the 
number of credits, amount of general school supplies (section 10.1), the number of home (section 
13.4) and vehicular modifications (section 13.3), hours of adaptive driving (section 13.2), hours 
of personal assistance services (section 12.5), number of hearing aids (section 13.5), and a 
requirement for proof of a primary residence to receive maintenance (section 12.2)) that may be 
impeding the provision and accurate reporting of VR services and expenditures.  
 
VR agencies may implement strategies to increase client engagement from application to IPE; 
however, if AZRSA does not have the infrastructure, policies, and procedures in place to support 
and provide comprehensive services to assist participants to achieve their career goals, 
individuals will not have an incentive to remain engaged in the VR process, and unsuccessful 
closures may increase.

Inaccurate Reporting of Supported Employment Services and Expenditures  
 
In FFYs 2019 and 2020, AZRSA relinquished half of its State Supported Employment program 
funds from Supported Employment-A (SE-A) and Supported Employment-B (SE-B) awards and 
did not report any supported employment expenditures for FFYs 2020 or 2021. Furthermore, 
AZRSA did not report expenditures for the provision of supported employment services for 
individuals with most significant disabilities or the provision of extended services to youth with 
the most significant disabilities on the RSA-911 for PYs 2020, 2021, and 2022.  
 
According to AZRSA, the Financial Services Administration (FSA) team “knows to move” the 
supported employment services expenditures to the supported employment funding codes (SE-A 
and SE-B). However, expenditures were not reported on the SF-425 prepared by the FSA team 
located within DES. Staff within DES, the umbrella agency over the DSA and DSU submits the 
SF-425. In addition, a centralized information technology (IT) staff is responsible for 
electronically pulling case files and reporting supported employment services for individuals 
with most significant disabilities and extended services for youth with most significant 
disabilities. However, the IT staff did not report any supported employment services and 
expenditures for supported employment services or extended services for PYs 2020, 2021, and 
2022 on the RSA-911.  
 
AZRSA reported that system limitations restricted the collection and reporting of supported 
employment expenditures as the data team does not pull supported employment expenditures 
unless the IPE is marked as a supported employment plan (i.e., “SE plan type”), the individual is 
in job status, and received a paid service. The data team separates participants under age 25 and 
age 25 or older and sends this information to the finance team to perform expenditure 
corrections. An additional factor impeding AZRSA’s ability to track, and report supported 
employment services includes its practice of first charging the VR grant award and not ensuring 
expenditure corrections were completed by charging or journaling supported employment 
expenditures to the supported employment grant award. AZRSA’s lack of internal controls to 
validate, track, and report supported employment and extended services and expenditures 
contributed to AZRSA’s failure to correctly report supported employment expenditures using 
Title VI funds for the last couple years and resulted in unspent and relinquished Federal funds 
available to serve individuals with the most significant disabilities.  
 
AZRSA informed RSA that it would not be accepting Title VI funds in its PY 2024-2027 State 
Plan. RSA can confirm that AZRSA did not receive an FFY 2025 SE-A or SE-B award. The VR 
agency shared that it plans to hire a contract liaison who will be responsible for working with 
vendors. AZRSA has hired statewide coordinators to assist with coordination, and providing 
technical assistance and training to ensure the accurate reporting of supported employment 
services and expenditures using Title I funds.

Insufficient Tracking and Reporting of MSG, Credentials and Post-Exit Measures  
AZRSA’s data show performance measures specific to employment rate in the second quarter 
after exit, median earnings in the second quarter after exit, and credential attainment rates 
improved during PY 2020 through 2022; however, the performance measures specific to 
measurable skill gains and employment rate in the fourth quarter after exit showed a decrease in 
PY 2022 from PY 2021. During the same time period, Arizona performed below the national 
average on all five performance measures.  
The total number of participants who earned MSGs, total MSGs earned, including postsecondary 
transcripts/report card training milestones and skills progression MSG, decreased from PY 2021 
to 2022. However, the percentage of participants eligible to earn MSG increased from 18.1 
percent in PY 2021 to 26.3 percent in PY 2022, and the total number of credentials earned (i.e., 
secondary and postsecondary), participants who earned credentials, and the percentage of 
participants eligible to earn credentials also increased from PY 2021 to PY 2022.  
While supervisors conduct field case reviews of documentation of wages, school records and 
diplomas, AZRSA communicated that it has not conducted targeted reviews to determine why it 
is not capturing and reporting MSG. At the time of the review, the VR agency did not have 
internal controls to ensure the tracking and reporting of a student’s graduation from high school, 
and tracking whether graduates are employed within a year or enrolled in postsecondary 
education. Internal controls are needed to document, track, and report all individuals eligible to 
earn a MSG. AZRSA was erroneously reporting enrollments and disenrollments and RSA 
demonstrated how the erroneous reporting was affecting performance results. Specifically, 
individuals never reported as enrolled in an educational program were receiving postsecondary 
education funded by AZRSA. Furthermore, AZRSA also reported individuals as disenrolled 
from an educational program, while reporting an MSG. RSA provided cohort data for the 
performance measures to AZRSA and provided a list of cases with missing data for performance 
measures to be addressed by AZRSA. The VR program shared that it could add internal controls 
to its current “401 cleanup” process. Because AZRSA did not submit any post-exit measure 
policies during the review, RSA concluded that AZRSA did not have post-exit measure policies. 
Additionally, AZRSA reported that it did not have internal controls to ensure the coding, 
verification, tracking, and reporting of MSG rate and post-exit measures. These deficiencies 
impact the accuracy of AZRSA’s reported performance.

Finding 2.2 Cost Allocation Requirements Not Met 
Issue: Whether AZRSA satisfied the requirements for cost allocation cited in the 
intergovernmental agreements (IGA) with school districts. 
Requirements: In accordance with Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(a), a cost is 
allocable to a Federal award or cost objective if the goods or services involved are chargeable to 
that cost objective in accordance with the relative benefit received. Additionally, in accordance 
with 2 C.F.R. § 200.430(i)(1)(vii), charges to Federal awards for salaries and wages must be 
based on records that accurately reflect the work performed and must, among other things, 
support the distribution of the employee’s salaries or wages among specific activities or cost 
objectives if the employee works on more than one Federal award. 
 
Federal award recipients must establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal 
award (2 C.F.R. § 200.303). The Uniform Guidance in 2 C.F.R. § 200.1 defines “internal control 
over compliance requirements for Federal awards” as processes designed and implemented by 
non-Federal entities to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in 
effectiveness and efficiency of operations; reliability of reporting for internal and external use; 
and compliance with applicable laws and regulations.  
 
States are also required to establish and maintain effective control over and accountability for all 
funds, property, and other assets. The non-Federal entity must adequately safeguard all assets 
and assure that they are used solely for authorized purposes (2 C.F.R. § 200.302 (b)(4)). 
 
Analysis: RSA reviewed the VR agency’s transition from school to work (TSW) contracts, 
including allocation of costs for personnel, to ensure the agency was assigning personnel costs, 
including fringe benefits, in accordance with the Uniform Guidance.  
 
Section 5.3.8 of the IGA with the County of Maricopa Deer Valley Unified School District #97 
states that ADES/AZRSA will, “Provide administrative oversight for the service provision by 
determining that all expenditures are an allowable cost from title 2 C.F.R. part 225 and are 
justifiable, reasonable, and necessary for the provision of the TSW program.” Section 9.1.22 of 
the same agreement states, “The Contractor’s Budget shall include only allowable costs 
associated with each enhanced service program provided by the Contractor per 2 C.F.R. part 
225 . . .” Part 225 of Title 2 was superseded by part 200 in 2014, The IGAs referenced in this 
finding were executed in June 2017 and later; thus, the pertinent requirements cited at 2 C.F.R. 
200 are applicable to these agreements. This same language was cited in IGAs with the Nogales 
Unified School District #1 and the County of Gila, Miami Unified School District #40. The 
agreements are created from templates, and it is assumed that the same or similar requirements 
are cited in all the agreements. 
 
As a result of RSA’s review, including on-site interviews with AZRSA staff, RSA determined 
AZRSA was funding school personnel not assigned to work on the VR program, including 
transition specialists and coordinator positions not available through school district resources, to 
ensure transition planning as required under IDEA. Additionally, AZRSA was funding guidance 
counselors and substitutes when school district resources were not available. Not all school 
personnel were allocating time between the separate cost objectives appropriately. For example,

19 
staff that work with multiple programs (i.e., cost objectives), like certified teachers, guidance 
counselors, and substitutes, charged their time to the VR program even though their functions 
required them to work on multiple programs that the school district administers.  
For staff working on multiple cost objectives, personnel costs must be allocated proportionally 
among all the different programs, including those administered by the VR agency (2 C.F.R.  
§§ 200.403(a), 200.404(b), and 200.405) or included in the indirect cost rate or cost allocation 
plan. At the time of the review, time and effort benefitting other programs was charged directly 
to the VR program in violation of 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403(a), 200.404(b), and 200.405 that requires 
allocation of costs based on relative benefits received.  
Funding school personnel, who are not assigned to work on the VR program, demonstrates a lack 
of control over and accountability for Federal dollars. It prevents AZRSA from ensuring Federal 
funds are used only for authorized purposes.  
Conclusion: AZRSA did not satisfy the general cost allocation and personnel cost allocation 
requirements in the IGAs and the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403(a), 200.404(b), 
200.405 and 200.430), as the VR agency improperly charged costs to the VR program that 
should have been allocated proportionally to all benefiting programs. Additionally, AZRSA did 
not have sufficient internal controls to verify the proper assignment of personnel staff time in 
accordance with the appropriate cost objective. The improper allocation of personnel costs 
resulted in unallowable costs being charged to the VR program. The unallowable personnel costs 
represent questioned VR costs.  
Corrective Actions: RSA requires that AZRSA—  
 
2.2.1 Immediately cease using VR funds to pay for direct and personnel costs that must be 
allocated to other cost objectives; and within 30 days of receipt of this report, submit 
documentation demonstrating compliance.    
2.2.2 Immediately establish and implement policies, procedures, and internal controls over 
contractor costs to ensure only allowable costs are paid from VR funds; and submit to 
RSA for review within 30 days of receipt of this report. 
2.2.3 Within 60 days of receipt of this report, review all TSW contracts in effect in FFY 2021 
and, 1) calculate the amount of unallowable costs paid from VR funds; 2) provide RSA 
with the methodology that will be used to make the determination; and 3) provide the 
cost data to RSA in a spreadsheet, which must include (but is not limited to) vendor 
name, contract number, total costs, unallowed costs, transaction dates, and expense type;   
2.2.4 Within 60 days of receipt of this report, submit verification of the proper assignment of 
personnel time in accordance with the appropriate cost objective;  
2.2.5 Within 90 days of receipt of this report, revise RSA-17 reports for all Federal awards not 
closed to remove unallowable and unallocable direct costs; and  
2.2.6 Within 90 days of receipt of this report, develop and implement a written internal control 
process, including a monitoring component, to ensure ongoing compliance with cost 
allocation requirements and accurate RSA-17 reporting. 
 
VR Agency Response: AZRSA acknowledges the finding and commits to reviewing budgets, 
contract methodologies, job duties to determine corrective actions associated with Finding 2.2 to 
ensure compliance.

Non-Federal Share  
Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.28, the DSU may enter into TPCAs with another State agency or 
local public agency for the provision of VR services only if the cooperating agency provides 
some or all of the non-Federal share (i.e., match) for those services through the mechanisms 
permitted by 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(c). RSA has identified concerns regarding the receipt and use of 
TSW matching funds, which will be addressed outside of this monitoring report. 
 
New or Expanded VR Services  
A fundamental requirement of a TPCA is that the cooperating agency must provide new services 
that have a VR focus or must modify, adapt, expand, or reconfigure existing services to have a 
VR focus. In other words, the cooperating agency cannot provide typical services it would 
ordinarily provide during the regular course of business as part of its TPCA responsibility. In 
reviewing the TSW arrangements, many included transition services that closely align with the

includes activities such as job applications, FAFSA, financial aid information, resumes, 
interview skills, and IPE amendments (e.g., Gila County, (DI-17-002100). The bundling of 
activities may lead to inaccurate reporting of required activities provided. Specifically, job 
exploration counseling should include interest inventory assessments, resume writing and 
interview skills, and job applications, while counseling on enrollment opportunities in 
comprehensive transition programs or educational programs at IHEs should include assistance 
with FAFSA applications and financial aid information.

Administrative Supervision  
VR program regulations in 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a)(3) require AZRSA to maintain administrative 
supervision over the program expenditures and cooperating agency staff providing VR services 
under the TPCA. In addition, as is true with all VR services regardless of the means by which 
they are provided (including by TPCAs), AZRSA remains solely responsible for all decisions 
affecting the nature, scope, and provision of VR services and the allocation and expenditure of 
VR funds (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(i) and (iv) and (2)). This means it is AZRSA’s responsibility 
to identify the services to be provided under the TPCA, the population to be served, the number 
of individuals to be served, and the duration of each of those services. As a Federal grantee, 2 
C.F.R. § 200.329(a) requires AZRSA to conduct monitoring and oversight activities of all of its 
grant supported activities, which would include TPCAs, to ensure compliance with Federal 
requirements. Through proper contract development, implementation, and monitoring, AZRSA, 
would exercise its administrative supervision over the program expenditures and cooperating 
agency staff providing services under the TPCA, as required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a)(3).  
 
The TPCA agreements state that the contractor is responsible for the administrative oversight of 
the TSW program (section 5.2.11 contract DI17-002117 Nogales Unified School District), 
including service provision, providing supervision and guidance for the TSW coordinators and 
TSW technicians, and the expenditure of funds for TSW services.  Section 5.2.11.2 details the 
contractor responsibilities to document TSW personnel activity reports and timesheets; however, 
there is no requirement that personnel activity reports and timesheets be submitted with invoices 
for AZRSA to verify TSW staff time spent on the provision of TSW services, including pre-
employment transition services and transition services. The contractor is also responsible for 
hiring qualified personnel to provide TSW services (section 5.2.8). Section 5.3.8 of these 
contracts limits AZRSA’s administrative oversight to determining that all expenditures are 
allowable, verifying students in the TSW program are qualified VR applicants or recipients, and 
providing technical assistance to TPCA partners and supervision and guidance of the VR 
counselors from a designated VR supervisor.

basis that they are TSW program referrals alone. RSA clarified that if AZRSA was implementing

3.1 Failure to Exercise Sole Control over the Expenditure of VR Program Funds (Non-
Delegable Functions) 
 
Issue: Whether AZRSA maintained sole responsibility for the expenditure of VR funds, as 
required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2).  
 
Requirements: The VR program regulations in 34 C.F.R. § 361.13 specify minimum activities 
that are the responsibilities of the DSU. Specifically, AZRSA, as the DSU, must be solely 
responsible for: all decisions affecting the eligibility for VR services; the nature, scope and 
provision of services; determinations to close service records; policy formulation and 
implementation; the allocation and expenditure of VR funds; and participation of the VR 
program as a partner in the one-stop service delivery system (see also Section 
101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(V) of the Rehabilitation Act). AZRSA may not delegate any of these functions 
or responsibilities to another entity or individual (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(2)).  
 
Analysis: According to the conditionally approved (May 31, 2024) VR services portion of the 
PY 2024-2027 Unified State Plan, DERS is the designated State agency and AZRSA is the 
designated State unit. The U.S. Department of Education’s Grants Management System (GMS) 
reflects the AZRSA director as the State recipient director and another DSU staff person as the 
State authorizing official. Although DES is not included in the VR services portion of the PY 
2024-2027 State Plan as the DSA or DSU, it is assigned the UEI and has access to the GMS. By 
submitting the State Plan, DERS and AZRSA assured RSA that they would satisfy all Federal 
requirements governing the VR program, which would include those related to AZRSA’s non-
delegable functions discussed herein. 
 
DES staff, including the senior managing analyst for the Financial Services Administrative 
Team, DERS staff, including a senior financial analyst for the Finance and Business Operations 
Administration, and the AZRSA director attended the fiscal monitoring sessions. The Managing 
Analyst for the Financial Services Administrative Team indicated that the overall responsibility 
for incurred costs, cost allocation, payroll administration, Federal reporting and Federal 
drawdowns were managed and overseen at the DES (department in which DERS is located) and 
DERS/DSA levels. DERS and AZRSA managers met regularly to discuss payroll; however, this 
was a recently implemented practice. It did not appear the AZRSA director maintained any 
control or autonomy over payroll costs funded from the VR award and/or matching funds. For

39 
example, payroll costs allocated to the VR program were shared with the AZRSA director on a 
regular basis, but there was nothing provided to the AZRSA director to verify that time and 
effort reported was accurate and allocable to the VR program. Thus, the AZRSA director is 
unable to ensure that payroll costs paid from VR funds are incurred only in support of the VR 
program. 
 
After the monitoring review, AZRSA communicated that the Financial Services Administration 
at the DES level is responsible for Federal reporting of the SF-425/RSA-17 data and Federal 
drawdowns. The Finance and Business and Operations team shares responsibilities with DERS 
and AZRSA staff to ensure proper cost allocation of incurred expenses such as payroll and 
AZRSA program costs. However, during the on-site monitoring review, the AZRSA director 
stated she had no familiarity with or working knowledge of the number, titles or functions of 
staff paid from VR funds.  
 
The fiscal management of the VR program, particularly with respect to the allocation and 
expenditure of VR program funds, is performed by a three-tiered hierarchy in Arizona, including 
DES at the top tier, DERS (the DSA) in the center, and AZRSA (the DSU) at the lowest tier. The 
VR program is implemented and executed at the lowest tier. DES staff advised that while there is 
communication between the top, middle and lowest tiers, the funding decisions, payroll 
administration, Federal reporting and Federal drawdowns are all managed and controlled at the 
department and/or division levels. 
Although the DSA (i.e., DERS) has centralized functions including HR, IT and a Finance and 
Business Operations Administration, including business operations, finance and budget, 
contracts and contract monitoring and facilities, DES (one level above the DSA), also includes a 
Human Resources Administration, Division of Technical Services, Division of Financial 
Operations, and Division of Business Services.  
During the onsite portion of the review, AZRSA was unable to document or otherwise 
substantiate that its degree of control over VR program funds exercised at the department (DES) 
and DSA (DERS) levels is consistent with the regulations governing non-delegable functions of 
the DSU. At the time of the review, DES was retaining half of the non-Federal share transferred 
through the TSW TPCAs to AZRSA. Specifically, DES only transferred 21.3 percent of the 42.6 
percent non-Federal share to AZRSA. The other 21.3 percent was deposited into the DES VR 
“TSW Admin Account” that is managed by the finance and business operations administration 
(FBOA) shared services. During the monitoring review, the AZRSA director was aware of the 
portion of the non-Federal funds generated from the TSW TPCAs that was removed from the VR 
program and deposited in DES’s “TSW Admin Account.” At the time of the review, the AZRSA 
director was aware of the full match portion being generated by the contracts but, could not 
access the half of the match generated by and for the VR program. This was not consistent with 
the DSU’s non delegable responsibility for the allocation and expenditure of VR funds. RSA will 
address other concerns pertaining to the non-Federal share generated from TPCAs outside of this 
monitoring report. 
Section 101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(b)(1)(i) requires that 
AZRSA, as the DSU, be responsible for the administration of the VR program. Although the 
statute does not describe the nature and scope of this responsibility or how the DSU is to carry it

40

DSU, meaning that AZRSA cannot delegate them to any other agency or individual. These “non-
delegable” functions relate to decisions affecting, among other things, the allocation and 
expenditure of funds and the provision of services, as discussed above. 
RSA has long interpreted
2 these provisions to require that the specified functions and activities 
be carried out by the DSU’s own staff (60 FR 64475, 64482 (December 15, 1995) and 62 FR 
6307, 6316 (February 11, 1997)). Further, RSA has long believed that the non-delegation

Retaining these non-delegable functions within AZRSA:

federally funded programs do not entrust these key VR programmatic decisions to 
individuals who lack experience in meeting the needs of individuals with 
disabilities…[T]h e benefits derived from DSU retention of these functions – enhanced 
program efficiency and effectiveness – outweigh any costs that may be associated with 
the non-delegation requirements in the final regulations (62 FR at 6316). 
 
Because the State has centralized certain financial management and procurement functions 
within DERS or higher with the DES, RSA’s monitoring activity assesses the manner in which

the procurement of VR services, centralization of these functions is impermissible if it results in 
interference with AZRSA’s decision-making capacity to administer the VR program, given that 
it has been designated as the DSU responsible for administering the VR program under the VR 
State plan (Section 101(a)(2)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(b)(1)(i)).  
 
As discussed above, the facts demonstrate that the centralization of the financial management 
responsibilities within DERS and DES impacts AZRSA’s ability to know the specifics about VR 
program fiscal transactions for payroll. Specifically, AZRSA is not solely responsible for the 
expenditure of VR program funds for personnel costs as it must be pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 
361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2). If AZRSA were exercising its non-delegable responsibility over the 
financial management of VR program funds properly with respect to payroll expenditures, it 
would have been aware of any payroll costs not allocable to the VR program, and taken 
immediate action to rectify them, but it could not.

by DES on behalf of the VR program. Therefore, the AZRSA director had no knowledge of the

Finding 3.2 Insufficient Documented Policies, Procedures, and Internal Controls 
Governing the VR Program  
 
Issue: Whether AZRSA maintains effective internal control over the Federal award to provide 
reasonable assurance that it is managing the VR program award in compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award. 
 
Requirements: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and

43 
efficient administration of the VR program. These methods of administration (i.e., the agency’s 
internal controls) must include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial 
accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). 
 
“Internal controls” means a process, implemented by a non-Federal entity, designed to provide 
reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in the following categories:   
 
● Effectiveness and efficiency of operations;   
● Reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and  
● Compliance with applicable laws and regulations (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). 
 
Additionally, 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, among other things, requires a non-Federal entity to—   
 
● Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award…;   
● Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards;   
● Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statute, regulations, and 
the terms and conditions of Federal awards; and 
● Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings. 
 
In accordance with the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a)), a State’s financial 
management systems, including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the—   
 
● Preparation of reports required by general and program-specific terms and conditions; 
and   
● Tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have 
been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of 
the Federal award.  
 
Furthermore, provisions at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(4) require that the financial management 
system of each non-Federal entity must ensure effective control over, and accountability for, all 
funds, property, and other assets. The non-Federal entity must adequately safeguard all assets 
and ensure they are used solely for authorized purposes. In its guidance titled The Role of 
Internal Control, Documenting Internal Control, and Determining Allowability & Use of Funds, 
the Department states that internal controls represent those processes by which an organization 
assures operational objectives are achieved efficiently, effectively, and with reliable, compliant 
reporting. Therefore, an internal control deficiency would exist when the design or operation of a 
control does not allow management or employees, in the normal course of performing their 
assigned functions, to prevent or correct processes that might lead to noncompliance with 
Federal and State requirements.

44 
Finally, the Education Department General Administrative Regulations (EDGAR) at 34 C.F.R. § 
76.707 cites requirements for determining when financial obligations have been incurred under 
the VR program for various kinds of property and services. VR agencies must recognize, and 
record obligations based on EDGAR requirements for the following activities: 
 
● Acquisition of real or personal property; 
● Personal services by an employee of the State; 
● Personal services by a contractor who is not an employee of the State; 
● Performance of work other than personal services; 
● Public utility services; 
● Travel; 
● Rental of real or personal property; and 
● A pre-agreement cost with prior approval. 
 
The Arizona Department of Administration, General Accounting Office, publishes the State of 
Arizona Accounting Manual (SAAM). SAAM is required for use by all State agencies, and 
Section 25, Agency-specific Policies Based upon SAAM, states, “… An agency is at times 
required and at other times permitted to produce agency-specific policies and procedures based 
upon SAAM. Such agency-specific policies are limited to finance, accounting or other topics 
addressed by SAAM. Agency-specific policies based upon SAAM may be more restrictive, but 
not more permissive, than the statewide policies promulgated in SAAM.” Section 05, General 
Internal Controls, states, “… To support its processes and operations, each agency must develop 
and maintain an appropriate system of internal controls.” Section 05 also states that, “Each 
agency must, to the extent practicable, establish a system of checks and balances to ensure that 
no one person has control over all parts of a financial transaction. This is frequently referred to 
as the separation or segregation of duties.” SAAM lists the types of transactions and activities 
requiring segregation, as well as approval levels required for financial transactions.  
 
Analysis: As noted in the preceding section, SAAM requires AZRSA to develop agency-specific

documents governing the VR program, AZRSA submitted other SAAM documents covering

disbursements, encumbrances, payroll, documentation, and records retention. However, none of

managed in compliance with the regulations cited in the “Requirements” section of this finding.  
 
AZRSA also submitted procedural documents governing Federal drawdowns and Federal

apply; they may not be resolved or closed by the Federal cognizant agency unless such

awards it makes to non-Federal entities (2 C.F.R. § 200.521(b)). 
 
The U.S. Department of Education’s (Department) Audit Accountability and Resolution 
Tracking System (AARTS) database stores detailed information of findings within each audit; 
recommended actions; responsible individuals; progress; and eventual resolution of findings. 
AARTS is designed to assist the Department’s management with audit reporting and follow-up

auditees to promptly resolve findings, as cited in the preceding paragraph. 
  
Analysis: The AARTS database indicates AZDES had eight open findings arising from State 
fiscal years 2017 through 2018. The findings involved period of performance, program income, 
earmarking (specifically, pre-employment transition services reserve and expenditure 
requirements), allowable costs/cost principles, maintenance of effort, and inadequate

non-compliance pre-employment transition services corrective action plan.  
 
Conclusion: Based on the information and documentation provided, RSA concludes that

C.F.R. §§ 200.508(c) and 200.511(a)).

Finding 3.4 Insufficient Contract Management and Oversight  
 
● Issue: Whether AZRSA established and maintained effective and efficient management 
and control over contracts sufficient to meet the requirements of 2 C.F.R. § 200.328(a) 
and AZRSA’s Contract Monitoring Process guidance document. 
 
Requirements: Federal regulations state that the non-Federal entity is responsible for oversight 
of the operations of the Federal award supported activities (2 C.F.R. § 200.328(a)). The non-
Federal entity must monitor its contracted activities under Federal awards to ensure compliance 
with applicable Federal requirements and that performance expectations are achieved. 
Monitoring by the non-Federal entity must cover each program, function, or activity.  
 
AZRSA cites the following objectives in its Contract Monitoring Process guidance document:  
 
● Conduct regular reviews of Contractor’s program activity and outcomes to ensure they 
are compliant with requirements of the Contract;  
● Evaluate the relationship between program accomplishments and the financial resources 
allocated to achieve those accomplishments and ensure that the level of spending is 
commensurate with the actual program activity/outcome;  
● Examine effectiveness of Contractor’s internal control processes and accuracy of 
Contractor’s fiscal expenditures during the monitoring period;  
● Ensure Contractor’s compliance with applicable State and federal regulations, policies  
and procedures, and the terms of the Contract;  
● Provide technical assistance to Contractors to assist them in understanding the specific 
Contract elements including the Scope of Work, Service Specs, making improvements, 
and addressing corrective actions as identified in the monitoring process; and  
● Ensure Contractor compliance with the terms of the Contract in order to identify non-
compliance which if not corrected, could result in failure to provide adequate services to 
RSA clients and/or RSA programs, possible harm to clients and/or diversion of resources.

48 
The guidance details the activities and procedures necessary to conduct the three monitoring 
categories identified by AZDES/AZRSA: administrative; programmatic and fiscal.  
 
Analysis: RSA requested copies of monitoring reports for several of the contracts under review. 
The monitoring reports provided were very limited in detail and did not address any of the 
monitoring objectives cited in the “Requirements” section of this finding. AZRSA indicated it 
conducted in-person monitoring activities with the limited staffing resources available.  
 
AZRSA provided equipment inventory reports for equipment purchased by vendors under the 
TSW agreements, including several vehicles. The reports listed item descriptions, serial 
numbers, title holders, acquisition dates, costs, sources of funding, locations, and conditions of 
equipment. The equipment report for the Tucson Unified School District (TUSD) under contract 
DI17-002119 shows the purchase of six Ford F-150 4X4 trucks over several years and all titled 
to TUSD. The agreement cites the rationale for the vehicle purchases is to “. . . transport staff, 
students and tools to area elementary schools and parks to perform Curb Appeal. The Curb 
Appeal program is an enhanced service that we want to expand to all six of our sites. It provides 
students with the opportunity to learn skills in landscaping, building maintenance, and 
playground safety.” The program description indicates each school must be checked at least 
three times per week, and each day two to three students will provide the services. Since the 
vehicles were used only to transport students and equipment, the time during which the vehicles 
were unused (idle time) should have been calculated, and the cost to the VR program adjusted 
proportionately.  
 
Section 13 of the agreement states. “. . . capital equipment purchases should be made during the 
initial year of this Agreement . . .” However, TUSD classifies vehicles as depreciable capital 
assets;
3 thus, the AZRSA should have required the TUSD to comply with their accounting 
policies and depreciated the costs over the life of the asset class instead of expensing the costs in 
the contract budget year.  
 
The agreement goes on to say in Section 13.2, “The Contractor shall include AZRSA as a co-
owner, and co-insured on any insurance policy which covers equipment purchased under this 
Agreement. AZRSA shall retain an equitable interest equal to the purchase price paid, or a fair 
estimate or appraisal of current market value, whichever is greater, in all equipment purchased.  
 
Upon termination of this Agreement, any equipment purchased under this Agreement shall be 
disposed of by the AZRSA and, if sold, AZRSA shall be compensated in the amount of its 
equitable interest.” While on site, RSA asked to see vehicle titles and was told by AZRSA staff

in the name of the TUSD and not jointly titled to TUSD and AZRSA as required under the terms 
of the agreement.
