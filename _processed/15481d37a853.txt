INTRODUCTION
The Randolph-Sheppard Act ("R-S Act"), 20 U.S.C. § 107 et seq., and the regulations promulgated thereunder (34 CFR part 395) provide a program through which blind persons can apply for and receive licenses to operate vending facilities on any federal property. Priority is given to blind persons licensed by a state licensing agency.
The California Department of Rehabilitation ("DOR") is the California state licensing agency ("SLA") designated pursuant to the R-S Act, and it operates the Business Enterprises Program for the Blind (''BEP") pursuant to division 10, part 2, chapter 6 of the Welfare and Institutions Code (§§ 19625-19641) and the regulations promulgated thereunder (California Code of Regulations title 9, division 4, chapter 6). Under the BEP, in addition to licensing blind vendors to operate on federal property, DOR licenses blind vendors to operate on state and local agency property.
The purposes of the R-S Act and the BEP are to provide blind persons with remunerative employment, to enlarge economic opportunities of blind persons, and to stimulate blind persons to greater efforts in striving to make themselves
self-supporting.
BACKGROUND AND PROCEDURAL HISTORY
This arbitration arises under the R-S Act. Complainants are blind vendors who are licensed by DOR to operate vending facilities in Safety Roadside Rest Areas ("SRRAs") in the state of California. Respondent, DOR, is the SLA that licenses blind vendors pursuant to the Randolph-Sheppard Act and California's Business Enterprises Program ("BEP"). This dispute arises out of a 2012 DOR determination to reinstate an Interagency Agreement ("IA") with the California Department of Transportation ("Caltrans") -	the state agency with jurisdiction over matters contained in the Streets and Highways Code, including SRRAs­ based on a provision of California Streets and Highways Code section 220.5 which, at that time, required all operators of vending machines at SRRAs to reimburse Caltrans for costs associated with operation of those vending machines.
To facilitate the reimbursement requirement, Caltrans and DOR executed an
IA in the 1990s. This agreement required BEP SRRA vendors to reimburse Caltrans for the cost of utilities (electricity) for operating the vending machines in
the amount of $200 per month in order for DOR to reimburse Caltrans for those costs. DOR transferred the reimbursement payments it collected to Caltrans on a quarterly basis. That IA expired in 2008, and BEP SRRA vendors did not reimburse Caltrans for utilities from 2008 to 2012. In September of 2012, DOR and Caltrans executed an interim IA effective September 2, 2012 through February 28, 2013. This agreement required the vendors to resume paying $200 per month to DOR in order for DOR to reimburse Caltrans.
In November of 2012, the California Vendors Policy Committee ("CVPC") passed a motion objecting to the resumption of the monthly reimbursement payments to Caltrans. Seven blind vendors (''the Murphy Group") requested a full evidentiary hearing, and that hearing was held in January of 2013. On February 12 2013, DOR adopted the ALJ's proposed decision, upholding DOR's actions.

STANDARD OF REVIEW
Under the R-S Act, this Panel is directed, in accordance with the provisions of subchapter II of chapter 5 of Title 5, to "give notice, conduct a hearing, and render its decision[.]" 20 U.S. Code section 107d-2(a). Subchapter II provides, at 5
U.S. Code section 556(d), that the proponent of an order has the burden of proof, and that an order may issue "in accordance with the reliable, probative, and
substantial evidence." Accordingly, complainants have the burden of proof to establish by substantial evidence that DOR violated the Act. "This is something more than a mere scintilla but something less than the weight of the evidence." Pennaco Energy v. U.S. Dept of Interior, 377 F.3d 2247, 1156 (10th Cir. 2004).

CONCLUSIONS OF LAW AND ANALYSIS
Claimants have not met their burden of proving that Respondents violated the R-S Act.
The ultimate decision of }vfwphy is not binding on this Panel, but Claimants urge the Panel to adopt both the reasoning and the remedy from Murphy. However, we do not find that to be an appropriate course of action.
To the extent the Panel is being asked to rule on the validity of DOR's long-standing interpretation, Claimants face two significant challenges.
1.	First, as the practice in question ceased more than 30 days
before Claimants requested a full evidentiary hearing, it is not clear that making a determination on the validity of that previous interpretation is within the scope of the Panel's authority.
11.		Additionally, Claimants have not met their burden of proving that Respondent's actions were incompatible with Respondent's statutory obligations. The Panel does not deny that one viable reading of the totality of the relevant statutory language might have prohibited Respondent from charging Claimants for
utilities fees; however, we are unpersuaded by the evidence in the record that this is the best reading of Respondent's statutory responsibilities and restrictions. In this case, Caltrans is the state agency having authority to implement and enforce section
220.5 of the Streets & Highway Code. The statute was uniformly interpreted by Caltrans from at least 1995 through 2015. When the statute was ultimately amended, there was no indication that the amendment was reflective of existing law; rather the Legislature changed the law, which is what it does when it is dissatisfied with existing law. There was no evidence introduced at the hearing suggesting that at any time before 2014 there were concerns with Caltrans' interpretation.
To the extent that the Panel is being asked to rule that a settlement agreement between two parties resulting from a complaint under the R-S Act must be extended to all similarly situated non-parties, we cannot so find. Significantly, neither the Act nor the Murphy panel's interpretation of the Act lists reimbursement as a positive statutory obligation in cases where overcharging has occurred. The Panel is sensitive to the argument that voluntarily offering repayment is the
right thing to do, but we are not persuaded that Respondent is statutorily required to do so.
The question before the Panel is not whether Respondent acted blamelessly or whether Respondent could have done more to offset the burden that the 2012-2015 utilities payments had placed on Claimants; the question before this Panel is whether Claimants have met their burden of proving that Respondent wrongfully failed to reimburse Claimants as it did to Murphy claimants. In answer to this narrow question, the Panel concludes that the answer must be "no."
The Panel does not find a legal basis for concluding that Claimants are entitled to reimbursement for utilities fees paid between 2012 and 2015.
The evidence in the record does not establish that a violation of
§220.5, had one occurred, would have triggered an affirmative statutory duty for the DOR to reimburse affected blind vendors.
Similarly, the Murphy decision and settlement neither (1) established a duty for the DOR to apply the negotiated remedy to non-parties nor
(2) created a right of recovery by non-parties. None of the Claimants were parties to that settlement. The reasons for this are detailed in the record: some were not similarly situated at the time because they did not operate vending facilities at SRRAs when the Murphy case
commenced, some did not believe themselves to be similarly situated, and some had personal reasons for not wishing to get involved at that time. While the Panel may have compassion for reasons that kept each Claimant from being involved in Murphy, none of the reasons provide a legal basis for non-parties to lay claim to a resolution or a remedy of a matter to which they were not parties.
Three types of uniformity are implicated in the Claimants' arguments: (I) uniformity of operating agreements, (2) uniformity of application of statutory obligations, and (3) uniformity of outcome.
The R-S Act requires Respondents to "cooperate with the Secretary in applying the requirements of the Act in a uniform manner" (34 CFR
§395.3(a)(11 )). Claimant's strongest uniformity argument is that the payment of utilities was not uniformly required of other state or federal blind vendors. However, the evidence in the record demonstrates that there are a few exceptions to the general trend of uniformity in operating agreements for vendors. This fact does not, in and of itself, confer legitimacy on the operating agreements in question. It does, however, undermine the basis for a conclusion that any difference in operating agreement requirements is per se a statutory violation.
Furthermore, the Panel does not find the Murphy settlement to be implicated in Respondent's duty of uniform application of the requirements of the Act. The Act does not include a requirement that a finding of fault in favor of any one complainant should be automatically applied to all similarly situated parties, regardless of their participation in a class action. Similarly, the Act does not contain a requirement that any remedy afforded to one individual or group -
as might be awarded at the end of the properly-executed statutory procedure for challenging an agency action -	be applied to any similarly situated individuals regardless of their adherence to the procedural requirements for challenging an action and obtaining a remedy. Uniform application of the requirements of the R-S Act does not require that the settlement remedy reached in Murphy be applied to non-parties because the remedy in Murphy is not a requirement of a R-S Act. That remedy reflects that panel's interpretation of the Act; the remedy is not, in and of itself, a requirement of the Act. We conclude that the uniform application of such a remedy to individuals who did not participate in the statutorily mandated process for receiving a remedy would be incompatible with the uniform application of the requirements of the Act.
Finally, while we are aware of the practical and emotional desirability of obtaining an equal outcome for all who were disadvantaged by Respondent's previous interpretation of 220.5. we do not find a legal basis for enforcing an equal outcome to the exclusion of equal application of the statutory requirements for challenging that previous interpretation.
DECISION
For the reasons stated above, it is the decision of this Panel that the Claimants did not prove by substantial evidence that they are entitled to reimbursement by the California DOR for payments they made to it for electrical utilities at the applicable vending facilities between 2012 and 2015 based on the relevant findings of fact and conclusions of law reached by the arbitration panel in
Murphy, et al., v. CDOR. Therefore, the appeal is denied.

DISSENT
For the reasons elaborated below, I dissent from the majority decision. The Majority states "The evidence in the record does not establish that a violation of
§220.5, had one occurred, would have triggered an affirmative statutory duty for the DOR to reimburse affected blind vendors." In actuality, Claimants asserted no violation of 17 Cal. Sts. & Hwy. Code§ 220.5 (Section 220.5) in this case or in the Murphy case. Murphy established that the CA Department of Transportation (Caltrans) misinterpreted that section of the Streets and Highways Code, which misinterpretation DOR accepted when it negotiated the IA with Caltrans.
Caltrans apparently interpreted §220.5 to include electrical costs as one of those which should be reimbursed and that the Vendors should be responsible for that reimbursement. Because Caltrans was not a party to this Arbitration, the Panel has no jurisdiction to review that interpretation. However, DOR is a party to this Arbitration and adoption of that interpretation, under the circumstances of this matter, is reviewable by this Panel." (RS 12-10, P.12) Because Claimants introduced no evidence asserting a violation of Section 220.5, the majority's failure to find nonexistent evidence is neither surprising nor should it be relevant to this decision.
The majority states "there was not a violation of existing law when the DOR negotiated an interagency agreement ("IA") with Caltrans requiring blind vendors
to pay for utility fees at Safety Roadside Rest Areas (SRRAs) under 17 Cal. Sts. & Hwy. Code§ 220.5(d)". Quite remarkably one notes virtually no discussion in the Majority's opinion of any laws or regulations specifically governing the Business Enterprises for the Blind Program (BEBP) operated by California Department of Rehabilitation (DOR). This Arbitrator believes these statutes and regulatory provisions lie at the very heart of this case, and the Complainants' case was presented accordingly.
When the California Legislature enacted what is commonly called a "Little Randolph Sheppard Act", enabling DOR to expand its Business Enterprises Program to state and local facilities through Welfare & Institutions Code (Welf & Inst Code 19625-19641, it declared ''It is the intent of the Legislature that "the Randolph-Sheppard Act (20 U.S.C. Sec. l 07 et. seq.), and the federal regulations for its administration set forth in Part 395 (commencing with Section 395.1) of Title 34 of the Code of Federal Regulations, shall serve as minimum standards for the operation of the Business Enterprises Program for the Blind." (Welf & Inst Code Sec. 19625). This very simply means DOR may expand upon the rights and opportunities made available for blind vendors through the promulgation of state rules, and it may expand the Business Enterprises for the Blind Program (BEPB); but it may not infringe upon the rights of blind vendors or delegate the responsibilities assigned to itself as the state licensing agency (SLA) as set forth in
the Randolph Sheppard Act (20 U.S.C. Sec. I 07-107f)Act and the implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. Sec. 395.
When engaging in policy decisions or contract negotiations involving other agencies, DOR must consider the statutes and regulations under which it operates as the SLA for the BEPB, and any impact they might have on the legal requirements governing the entity with which it intends to contract. This DOR failed to do when renegotiating the IA with Caltrans in September 2012. DOR's IA with Caltrans creates the mechanism through which Caltrans recognizes blind vendors' priority in bidding on vending locations at safety roadside rest areas (SRRAs). The majority acknowledges that "one viable reading of the totality of the relevant statutory language might have prohibited Respondent from charging Claimants for utilities fees". The key phrase here is "the totality of the relevant statutory language". That "totality" is sadly missing in the majority's treatment of this case, resulting in a finding that runs contrary to both the RS Act and the welf & Inst Code.
When Section 220.5 of the Streets and Highways Code became law in 1963, blind vendors were not permitted to operate vending facilities at SRRAs because it was thought that the environment being along busy interstate highways constituted too great a danger for the blind. Thus sighted vendors operating vending facilities at SRRAs paid for electricity, as well as all other maintenance, operations, design
review, or other activities related to the vending machines, and reimbursed Caltrans for those costs from the revenues derived from the operation of the machines, in accordance with Section 220.5 of the CA Streets and Highways Code Sec.
220.5(d)(l).
When Congress removed the restriction on blind vendors operating locations at SRRAs along the Nation's system of interstate highways in the 1980's, it did so by amending the language of Title 23 Highways, Chapter I Federal Highway Administration-Department of Transportation, Subchapter H Right of Way and Environment, Part 752 Landscape and Roadside Development, Section 752.5 Safety Rest Areas. (23 CFR § 752).5: "In permitting the placement of vending machines the State shall give priority to vending machines which are operated through the State licensing agency designated pursuant to section (a)(5) of the Randolph Sheppard act". (23 C.F.R. Sec. 752.S(c). Thereafter blind vendors participating in the BEPB could bid on vending locations at the SRRAs established by Caltrans, and at some time after adoption of 23 C.F.R. Sec. 752.5 DOR first negotiated its IA with Caltrans.
Section 220.5 as it existed prior to January 1, 2016, when DOR ceased requiring the six Complainants herein to pay for electric utilities at SRRAs, provides in relevant part: "(d) (1) The department shall determine the costs for any maintenance, operations, design review, or other activities related to the vending
machines and shall be reimbursed for those costs from the revenues derived from the operation of the machines." Caltrans and DOR interpreted that the IA between them to require blind vendors to pay for electricity. According to the Murphy decision, the panel heard testimony that electricity was considered a cost of "Operation " (Sec. 220.5(d). Why did the agreement not require reimbursement for water service, gas, sewage, replacement of light bulbs? We know only that DOR
interpreted "other operation to mean electricity. This random linkage of two seemingly unrelated words operation = electricity contributed in part to Murphy's determination that charging SRRA vendors for electricity was arbitrary and
capricious. (RS 12-10 P.13)
What about maintenance, other operations, or design review? Why were vendors not charged for these costs? Perhaps because 34 C.F.R. § 395. l0(a) requires that: "The State licensing agency shall maintain (or cause to be maintained) all vending facility equipment in good repair and in an attractive condition and shall replace or cause to be replaced worn-out and obsolete equipment as required to ensure the continued successful operation of the facility." Thus, the handling of machine maintenance and operation appears to be assigned to the SLA. We also know the reimbursement of ''Caltrans for the costs enumerated above are to be taken from revenues derived from the operation of the machines.
Section 220.5(d)(1). As it happens, 34 C.F.R. Sec. 395.8(c) includes rather specific
language regarding machine income, i.e. revenues derived from the operation of the machines. "Any vending machine income shall be used by the State licensing agency for the maintenance and replacement of equipment, the purchase of new equipment, management services, and assuring a fair minimum return to vendors. Any assessment charged to blind vendors by a State licensing agency shall be reduced pro rata in an amount equal to the total of such remaining vending machine income". (34 C.F.R. Sec. 395.8(c)).
In light of these initial applications of federal law upon DOR's IA with Caltrans, it is neither appropriate nor proper to evaluate the impact of California Streets and Highways Code Section 220.5 upon blind vendors operating food service facilities at SRRAs to the exclusion of all other legal authority.
Any consideration of the interplay between the Streets and Highways Code, DOR, Caltrans and blind participants in the California Business Enterprises Program, must begin with an understanding of the parameters of that Program as established by the laws and regulations through which it was created. Clearly the Murphy arbitration panel performed the analysis necessary to that understanding; the majority in the instant case failed to take that approach.
The minimum standards referred to in Welf & Inst Code 19625 also require uniform treatment for all program participants. In Public Law 93-516, Title II, § 201, Dec. 7, 1974, 88 Stat. 1622, Congress expressly found that "uniformity of
treatment of blind vendors by all Federal departments, agencies, and instrumentalities" was necessary "to insure the continued vitality and expansion of the Randolph-Sheppard program....". The Department clearly violated that requirement when it permitted Caltrans to require payment of electric utilities from blind vendors at SRRAs located on state property, knowing that no other program participants operating at federal or state locations in California or across the country paid for electricity.
Whether this Panel believes blind Business Enterprise Program participants should or should not pay for electric utility costs, the Randolph-Sheppard Program has not permitted such payments since its inception in 1936. The California Legislature applied that same provision to state property by establishing the federal Jaw and regulations as minimum standards for the program now overseen and administered by DOR.
Before the department had authority to administer the federal/state Business
Enterprise Program, it needed to make application to the U.S. Department of Education Office of Special Education and Rehabilitative Services, for designation as a State Licensing Agency ("SLA"). In so doing DOR was required to provide assurances that it would "(i) Cooperate with the Secretary in applying the requirements of the Act in a uniform manner;" (34 CFR §395.3(a){l l))."
The majority highlights several instances in which vendors, including Mr. Balani and Mr. Hatch, paid for electricity and other operating expenses at BEBP locations. They perhaps failed to notice that these vending operations were located without
exception on county, municipal or private property. The Randolph Sheppard Act (20 U.S.C. 107 et seq) applies to BEBP locations on federal property and the Welfare and Institutions Code (Welf & Inst Code 19625-19641) applies to BEBP locations on state property. As noted earlier, DOR may expand its program through outreach to county, city, municipal and private entities, and we commend the agency for doing this. However, the regulations in particular do not have the same force and effect when applied to locations negotiated by the SLA on other than state and federal property. Thus, it is quite permissible for vendors to pay for utilities in each of the situations enumerated by the Majority in its list of additional facts discovered by the Panel.
Returning to the relevant issue of vendors with locations at SRRAs. Blind Vendors with locations on federal property do not pay for electrical costs, and blind vendors with locations on state property do not pay for electrical costs, DOR violated the terms of its designation as an SLA by permitting the interagency agreement with Caltrans to require payment of electrical costs by the blind vendors operating at SRRAs which are situated on state property along federal interstate highways. By applying only Section 220.5 to the question of whether blind vendors
should pay for electricity at SRRA locations, this Majority eschews the very existence of the Business Enterprises Program by insisting that its requirements need not apply to a particular IA between DOR and Caltrans or to the blind vendors operating locations under that agreement.
The majority believes that for this Panel to expand the remedy of Murphy to include the Balani Claimants who did not follow the statutorily mandated procedure for challenging utilities payments when they were first initiated would render that procedural language meaningless. In answering this question, it is fair to ask: Why should this complaint be considered now? When DOR accepted the six vendors' initial claims for full evidentiary hearings, it granted standing to the six Claimants here. Each Business Enterprise Program has rules which include a timeline for grieving an action or failure to act on the part of an SLA.
When vendors fail to meet that timeline, the common practice is to deny their request for a full evidentiary hearing. The California Code of Regulations (CCR) language requires this for vendors in California by including the timeline for each phase of appeal. (9 CCR Sec. 7227(1) and 7227(2). If a vendor's grievance fails to meet the appropriate standard, DOR is permitted to deny the applicant a fair hearing, because that applicant failed to comply with the regulation. At the same time, that denial could constitute failure to provide a service, giving the applicant the right to appeal. In this case, the agency accepted each man's
grievance, consolidating them into a single hearing. In so doing, DOR accepted that each of these claimants possessed a right to bring his complaint to the fair hearing, thereby failing to follow the statutorily mandated procedure for challenging utility payments. In so doing, DOR gave constructive consent to the resolution of this case regardless of how far it must proceed in order for that resolution to occur.
The Department itself contributed to each of the complainants' failure to file a grievance as soon as they became aware of the electric utility payment requirement. Let us examine the failure of these men either to join the Murphy case beginning in 2012, or to grieve the electric charges sooner: Michael Hatch was not operating an SRRA location when the Murphy case was initiated; he was eventually awarded an SRRA and presented with the Operating Agreement (OA) containing the electric utility payment clause. Mr. Hatch was the only blind vendor whose DOR Business Enterprise Consultant (BEC) sent him the OA electronically, whereupon he could read, print, sign and return it to his BEC. Mr. Hatch did object after signing his OA and was told he could not secure his new SRRA location unless he accepted the agreement to pay for electric utilities. Mr. Hatch was following the Murphy case and declined to file a grievance himself because he felt his case was completely duplicative and it would be inappropriate to file the same
case that was already in the hearing/arbitration process. (Transcript day 1, Pp. 186--204).
Naresh Balani testified he feared retaliation on the part of DOR ifhe joined the Murphy case. He questioned the utility charge when his business consultant informed him of its commencement in September of 2012, and he asked what would happen if he did not sign. The business consultant told Mr. Balani his new location could be taken away. Mr. Balani signed, continued to worry and therefore declined to join the Murphy case. (Transcript Day 1, Pp. 148-185).
Tristen Kelly was just completing his training and coming into the program. He was aware that vendors on state land did not pay for utility costs. and after finding himself forced to sign an operating agreement requiring utility payments at the Boron SRRA, he did file a grievance in which he prevailed when the ALJ found the addition of a requirement to pay for electric utilities after Mr. Kelly had signed an OA for Boron was not permissible. (Transcript Day 1, Pp. 24-26). Mr. Kelly then moved to the Wesley SRRA where his BEC informed him he would lose his vending license if he did not sign the OA which required payment of electric utilities. Tristen Kelley testified he too experienced a fear of retaliation from DOR after moving to Wesley. Transcript Day l, Pp. 104-148).
Messieurs Shoemaker and Kilpatrick did not yet operate highway vending
locations when the Murphy case was initiated. When Andrew Kilpatrick was
awarded his SRRA location, he knew that vendors could not be asked to pay for utilities and other expenses on state property. He had been a work-at-home dad since losing his vision and his family was in great need of his earnings. The location he won was lucrative. Mr. Kilpatrick testified that it was understood by all SRRA vendors in the BEBP that failure to sign an OA requiring electric payments to Caltrans meant the loss of your location. After questioning his Business Consultant, he learned of the utility payment requirement and was told he would not have a second chance to get the location unless he signed immediately. He signed the agreement but did not join the Murphy group. As the breadwinner for his family, Mr. Kilpatrick feared retribution from DOR if he joined the complaint
and he was only beginning to work his way out of debt. (Transcript day 1, Pp.
278-302).
Lawrence (Willy) Shoemaker entered the program after the Murphy case was begun, and he too only learned of the electric utility payment from his BEC. Mr. Shoemaker objected to signing the form at the time, because he knew vendors on state and federal property in the BEBP did not pay utility charges. His BEC informed Willy that he would not be permitted to begin operating the facility until he signed and that failure to do so would jeopardize his participation in the program. When Mr. Shoemaker learned Caltrans was converting all SRRAs to electric meters whereby vendors would pay the electric company directly rather
than paying a flat $200 per location to DOR, he became extremely concerned because he knew this would exponentially increase the cost for blind vendors. Consequently, he joined with other vendors to initiate a change in Section 220.5 by the General Assembly. (Transcript Day 1, Pp. 32-100.
Fernando Lopez did not join the Murphy group because he remained unaware of their filing. Operating an SRRA location east of San Bernadino, Mr. Lopez is somewhat isolated, and his location was barely providing him enough money to live on as it draws in an average of $23,000 in annual sales. Mr. Lopez testified he has applied for several locations closer to where he lives, as his current location is 170 miles from his home; and does not provide sufficient earnings to justify or allow him to relocate. Mr. Lopez testified his BEC brought the first agreement requiring utility payments to him in 2012, actually refused his request to read it aloud, and stated Mr. Lopez would lose the location unless he signed. Mr.
Lopez declined to file a grievance gearing even more negative treatment if he did that. (Transcript Day 1, Pp. 250-266).
Having related the above testimony from the hearing on this matter, disability civil rights law compels us to address grievous violations of these claimants' human dignity and civil rights, though they were not addressed by Claimants' counsel. Each of these six claimants signed several OAs including the requirement that he pay for electrical costs in direct violation of the R-S Act. Each
man knew or strongly suspected he was being asked to sign something that he should not sign. Only Michael Hatch was able to read the contract containing the improper utility payment clause. His was the only BEC who emailed Michael's OA to him; whereupon Mr. Hatch printed it out, signed and returned it to DOR. Each man suspected something afoul of the program because their business agents brought them new contracts in standard print, requiring an immediate signature.
These claimants are blind persons. They are generally not physically able to read the standard print of a contract. The Department is an agency providing vocational rehabilitation services to Californians who are blind. Yet DOR failed to bring accessible contracts for the men to sign. Each man asked if he could keep his contract until he could have it read before signing, and each request was denied.
Blind people use many alternative methods for managing print materials. Those who are computer literate request materials in electronic or Braille format, while those with lesser computer skills generally employ human readers to parse such materials.
Being an agency serving the blind, we expected DOR would automatically take these alternative reading methods into account when working with agency clients or Business Enterprises program participants. Only one of DOR's business consultants provided the nonconforming contracts in electronic, Braille, or large
print format. Not one mailed or delivered a contract in advance with instructions to have it read by the date a signature would be required.
This treatment is discriminatory on its face in addition to being a dramatic representation of abuse of power. When the unreadable contracts were presented to the six Complainants, each man was threatened with the loss of his business if he failed to sign his OA immediately. Such treatment constitutes a violation of Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 as amended; the very Act DOR is charged with carrying out in California. Compelling someone to sign an agreement that will govern his work life for the foreseeable future without providing him with that agreement in an accessible format also violates the Americans with Disabilities Act (42 U.S.C. Sec. 12101 et seq). Failure to provide informational materials in an accessible format constitutes unlawful behavior even when not accompanied by the coercion used against Claimants in the form of threats to take away their business locations.
If this Panel is concerned about providing reimbursement or restitution to these claimants in light of Bird V. Oregon Commission for the Blind, DOR's violation of the statutes enumerated above provides a clear avenue for restoring the funds wrongly taken from Mr. Balani, Mr. Hatch, Mr. Kelly, Mr. Kilpatrick, Mr.
Lopez and Mr. Shoemaker.
The majority finds DOR has no obligation to make restitution in any way to the six blind vendors. I disagree for the following reasons:
First DOR failed to take the parameters of the Business Enterprises Program into account before entering into an agreement with Caltrans that overstepped those parameters;
The business agents failed to disclose the utility payment requirement until the day they brought the contract for signature;
The Department never provided any of the relevant contracts in an accessible format;
No Business agent agreed to read the form to the blind vendors when they knew the vendors could not read it themselves;
The utility payments themselves were not consistent, with some vendors paying $200 monthly and others paying up to $1800 during summer months;
The Department knew no other vendor in the state paid for utilities on state property;
The Department knew or should have known no other vendor paid for utilities on federal property;
·The Department wrongly took utility payments from these six complainants in violation of state and federal law.
This Arbitrator believes an affirmative duty exists for DOR to make whole each of the vendors who was erroneously charged utility fees. Even absent such duty, DOR did reimburse seven of the vendors so impacted in the Murphy case. We already know these thirteen blind gentlemen are the only participants in the California Business Enterprise Program operating facilities on state property who have ever been charged for utility fees in the entire history of this program.
Additionally, we know they are the only blind persons operating vending facilities along state and interstate highways in any of the fifty-one blind vending programs across the entire Nation who have ever paid utility costs in any location, at any time, under any circumstances throughout the entire existence of the highway vending portion of the Business enterprise program.
Since highway vending opportunities became available to blind business enterprise program participants in the mid-1980's, thousands of blind men and women have operated vending businesses along our Nation's system of interstate highways. Perhaps as many as fifteen.of these thousands, all working in California, were charged for utilities. Seven of these have now been appropriately reimbursed. Six individuals remain who have paid utility costs and are still living and whose whereabouts are known, who have not been made whole.
How do we justify denying them any kind of compensation, restitution or remedy? In what kind of society is such disparate treatment of similarly situated
persons allowed to stand? In Taylor v. Westley, 402 F.3d 924 (2005), the court distinguished between remedies at law such as reimbursement or repayment, and an equitable remedy like restitution in cases where the complainants had funds wrongly withheld or taken from them. That case involved California's Controller failing to return unclaimed investment funds to persons whom she said were lost, deceased or had disappeared, when she knew the people's actual living whereabouts. The Controller simply declined to follow the rules for returning unclaimed funds when it is possible to locate their owners.
In this case, DOR declined to apply the rules of the R-S Act and the Business Enterprises for the Blind Act, which caused six blind vendors alone to pay for utilities on state property. Restitution for these claimants also finds purchase in the concept set forth in Griffin v. Harris, 480 F. Supp. I 072 (E.D. Pa. 1979). Although it is well-settled that there must be an explicit waiver of sovereign immunity before a damage award can be recovered against the United States, the cases have made a distinction between money damage awards to persons having a right to receive money from the United States and the restoration of money improperly taken or improperly withheld.
The Majority allows these six Claimants to bear the loss of between eight-thousand and fifty-one-thousand dollars in the form of unauthorized,
unsupported utility costs by simply saying it should not have been wrong for DOR
to disparately pass these costs down to these six men; each of whom is working to support his family? Even if we believe it would have been proper to charge all blind operators in the California Business Enterprise program for utility costs, whether they operate their locations in federal, state or municipal locations, Congress did not agree. The California General Assembly did not agree. The legislatures of the remaining 49 states and the Council of the District of Columbia did not agree. Neither the R-S Act, nor California's Business Enterprises Program permits these charges.
Both of these acts were designed to combat the tremendously high unemployment rate among blind persons, and as a result they sought to develop a clear pathway to economic independence for blind vendors. The denial of uniform treatment for these six complainants who remain the only living blind vendors in California and perhaps the Nation with locations on state property who have paid for electricity is simply an action that finds no support in law, regulation or fact.
