I. ISSUE
Whether the Air Force violated the Act and implementing regulations by failing to apply properly the Act and its implementing regulations when evaluating Utah’s SLA bid in the procurement of the Solicitation.
II. STANDARD OF REVIEW
This Panel is directed to, in accordance with the provisions of subchapter II of Chapter of Title 5, give notice, conduct a hearing, and render its decision. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a). That subchapter provides, at 5 U.S.C. § 556(d), that the proponent of an order has the burden of proof and an order may issue “in accordance with the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence.” Id. Accordingly, the SLA has the burden of proof to prove by substantial evidence that Hill AFB violated the R-S Act. “This is something more than a mere scintilla but something less than the weight of the evidence.” Pennaco Energy v. U.S. Dep’t of Interior, 377 F.3d. 2247, 1156 (10th Cir. 2004).

IV. CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
1. The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA), the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) and the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation (DFAR) apply “except in cases of other procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute.” 10 U.S.C. § 2304(a)(1), 41 U.S.C. § 330f. The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst. V. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001); NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003); NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the FAR and DFAR regulations do not apply to R-S Act procurements, insofar as they conflict with the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
2. The SLA is not required to file a pre-award bid protest, because the law does not require the doing of a futile act. Whether filed in the Government Accountability Office, the United States Court of Claims, or other venue, such a protest would be dismissed as not ripe for adjudication.
3. The R-S Act’s priority must be implemented by placing an SLA’s bid in the competitive range where the bid may be made acceptable through discussions, thus implementing the R-S Act priority.
4. When a Federal property managing agency evaluating a cafeteria solicitation in which an SLA places a bid, the agency must evaluate the bid pursuant to the R-S Act; it is impermissible by law for an SLA bid to be evaluated pursuant to “best value” or lowest price technically acceptable offer.
5. The R-S Act priority is not a mere invitation to bid; the R-S Act’s priority supersedes preferences such as the Small Business 8(a) preference found in more general procurement statutes. Department of the Air Force, B-250465.6, June 4, 1993; Automated Comm. Sys, Inc. v. United States, 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 578 (2001).
V. ANALYSIS
A. The R-S Act Requires that Federal Agencies Establish a Competitive Range when Evaluating an SLA’s Proposal Submission
The R-S Act and its implementing regulations establish a priority for blind vendors to operate all cafeteria and cafeteria-related contracts on Federal property. 20 U.S.C. §§ 107(b), 107d-3(e). Because Hill AFB is located on Federal property, the Department of the Air Force is responsible to take all steps necessary to ensure, in light of appropriate space and potential patronage, that one or more vending facilities for operation by blind licensees shall be located on all Federal property. 34 C.F.R. § 395.30(a).
The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA), the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) and the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation (DFAR) apply “except in cases of other procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute.” 10 U.S.C. § 2304(a)(1), 41 U.S.C. § 330f. The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst. V. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001); NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003); NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the FAR and DFAR regulations do not apply to R-S Act procurements, insofar as they conflict with the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
The regulation at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) that a cafeteria contract be awarded to the SLA provided that the SLA can operate the cafeteria at comparable cost and comparable high quality as that currently provided to employees was violated when the SLA’s bid was not accepted. Utah has provided satisfactory service at Hill AFB since 2000. TR Vol. I, 187:17-19, TR Vol. I, 216:19-22, TR Vol. 1, 227: 3-7. Moreover, it is the Secretary of Education who makes the determination, not the contracting officer. See 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a).
The R-S Act’s priority supersedes preferences such as the Small Business 8(a) preference found in more general procurement statutes. Department of the Air Force, B- 250465.6, June 4, 1993; Automated Comm. Sys, Inc. v. United States, 49 Fed. Cl. 570,
578 (2001).
The R-S Act’s implementing regulations require that the appropriate SLA be invited to apply "when a cafeteria contract is contemplated" and establish that the priority shall apply to "all contracts or other existing arrangements pertaining to the operation of cafeterias on Federal property . . . ." 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) and (c). The Air Force violated its duty to assure that, whenever feasible, one or more vending facilities are established on all Federal property. 34 C.F.R. § 395.30.
The R-S Act regulations, and in particular the cafeteria regulations (including the requirements to maximize employment opportunities for blind vendors and their application to contracts pertaining to the operation of cafeterias), are entitled to the highest level of deference, known as Chevron deference, in reference to Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984).
The R-S Act’s implementing regulations require that contracting officers establish a competitive range when evaluating contracts that pertain to the operation of a cafeteria. Applying Congress's direction "to establish a priority for the operation of cafeterias on Federal property by blind vendors" to include contracts "pertaining to the operation of cafeterias on Federal property" in no way contradicts the R-S Act, nor does a contracting officer have the authority to eviscerate the R-S Act by simply foregoing the establishment of a competitive range. If all Federal agencies decided to forego the establishment of a competitive range in evaluating all procurements where the R-S Act requires that the SLA be invited to respond to a solicitation for a cafeteria contract, there would be no cafeteria contracts (or contracts pertaining to the operation of a cafeteria) operated by blind vendors. The R-S Act cannot be so interpreted, for to do would be to nullify the statute. Hill AFB interprets the R-S Act in such a way as to nullify the priority. Such is a “stark violation of the elementary principle” of statutory interpretation that requires “every clause and word of a statute” be given effect and is simply not permissible. King v. Burwell, 135 S.Ct. 1480, 2498 (2015).
Hill AFB cannot circumvent the R-S Act by determining that “best value” governed the award of the contract. The R-S Act standard governing the award of this contract is at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b), it is whether the blind vendor can “operate a cafeteria in such a manner as to provide food service at comparable cost and comparable high quality as that available from other providers of cafeteria services.”
The contract at issue is a contract that is required to remain R-S Act, as it was operated by a blind licensee pursuant to the R-S Act since 2000. By determining that it did not have to establish a competitive range and that it could use the “best value” standard, rather than the standard set forth in the R-S Act, the Air Force placed a limitation on the operation of the Hill AFB contract. This is expressly prohibited unless the Air Force first seeks, and obtains, the approval of the Secretary of the Department of Education to place such a limitation on the operation of the Hill AFB contract. 20 U.S.C. § 107(b)(2).
B. Hill AFB’s Waiver Argument Fails Because a Pre-Bid Protest Would Be Dismissed by the GAO and the Court of Claims; the Law Does Not Require the Doing of a Futile Act
Hill AFB claims that, by its submission of its offer, the SLA acceded to the solicitation’s terms that it need not establish a competitive range or conduct discussions with offerors. That is not the case. Had the SLA filed a pre-award bid protest, it would be dismissed. Because “[t]he law does not require the doing of a futile act,” Ohio v. Roberts, 448 U.S. 56, 74, 100 S.Ct. 2531, 65 L.Ed.2d 597 (1980), abrogated on other grounds by Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S.Ct. 1354, 158 L.Ed.2d 177 (2004), this Panel will not penalize the SLA for failure to file a pre-award bid protest.
In 2017, the Texas SLA brought a pre-award bid protest against the Air Force. State v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017). There, the Air Force moved to dismiss the pre-award bid protest. Its motion was granted on the basis that:
“Protestor’s allegations arising from the Randolph–Sheppard Act are not ripe for judicial decision because there has not been a determination as to whether the State of Texas is entitled to a priority contemplated in accordance with the Randolph–Sheppard Act and the implementing regulations.”
State v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8, 26 (2017). Even if Utah had filed a pre-bid protest, it would have been dismissed, whether it was filed in the Court of Federal Claims or whether it was filed in the Government Accountability Office (GAO). As a matter of routine, the GAO dismisses bid protests brought by SLAs. B-400912.2, GAO B-400583; B400583.2. Since 2004, the Court of Claims has repeatedly stated that it lacks jurisdiction to determine cases alleging violations of the R-S Act. Kentucky v. United States, 62 Fed. Cl. 445, 460 (2004), aff’d sum nom. Kentucky, Educ. Cabinet Dep’t for the Blind v. United States, 424 F.3d 1222 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (“this court concludes that the statutory scheme of the RSA requires exhaustion of administrative procedures before an aggrieved SLA may raise an RSA claim in this court.”); Colo. Dep’t of Human Serv. v. U.S., 74 Fed.Cl. 339 (2006) (“The Court of Claims lacks jurisdiction under the Tucker Act and the APA and will not entertain a motion for preliminary injunction pending the outcome of the R-S Act arbitration.”) See, also, State of Kansas v. U.S., 192 F.Supp.3d 1184, 1194-95 (D. Kan 2016), aff’d in part sub nom. Kansas by & through Kansas Dep’t for Children & Families v. SourceAmerica, 874 F. 3d 1226 (10th Cir. 2017).
Hill AFB cites to N. Carolina Div. of Servs. for Blind v. United States, 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 165 (2002), aff’d sum nom. N. Carolina Div. of Servs. for the Blind v. United States, 60 F. App’x 826 (Fed. Cir. 2003) for the proposition that the SLA, by not seeking a pre- award review of the solicitation terms, has waived that right. Yet, the Air Force itself argued against this view in State v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017), and obtained a dismissal of a pre-award bid protest. Accordingly, the Panel concludes that the North Carolina case cited above no longer represents the view of the Court of Claims with respect to cases founded on the R-S Act.
Hill AFB also cites to Moore’s Cafeteria Services v. U.S., 77 Fed. Cl. 180, 184 (Fed. Cl. 2007). Because Plaintiff in Moore’s was a disappointed bidder, but not an SLA, the R-S Act’s arbitration procedures do not apply to Plaintiff Moore. Accordingly, the Panel concludes that Moore’s is inapplicable to the facts in this case.
Given that both GAO and the Court of Claims would have declined jurisdiction had Utah filed a pre-award challenge, Hill AFB has not established that waiver principles apply. Thus, Utah was not required under current jurisprudence to file a pre-award bid protest.
C. The Handbook’s Guidance Illuminates Current Law and is thus entitled to Skidmore Deference
The Air Force argues that the DoE handbook (Utah Exhibit 17) is inapplicable, and that expert witness Dr. Frederick Schroeder’s testimony “should hold zero weight.” The Air Force is incorrect. In response to an inquiry from counsel for Air Force in this case, DoE advised, on July 15, 2021, that the handbook is:
retired, and therefore, is no longer in effect. However, we are reviewing the document to determine whether any parts of the document are contained within current RSA guidance. We will respond to you with more detail once we complete this review.
Since at least 1944, when the Supreme Court in Skidmore v. Swift Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), decided this issue, agency interpretations reached without benefit of notice and comment procedures may be considered as persuasive evidence:
We consider that the rulings, interpretations and opinions of the Administrator under this Act, while not controlling upon the courts by reason of their authority, do constitute a body of experience and informed judgment to which courts and litigants may properly resort for guidance. The weight of such a judgment in a particular case will depend upon the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade, if lacking power to control.
Skidmore v. Swift Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The handbook in question was issued in 1988, and remained in effect until January 18, 2017, when it was withdrawn, along with numerous other policy issuances that “are outdated due to changes in law, regulations, or reporting requirements resulting from the amendment to the rehabilitation Act of 1973 made by the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act.” The Panel concludes that, because the Handbook has been in effect since 1988, and because the R-S Act has not been amended in the interim, the Handbook is persuasive in determining when and how an SLA’s bid is to be considered in the competitive range.
With Utah’s technically acceptable rating and its satisfactory past performance, its bid should have been placed in the competitive range to initiate discussions to determine the “possibility of being made acceptable.” Because Utah’s bid was technically acceptable and it delivered cafeteria services at Hill AFB for many years, it is more likely than not its proposal would be ranked among those proposals with a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, but for the Air Force’s determination that it would evaluate bids solely based on “best value” unfairly and not entering into meaningful discussions about its pricing.
It is the Panel’s opinion that Utah’s bid could have been lowered had the Air Force properly conducted meaningful discussions with Utah as to its price. Utah was correct to believe that its bid should contain the costs of supervisory personnel, based on many years of such supervisory personnel being required by the Air Force. Under these circumstances, the Air Force’s decision to refuse to place Utah’s bid in the competitive range and to conduct meaningful discussions violated the R-S Act.
D. Legislative History Illuminates the Correct Application of the Law
According to Hill AFB, the Panel should not rely on the statute’s legislative history. Yet, a brief review of significant Randolph-Sheppard decisions reveals that courts consider legislative history, even where ambiguity is not an issue: New Hampshire v. Ramsey, 366 F.2d 1, 27 (1st Cir. 2004) (“But the legislative history of the R-S Act is clear that Congress intended "priority" to signify more than a mere preference. Congress found that "to [e]nsure the continued vitality and expansion of the Randolph-Sheppard program," it had to "establish a priority for vending facilities.”); NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263, 1268-69 (10th Cir. 2003) (While concluding that there is no ambiguity on the face of the statute, the Tenth Circuit nonetheless noted that “[t]he legislative history suggests that Congress was aware of the potential application of the cafeteria provision to military mess halls”); Minn., Dep’t of Jobs & Training v. Riley, 18 F.3d 606, 609 (8th Cir. 1994 (“Although we need not resort to other tools of statutory construction because the statute is clear,    the Act's legislative history and the Act's related provisions support our conclusion. When Congress amended the Act in 1974, Congress was concerned with federal agency abuses of blind vendor's operations, like forcing blind vendors to pay commissions. See S.Rep. No. 93-937, 93d Cong., 2d Sess. (1974).”)
Thus, the Panel rejects Hill AFB’s arguments regarding the R-S Act’s legislative history.
E. By its terms, Management Costs are Necessary to Properly Administer the Collective Bargaining Agreement
Panel Chair Valverde asked the parties to provide the Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) in effect at the time covering the affected employees, Utah agreed to provide the CBA, and Hill AFB made no objection. The CBA was subsequently provided by Utah, and is designated as Utah Exhibit 31. TR Vol. II, 60:5-13.
A review of the CBA makes clear that the union represents non-supervisory personnel, and that management is required, inter alia, to (1) deduct union dues from non-supervisory personnel and remit same to the Union; (2) notify the Union of all job openings within the bargaining unit;
(3) cooperate with the Union regarding enforcement of the CBA (this requires contact between the higher ranking contractor employee then present at the facility; (4) ensure that the work schedule is posted four days prior to the beginning of the work week; (5) issue reprimands and discharges to the employee and shop steward; and (6) work with the Union to resolve employee grievances. These are management tasks that cannot be performed by union members. Nor can they be performed by Hill AFB personnel. Hence, this is a management task that is required for compliance with the terms of the solicitation at issue in this case.
Accordingly, the Panel Majority concludes that management (i.e., supervisory) personnel was required to supervise and administer the CBA and was legitimately identified as a cost item by Utah. Hill AFB should have recognized the need to administer the CBA and recognized and accepted the need for inclusion of management costs.
F. The R-S Act provides SLA a Priority that Hill AFB Failed to Recognize in this Case
Hill AFB pejoratively claims that the “SLA seems to operate under that premises that it is guaranteed award or owed a premium.” Hill AFB has misconstrued the R-S Act. The R-S Act provides a priority for licensed blind vendors to operate vending facilities on Federal property. This priority – in the case of a contract pertaining to the operation of a cafeteria – requires that a Federal agency evaluate the bid of the SLA and place the bid within a competitive range pursuant to the definition of competitive range that was in effect at the time that 34 C.F.R. § 395.33 was promulgated, i.e., “[w]hen there is doubt as to whether a proposal is in the competitive range, the proposal should be included ... the competitive range includes ‘all proposals that have a reasonable chance of being selected for award.’” The fact that the current FAR and/or DFAR have a different interpretation of competitive range cannot change the fact of the R-S Act priority, which must be implemented by placing the SLA’s bid within the competitive range unless there is no reasonable chance that the SLA’s bid could be selected for award.
Because Utah was technically acceptable and had the Hill AFB contract for many years, it is more likely than not that its proposal would rank among those proposals with a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, but for the Air Force’s decision to remove management services that it considered to be duplicative and not entering into meaningful decisions about its pricing. The R-S Act priority cannot be implemented without the property managing agency giving serious consideration to the SLA’s bid. It is clear that Hill AFB failed to do so in this case, especially given that it only “allowed” Utah to bid after Utah sought arbitration for its exclusion as a non 8(a) entity.
Hill AFB also violated the R-S Act when it failed to bring the day-to-day problem of duplicative services to the attention of the Utah SLA. 34 C.F.R. § 395.36 provides:
(a) The State licensing agency shall attempt to resolve day-to-day problems pertaining to the operation of the vending facility in an informal manner with the participation of the blind vendor and the on-site official responsible for the property of the property managing department, agency, or instrumentality as necessary.(b) Unresolved disagreements concerning the terms of the permit, the Act, or the regulations in this part and any other unresolved matters shall be reported in writing to the State licensing agency supervisory personnel by the Regional or other appropriate official of the Federal property managing department, agency, or instrumentality in an attempt to resolve the issue. 34 C.F.R. § 395.36.
It is the Panel Majority’s opinion that Utah’s bid could have been fairly evaluated had the Air Force properly placed management cost in the solicitation and properly conducted meaningful discussions with Utah as to its price. Instead, the Air Force decided to rely on claims of duplicative services (that were asserted but not fully developed in this record) that were never conveyed to Utah so that they could be resolved, and on the price that Utah believed was necessary to provide the same level of management as was historically required by Hill AFB to disqualify Utah. Under these circumstances, Hill AFB’s decision to refuse to place Utah’s bid in the competitive range and to conduct meaningful discussions violated the R-S Act.
With respect to Hill AFB’s claim that the SLA expected to be paid a premium, Hill AFB ignores long-standing precedent that the R-S Act is superior to other socio-economic priorities. The R-S Act “takes priority over small business preferences.” Dep’t of the Air Force-Recon. B-250465.6 (June 4, 1993); Automated Communications Sys., Inc. v. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570 (2001) (the small business set aside need not be eliminated to give effect to the R-S Act). According to two witnesses with extensive background and experience with respect to the R-S Act, this means that “it’s not uncommon that the final word is 20 percent, 30 percent, even higher than the lowest technically acceptable bid.” (TR Vol 1, 163:1-7) “90% of the SLA contracts that are awarded are somewhere between 25 and 30 percent higher than the lower bid, the lowest bid.” (TR Vol. 1, 245:6-9).
The reason for this is that the standard for award of a cafeteria contract pursuant to the R-S Act is not lowest price, the standard is “reasonable cost with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided[.]” 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). When Hill AFB implemented the Evaluation Factors for Award, that “the lowest price offeror that is also Technically Acceptable will be the successful offeror,” it violated the R-S Act’s requirement that the Secretary of Education is to determine, “on an individual basis and after consultation with the head of the appropriate installation, that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost of a high quality comparable to that currently provided to employees, whether by contract or otherwise.” 20 U.S.C.. § 107d-3(e).
VI. CONCLUSION
Here, Utah was eliminated from the competitive range solely because of price, and the Air Force did not consult with the Secretary of Education at any time about the issue of price reasonableness. The Panel Majority finds that Hill AFB violated 34 C. F. R. § 395.33(a) when it failed to consult with the Secretary of Education. Further, it finds that even if the state licensing agency's bid is not within the competitive range set by the contracting agency, the matter still must be returned to the Secretary to decide, after consulting with the contracting agency, if the blind vendor can provide an operation at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality. The statutory and regulatory scheme make it clear that an SLA's bid cannot be rejected without consulting with the Secretary of Education who makes the final decision. 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a).
1. As a matter of law, the Panel Majority concludes that the R-S Act applies to the Solicitation, and that Hill AFB violated the R-S Act as set forth above.
2. Having found that Hill AFB violated the R-S Act, the next issue is the remedy to be afforded to Utah. Hill AFB contends that the Panel’s role is solely limited to determining whether the R-S Act was violated. With this constraint in mind, the Panel Majority finds:
AWARD
a. The Hill AFB Solicitation pertains to the operation of a cafeteria and is therefore subject to the requirement of the R-S Act and its implementing regulations.
b. Hill AFB violated the R-S Act and its implementing regulations when it failed to properly apply the R-S Act priority for blind vendors to the Solicitation and Contract at issue in this arbitration.
c. Hill AFB violated the R-S Act and its implementing regulations in failing to maximize opportunities for blind vendors.
d. Hill AFB shall cause the acts or practices found by this Panel to be in violation of the R-S Act and its implementing regulations to be terminated promptly; and is urged to take such other action as may be necessary to carry out the decision of the Panel Majority.
e. To that end, the Panel Majority finds as a matter of law that Hill AFB is obligated under the R-S Act and its implementing regulations to resolicit the Solicitation at issue and grant Utah and its blind vendor the priority afforded by the R-S Act and urges Hill AFB to do so.

DISSENTING OPINION
On February 9, 2022, Panel Chairperson Edward B. Valverde issued his written Decision and Award for RS 20-04, the hearing for which occurred on October 6 and 7, 2021 at Hill Air Force Base. Ms. Susan Gashel, panel member selected by the Petitioner State Licensing Agency, Utah Division of Services for the Blind and Visually Impaired, concurred with Panel Chair’s decision. I respectfully dissent and submit this Dissenting Opinion as the panel member selected by the Respondent, United States Department of the Air Force, Hill Air Force Base.
I. SUMMARY
In their complaint to the Department of Education seeking arbitration pursuant to 20 U.S.C. §§ 107d-1(a) and 107d-2 of the Randolph Sheppard Act (“RSA” or “Act”), the Utah State Licensing Agency (“SLA”) alleged that the Department of the Air Force (“Air Force”) violated Sections 107(b)(2) and 107d-3(3) of the Act and 34 C.F.R. § 395.33 of its implementing regulations in the evaluation of the SLA’s proposal submitted in response to Solicitation Number FA20120R0002. The Solicitation sought proposals for dining facility support services at Hill Air Force Base. As noted in the Department of Education’s May 7, 2020 convening letter, the Utah SLA asserts that the Air Force failed to determine if it were feasible for the Utah SLA and its blind vendor to operate the contract, establish a competitive range, and place the Utah SLA in the competitive range. Respondent Exhibit II (May 7, 2020 DOE Convening Letter).
As discussed in detail below, the Air Force did not violate the RSA or its implementing regulations by not establishing a competitive range as neither the RSA nor the Federal Acquisition Regulation (“FAR”) requires it. As noted in the Panel Decision, the SLA bears the burden of proof to show that the Air Force violated the RSA or its implementing regulations. Panel Decision, page 2; see 20 U.S.C. § 107d2(a), 5U.S.C. § 556(d). However, the SLA failed to identify any provision in the RSA or its implementing regulations during the entirety of this arbitration that expressly requires the Air Force to establish a competitive range for this acquisition. Additionally, the Solicitation clearly notified all offerors that the Air Force was going to award the contract without conducting discussions and, therefore, was not going to set a competitive range or conduct discussions. As such, offerors were on notice that their proposal must be their best and final offer. The SLA did not object to the issuance of the Solicitation prior
to evaluation of their proposal and under federal procurement law, the SLA waived its right to object to the Solicitation.
In their third amended complaint to the Department of Education (“DOE”) seeking arbitration, the SLA cites to 20 U.S.C. §§ 107(b)(2) and 107d-3(e) to support its argument that the Air Force failed to comply with the “feasibility standard”. The Panel Decision failed to fully examine these two cited statutory provisions upon which the SLA relies. Upon closer examination, these two RSA provisions do not create a “feasibility standard” when it comes to contract award.
Specifically, section 107(b)(2) discusses the feasibility of vending facility establishment on Federal property and the DOE’s obligation to promulgate regulations prescribing such guidance; it does not mention anything about a “feasibility standard” for contract award. In fact, section 107(b)(2) of the Act is implemented at 34 C.F.R. § 395.30. As noted in footnote 3, the “feasibility standard” relates to whether there is ample space and potential patronage on the Federal property to locate a vending facility/vending stand. See 34 C.F.R. §395.30(a). As such, section 107(b)(2) does not impose an obligation on the Air Force to award a contract to the SLA “wherever feasible.”  Further, section 107d-3(e) requires the DOE to “prescribe regulations to establish a priority for the operation of cafeterias on Federal property” when “such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality.” This section strictly applies to DOE’s obligation to promulgate regulations, which were enacted in 1977 when Congress enacted Title 34, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 395. In fact, the statutory language in section 107d-3(e) is implemented in 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a), almost verbatim. Section 395.33(a), Operation of Cafeterias, of Title 34, Code of Federal Regulations, discusses the operation of cafeterias and outlines certain procedural requirements regarding the application of the priority. It does not mention the word “feasible” or “feasibility.” The SLA’s argument that the Air Force violated the “feasibility standard” is a frivolous argument and should have been dismissed by the Department of Education prior to issuance of their convening letter. The majority opinion erred by accepting the SLA’s assertion because the two cited statutory provisions do not create a “feasibility standard” for contract award.
Accordingly, the SLA has failed to meet its burden in this regard.
The ultimate issue in this case is not factual, but rather a legal issue. The majority Decision and Award concludes that the Air Force violated the RSA and its implementing regulations when it failed to “properly apply the R-S Act priority for blind vendors to the Solicitation and Contract at issue in this arbitration.” Panel Decision and Award, page 21. This legal conclusion is fundamentally flawed as the RSA and its implementing regulations do not contain any language requiring a federal agency to establish a competitive range in such a procurement. Absent an express provision in the RSA and its implementing regulations requiring a federal agency to establish a competitive range in such procurements, the Air Force Contracting Officer did not violate the RSA and its implementing regulations. Further, the Air Force evaluation of the Petitioner’s proposal was conducted properly and did not violate the RSA and its implementing regulations. For the reasons stated below, the Panel Decision and Award is erroneous.

III. CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
1. The SLA is subject to all federal statutes, federal regulations, and relevant federal case law. As such, the SLA was required to file a pre-award bid protest challenging an ambiguous solicitation provision or the SLA will be subject to a claim of waiver. The appropriate time for a potential offeror to raise concerns regarding potential ambiguities or patent defects in an RSA solicitation is before the submission deadline. In fact, when the potential offeror recognizes the ambiguity or patent defect, to avoid costly and time-consuming litigation, potential offerors have the duty to “inquire and must seek clarification of the proper contract interpretation.” Cmty. Heating and Plumbing Co. v. Kelso, 987 F2d 1575, 1579 (Fed. Cir. 1993) citing Interstate Gen. Gov’t Contractors, Inc., v. Stone, 980 F.2d 1433 (Fed Cir. 1992); J.A. Jones Constr. Co. v.
United States, 395 F.2d 783, 790 (Ct. Cl. 1968). Every potential offeror, including the SLA, has the obligation to protest with the federal agency (Air Force) directly, the Government Accountability Office (GAO), or the Court of Federal Claims (COFC), relating to an alleged defect that is apparent prior to the time for submission of proposals. 4 C.F.R. § 21.2(a)(1); People, Technology and Processes, LLC, B- 413104.19, September 6, 2018; Blue and Gold, Fleet, L.P. v. U.S., 492 F.3d 1308, 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2007). Failure to file a timely challenge to a defective or ambiguous solicitation prior to the submission deadline results in a waiver of that claim.
2. The R-S Act’s priority is enacted only if the conditions specified in 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.33(a) and (b) are met. A proposal submitted by an SLA must satisfy the evaluation criteria specified in a Solicitation to be eligible for inclusion in the FAR 15.306 competitive range. The “RSA Priority,” contrary to the Panel Majority Conclusion of Law, does not automatically entitle the SLA proposal to be placed in the competitive range or to automatic award of the contract. See FAR 15.306; The State of Texas v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017).
3. When a Federal property managing agency evaluating a cafeteria solicitation in which an SLA submits a proposal, the federal agency is required to evaluate the proposal in accordance with the evaluation criteria in the Solicitation. The Panel Decision conflates the term “RSA priority” with “automatic award.” Contrary to the assertion by the Panel Decision, the RSA priority is only triggered when the conditions specified in 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.33(a) and (b) are met. See 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.33; State of Texas v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017). There is no legal impediment to evaluating an SLA proposal according to the specified criteria in the applicable Solicitation, including a Solicitation that is a “best value” or a “lowest price technically acceptable offer.”
4. Title 34, Code of Federal Regulation, Section 395.33(b) requires a federal agency to invite an SLA to respond to solicitations for offers when a cafeteria contract is contemplated by the federal agency. The solicitation for offers shall establish criteria under which all responses will be judged. Such criteria may include sanitation practices, personnel, staffing, menu pricing and portion sizes, menu variety, budget, and accounting practices. See 34 C.F.R. 395.33(b). The federal agency evaluates the SLA’s proposal based upon the evaluation criteria listed in the Solicitation; the RSA priority does not entitle the SLA to automatic award of the contract or automatic inclusion in the competitive range.
5. The RSA priority is enacted by two separate triggering events specified in 34 C.F.R. 395.33. The first triggering event is specified at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). As specified by section 395.33(b), if the SLA’s proposal is judged to be within the FAR 15.306 competitive range and has been ranked among those proposals which have a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, the federal agency shall consult with the Secretary [of Education] in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a), which is the second triggering event.. Priority in the operation of a cafeteria shall be afforded when the Secretary determines, after consulting with the agency, whether the SLA’s proposal can provide cafeteria operations at a reasonable cost with high quality food. See 34 C.F.R. 395.33(a). Under section 395.33(a), the Secretary of Education will consult with the federal agency and determine whether the SLA can provide operation of the cafeteria at a reasonable cost and with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees. To summarize, priority in the operation of the cafeteria by a blind vendor will be afforded if the SLA satisfies three regulatory hurdles: (1) section 395.33(b) requires that the SLA proposal be within the competitive range with a chance for final award, which then triggers consultation as prescribed by section 395.33(a); (2) under section 395.33(a), the Secretary consults with the agency and determines if the SLA can provide cafeteria operations at a reasonable cost and provide high quality food; (3) if the Secretary determines in favor of the SLA, the Secretary can grant the SLA a priority award over other proposals whose price might be lower. In The State of Texas v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 8, 23 (2017), Judge Horn provides a comprehensive and detailed explanation of how the RSA priority is determined and processed. In The State of Texas, Judge Horn states that “[t]he implementing regulations allow the United States Secretary of Education to have a specific role in whether a priority should be afforded to an SLA.” Id. at 25. The RSA priority need not be stated in the Solicitation, nor does the RSA priority require automatic award to the SLA by the agency. Rather, the RSA priority is applied by the Secretary of Education only after “the multi-step process delineated in the Randolph Sheppard Act implementing regulations has effectively been completed.” Id. IV. ANALYSIS
A. Neither the RSA nor its implementing regulations require establishment of a competitive range for this procurement.
The ultimate issue before this Panel is whether the Air Force violated the RSA or its implementing regulations by not establishing a competitive range as part of its evaluation process. The SLA failed to meet its burden under 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a) and 5 U.S.C. § 556(d) because the SLA fails to identify any express provision in the RSA or its implementing regulations that expressly requires a procuring agency to establish a competitive range for such procurements. With respect to the RSA, the term “competitive range” is not mentioned at all in the Act. The term “priority” is not defined in the Act or its implementing regulations. 20 U.S.C. § 107e; 34 C.F.R. § 395.1.
Although the term “competitive range” is not defined in either the Act or the implementing regulations, it is mentioned in 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b), but is not defined in the implementing regulations. See 34 C.F.R.§ 395.1 (Terms). Section 395.33(b) provides that when a federal agency contemplates a cafeteria contract, the agency shall invite the SLA to respond to the agency solicitation. Such solicitations shall contain criteria under which all responses will be judged. See 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). This section further provides that if the proposal submitted by the SLA is judged to be within a competitive range and ranked among those proposals which have a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, the agency shall consult with the Secretary as required by section 395.33(a). It should be noted that this section contains the word “shall” in three distinct parts mandating specific action by the agency. However, the sentence pertaining to the competitive range does not contain such mandatory language. The drafters of the implementing regulation had the opportunity to mandate that the agency establish a competitive range for solicitations when a cafeteria contract is contemplated, but no such language is present. Based upon a fair reading of the express terms of section 395.33(b) and the absence of the word “shall,” the establishment of a “competitive range” by a federal contracting officer is not required by the RSA or its implementing regulations.
The Air Force complied with the competitive selection process outlined in the RSA and implementing regulations, that is, the Air Force invited the SLA to submit a proposal and the Air Force prepared a solicitation with specific evaluation criteria. See 34 C.F.R. 395.33(b). The Panel’s Decision wants to read into the implementing regulation words that are simply not there. Doing so would be tantamount to legislating, which would exceed this Panel’s authority. The SLA has failed to reveal any language in the RSA or its regulations or applicable case law that requires the procuring agency to establish a competitive range in every procurement.
The contract in question is a Federal acquisition conducted by a Federal agency, by a warranted contracting officer and funded by specific Congressional appropriations for the Department of Defense. As such, the acquisition in question is subject to the requirements of the Federal Acquisition Regulation (“FAR”) and other federal procurement statutes. Ms. Amanda Holmes, the Air Force Contracting Officer, testified that the RSA and its implementing regulations provide some limited procedural guidance for contemplated cafeteria contracts. Transcript Record (“TR”), Volume 2, pg. 78. The RSA does not consider acquisition strategy, marketing strategy, the composition of a solicitation, the creation of a performance work statement, the multitude of FAR provisions that must be followed, and many other federal procurement requirements that impact the procedures and execution of a federal procurement. TR, Volume 2, pg. 79. Since the RSA and its implementing regulations are silent and fail to define the term “competitive range,” contracting officers are required to follow the requirements specified in the FAR and applicable case law.
Because the FAR provisions do not conflict with the DOE implementing regulations, the FAR can be equally applied to this acquisition. “Competitive range” is defined in the FAR and provides detailed guidance for all federal procurements and federal contracting officers. See FAR section 15.306. Federal procurement law and the FAR require that the evaluation of proposals, and the determination of whether a proposal is in the competitive range, are matters within the contracting agency’s discretion as agencies are responsible for defining their needs and for deciding the best method for meeting them. Capital Co., LLC., B-287460, 2001 Comp. Gen. Proc. Dec. P107 at 3-4. The decision to establish or not establish a competitive range is solely vested with the federal contracting officer. See FAR 15.306(c). Contracting officers have wide discretion under federal procurement regulations to apply and interpret procurement regulations. North Carolina Division of Services for the Blind v. United States, 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 163 (Fed. Cl. 2002).7 Courts have long recognized that contracting officers are “entitled to exercise discretion upon a broad range of issues confronting them” in the procurement process. Impresa Construzioni Geom. Domenico Garufi v. United States, 238 F.3d 1324, 1332 (Fed Cir. 2001). Contracting officers need to only provide a coherent and reasonable explanation of their exercise of discretion and Ms. Holmes provided her reasoning in her Award Decision Document. Id., Respondent’s Exhibit S, Award Decision Document. A “disappointed bidder bears a ‘heavy burden’ of showing that the award decision ‘had no rational basis’” and the SLA has failed to meet that burden. Impresa, 238 F.3d at 1333. Neither the RSA nor its implementing regulations require the contracting officer to establish a competitive range. The work contemplated by the contract in question is not unique or complex. The Air Force, Navy, Army, and other Federal Agencies contract for food services. The food services provided under the contract are clearly defined and are widely available on the commercial market. As Ms. Holmes testified, a “discussion” is a communication with offerors within the competitive range identifying significant weaknesses or deficiencies with the offeror’s proposals. TR, Volume 2, pg. 20-23. Based upon her market research, the receipt of eight (8) proposals, of which four (4) were found to be technically acceptable, Ms. Holmes felt that establishment of a competitive range and discussions with offerors were not necessary. TR, Volume 2, pg. 24-26; Respondent’s Exhibit S, Award Decision Document at paragraph 4. This decision was within her discretion and is consistent with the RSA, its implementing regulations, and the FAR.
The assertion by the Panel Decision that the Act (20 U.S.C. §§ 107 et seq.) is nullified if a federal agency does not establish a competitive range is erroneous. See Panel Decision, page 11. The SLA’s proposal was eliminated from award because of its 24% price differential. Had the SLA’s price been competitive, they would have received the award despite there being no competitive range. Failure to establish a competitive range in every procurement is permitted by the FAR and does not nullify the RSA. The Source Selection Evaluation Board (“SSEB”) comprised of a team of experienced procurement professionals evaluated every proposal thoroughly and fairly. The Air Force had changed the dining facility procurement from previous contracts by assuming more of the managerial responsibilities and maintenance and repair. The SLA did not adjust their pricing proposal to reflect this change in the contract requirements.
The Panel Decision erroneously avers that the “[t]he RSA Act standard governing the award of this contract is at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b), it is whether the blind vendor can ‘operate a cafeteria in such a manner as to provide food service at comparable cost and comparable high quality as that available from other providers of cafeteria services.’” (emphasis added). Panel Decision, page 11. The above underlined quote is incomplete as it is missing the beginning and end of the sentence (underlined below in footnote 8) which adds proper context and meaning to the entire first sentence of section 395.33(b). The entire first sentence (see footnote 8) of section 395.33(b) requires federal agencies to invite SLAs to submit a bid when a cafeteria contract is contemplated; in order to determine whether blind vendors have the ability to provide food service at comparable cost and quality as other providers. The first sentence of section 395.33(b) does not establish the ‘RSA Act standard’ governing award. Rather, the governing standard for award of any federal contract is that the awardee’s proposal is judged by the criteria specified in the Solicitation as evidenced by a reading of the entirety of section 395.33(b). See 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b).
The SLA has failed to satisfy its “heavy burden” in establishing that the Contracting Officer’s decision to not establish a competitive rage had no “rational basis.” Accordingly, the Contracting Officer’s decision to not establish a competitive range did not violate the RSA and its implementing regulations because neither requires that a competitive range be established.
B. The SLA waived its right to challenge the solicitation when it submitted its offer.
In its complaint, the SLA alleges that the Air Force violated the RSA by not setting a competitive range, even though neither the RSA nor its implementing regulations require it. The solicitation made clear that the Air Force was going to award the contract without conducting discussions with offerors and, therefore, was not going to set a competitive range. By submitting its offer, the SLA acceded to the terms and conditions in the solicitation and waived its claim. The SLA first learned about the Air Force’s intention not to establish a competitive range when the solicitation was first posted on November 12, 2019. Proposals were due on December 17, 2019. At no time during that period did the SLA object to the Air Force’s decision to award the contract without having discussions and setting a competitive range.
The Solicitation included FAR provision 52.212-1, Instructions to Offerors – Commercial Items. Subparagraph (g) of provision 52.212-1 stated
(g) Contract Award (not applicable to Invitation for Bids). The Government intends to evaluate offers and award a contract without discussions with offerors. Therefore, the offeror's initial offer should contain the offeror's best terms from a price and technical standpoint. However, the Government reserves the right to conduct discussions if later determined by the Contracting Officer to be necessary. The Government may reject any or all offers if such action is in the public interest; accept other than the lowest offer; and waive informalities and minor irregularities in offers received.
Respondent’s Exhibit F, Excerpts from Solicitation, pg. 58/139.
It was clear from the Solicitation that the Air Force intended to award the contract without discussions with offerors. Discussions are negotiations conducted in a competitive acquisition after the establishment of a competitive range. FAR 15.306(d). As explained in FAR 15.306(c), an agency establishes a competitive range only when the agency decides it is unable to make award without discussions. FAR 15.306(c)(1) directs in part that “if discussions are to be conducted, establish the competitive range.”
It was clearly stated in the solicitation that award of the contract would be made without discussions. Respondent’s Exhibit F, Excerpts from Solicitation, pg. 58/139. If award is made without discussions, the Air Force would not set a competitive range nor is it required to under the applicable laws and regulations. As Mr. James testified, the SLA and its teaming partner knew the meaning of “without discussions.” TR, Volume 1, pg. 217, 251. The SLA assumed they would win the award and did not revise their price from previous contracts even though the performance requirements had significantly changed. TR, Volume 1, pg. 247. In fact, Mr. James seemed to be under the impression that the Contracting Officer would reverse engineer his company’s pricing by referring to their charts to take out their management, which was not a contractual requirement. TR, Volume 1, pg. 249. If the SLA disagreed with the Air Force’s decision to award the contract without conducting discussions and establishing a competitive range, the proper time to object was prior to submitting its offer. However, by submitting its offer the SLA acceded to the terms and conditions of the solicitation and represented to the Air Force that its initial offer contained its best terms from a price and technical standpoint. Respondent’s Exhibit F, Excerpts from Solicitation, pg. 58/139. By failing to clarify whether the Air Force was going conduct discussions (and thus establish a competitive range) before it submitted, the SLA acceded to the terms and conditions in the solicitation and therefore waived its claim.
The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has expressly held that if a party fails to object to an ambiguity or patent error in a solicitation prior to the solicitation closing time, it waives the ability to raise the objection subsequent to the closing date. Blue & Gold Fleet, L.P. v. United States, 492 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2007). The Court explained that it would be inefficient and costly to allow dissatisfied parties to protest the terms of the solicitation after offerors and the agency had expended considerable time and effort submitting or evaluating proposals in response to a defective solicitation. Id. at 1315. Rather than challenge the plain terms of the solicitation prior to submitting its offer, the SLA opted to bid on the contract. But ‘“[v]endors cannot sit on their rights to challenge what they believe is an unfair solicitation, roll the dice and see if they receive award [sic] and then, if unsuccessful, claim the solicitation was infirm.’” Id.
The waiver rule has been applied to cases involving the RSA. In North Carolina Division of Services for the Blind v. United States, the Court held where there is a "deficiency or problem in a solicitation. . .the proper procedure for the offeror to follow is not to wait to see if it is the successful offeror before deciding whether to challenge the procurement, but rather to raise the objection in a timely fashion." North Carolina Division of Services for the Blind v. United States, 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 165 (Fed. Cl. 2002). In Moore’s Cafeteria Services v. United States, the Court applied the holding in Blue & Gold Fleet and held that the “plaintiff [Moore’s] had the opportunity to object to the terms of the solicitation during the bidding process, and in not doing so, waived its right to do so before this court.” Moore’s Cafeteria Services v. United States, 77 Fed. Cl. 180,185 (Fed Cl. 2007). Just as in Blue & Gold Fleet, North Carolina Division of Services for the Blind and Moore’s Cafeteria Services, the SLA should have objected to the Air Force’s decision to not set a competitive range prior to submitting its offer. By submitting its offer, the SLA acceded to the terms and conditions in the solicitation, thus waiving its claim. Accordingly, their complaint should have been dismissed.
The panel majority erroneously relies upon The State of Texas, 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017) as support for its assertion that filing a pre-award bid protest would be futile. In The State of Texas, the Court of Federal Claims assumed jurisdiction over the SLA’s RSA pre-award bid protest but dismissed the matter because their claim was not ripe. Id. (“protestors, the State of Texas. . .and Roland Marshall, a blind vendor, bring this pre-award bid protest.”). The Court determined that case was not ripe because the Secretary of Education and agency had not completed the 34 C.F.R. §395.33(a) consultation process. Id. at 25. It should be noted that the procurement at issue in The State of Texas case was a best value competition, as in this case. Id. at 28.
The Court of Federal Claims has assumed jurisdiction over pre-award bid protests challenging agency RSA solicitations since 2003. In the State of Washington v. United States, 58 Fed. Cl. 781 (2003), the Washington SLA challenged an Army solicitation that did not include mention of the RSA priority in the solicitation. The solicitation at issue in the Washington State case was a Section 8(a) small business set-aside and best value contract - such as in this case. The Court of Federal Claims did not dismiss the SLA’s claim for lack of jurisdiction, but decided the case on the merits (“This action is a pre-award bid protest jurisdiction alleging the violation of an applicable procurement statute.”). State of Washington at 796. Accordingly, the Utah SLA had an obligation under applicable case law to file a legal challenge to a patent ambiguity in the Air Force solicitation prior to the submission of their offer. Failure to do so constitutes a waiver of their claim. As noted above, the Court of Federal Claims has assumed jurisdiction relating to RSA pre-award bid protests filed by SLA’s challenging agency solicitations. The Panel Decision finding that such legal actions would be futile is in error.
C. The Department of Education Handbook is not entitled to Skidmore Deference.
The panel majority erroneously finds that a retired Department of Education handbook, Administration of the Randolph-Sheppard Vending Program by Federal Property Managing Agencies (“DOE Handbook”) (undated), is entitled to Skidmore deference relating to competitive range determinations. See Petitioner’s Pre-Hearing Exhibit 5, DOE Handbook. As the Panel Decision indicates, the Department of Education advised the Air Force on July 15, 2021 that the DOE Handbook is “retired, and therefore, no longer in effect.” Panel Decision, page 14. The Department of Education, the agency that published the DOE Handbook, through the official action taken by Commissioner Janet L. LaBreck, officially retired the DOE Handbook – it is no longer in effect. Respondent’s Exhibit JJ; Panel Decision page 14. The Panel Decision’s conclusion that a retired agency handbook that is no longer in effect is somehow entitled to continuing Skidmore deference is not only erroneous, but could have unintended consequences for a federal agency. Under the Panel Decision, it is plausible that a retired and withdrawn federal agency “handbook” would still be persuasive authority long after it has been retired and no longer in effect. This could effectively preclude a federal agency’s efforts to repeal, rescind, withdraw, and retire agency publications that are outdated, contrary to law, or contrary to updated policies and procedures. Retired handbooks and regulations would continue to be sources of persuasive authority, particularly for those litigants who would choose to use the retired handbooks and regulations against the government. A federal agency is charged with
managing its agency publications and has authority to rescind, retire, and withdraw outdated publications. Until the agency promulgates a replacement publication, other existing and active agency publications provide guidance for that agency. Those regulations would be entitled to Skidmore deference. In this instance, the RSA implementing regulation enacted in 1977 at 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.1-395.37 provide guidance relating to the RSA, to include the requirement to establish or not establish a competitive range determination in all cafeteria contracts. Absent any definitive guidance in the implementing regulations on this issue at section 395.33, the comprehensive Federal Acquisition Regulation controls, specifically FAR 15.306.
Without any legal support, authority, or discussion, the Panel Decision concluded that “because the Handbook has been in effect since 1988, and because the R-S Act has not been amended in the interim, the Handbook is persuasive in determining when and how an SLA’s bid is to be considered in the competitive range.” Panel Decision, page 15. The Panel Decision would have a federal agency, the Department of Education, continue to follow a retired Handbook that was no longer in conformance with the law, as persuasive authority. In support of this legal conclusion, the Panel Decision cites to only one case, Skidmore v. Swift Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) as their legal authority for a “retired, withdrawn, no longer in effect” DOE Handbook to be entitled to Skidmore deference for federal agencies. In Skidmore, the deference discussed was not related to a ‘retired, no longer in effect’ handbook. Id.
In Petitioner’s Pre-Award Brief, Petitioner asserts that the DOE’s interpretation of its own regulations are “controlling unless plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation.” Auer v. Robbins, 519 U.S. 452, 461 (1997); Petitioner’s Pre-Award Brief, page 14. The only DOE regulations still in effect with respect to the competitive range determination are the implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.1- 395.37. As noted supra, section 395.33(b) does not require a federal agency to create a competitive range for an RSA procurement.
The Panel Decision’s conclusion that the retired DOE Handbook is entitled to Skidmore deference is erroneous.
D. The Decision and Award are Contrary to Law.
As noted above, the Panel Decision that the Air Force violated the RSA and its implementing regulations in the Hill AFB procurement is erroneous. Further, the Award determination by the Panel Decision that the Air Force “is obligated under the R-S Act and its implementing regulations to resolicit the Solicitation at issue and grant Utah and its blind vendor the priority afforded by the R-S Act” is ultra vires.
The authority of a DOE panel convened under 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(b)(2) is limited. The Fourth Circuit in Maryland State Dep't of Educ. v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affairs, 98 F.3d 165 (4th Cir. 1996) held that “the plain language of § 107d–2(b)(2) limits the authority of an arbitration Panel convened under § 107d–1(b) to a determination of whether the acts of the federal entity ‘are in violation’ of the substantive provisions of the Act.” 98 F.3d at 169. “[T]he statute places the responsibility for ending the violation on the head of the federal entity and does not authorize a § 107d–1(b) arbitration Panel to order the federal entity to take specific remedial action.” Id.; see 20 U.S.C. § 107d–2(b) (requiring that it is the responsibility of the head of the agency to “cause the violation to be terminated promptly, and to take other action required to carry out the arbitration Panel's decision.”) (emphasis added). The plain reading of the statute makes it clear that the Panel’s role is solely limited to determining whether an agency’s actions violated the RSA or its regulations. The scope of the arbitration Panel’s authority was also addressed in Georgia Dep’t of Human Resources v. Nash, 915 F.2d 1482 (11th Cir. 1990). The 11th Circuit looked specifically at the language of the RSA itself and concluded that arbitration Panels only have the authority to decide whether the federal entity's actions are in violation of the Act. The Court found that although the Panel may determine that a violation is occurring and may identify the discrete acts that are in violation, the statute does not authorize the arbitration Panel to order the federal entity to take any remedial action. Id. at 1492. The Court further stated that the statute expressly places the obligation of ending the violation on the federal entity itself and that the Secretary of Education has no authority to order another federal entity to take or terminate any action. Id. Finally, in United States v. Mississippi Vocational Rehab. for the Blind, the Court citing 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(b)(2) held “Panels convened to arbitrate disputes between federal entities and SLA’s have no affirmative remedial authority.” United States v. Mississippi Vocational Rehab. for the Blind, 794 F. Supp. 1344, 1347 (S.D. Miss. 1992). Again, the Panel’s role is solely limited to determining whether an agency’s actions violated the RSA or its regulations. The Arbitration Panel does not have the authority to order the Air Force to terminate a contract, terminate a solicitation, award a contract, resolicit, grant a priority, or cancel a procurement. Such limited authority is consistent with the limited remedial powers of a court as courts lack jurisdiction to order the government to award a contract to anyone. See Caddell Constr.v. United States, 7 Cl. Ct. 236 (1985). Any broader exercise of authority would exceed the limited authority of the Panel.
IV. CONCLUSION
The Air Force was not required by law or regulation to establish a competitive range or conduct discussions for this procurement. The published Solicitation notified all potential offerors, to include the SLA, that there would no discussions and as such, all offerors were on notice to submit their best and final offers. After evaluation of the submitted proposals by a Source Selection Evaluation Board, the contract was awarded to NRDCI. The SLA was not awarded the contract due to its exceptionally high price.
The Air Force’s procedures in preparing and issuing the Solicitation and evaluation of the proposals complied with the FAR, the RSA, and the RSA implementing regulations. As there was no competitive range established, the provisions of 34 C.F.R. § 395.33 to consult the Secretary of Education if the SLA’s proposal was in the competitive range did not apply.
There is no “feasibility standard” for contract awards in the RSA. Rather, federal agencies should seek to have an RSA vending facility located on federal property, “wherever feasible.” As the legislative history of this provision indicates, “wherever feasible” is a goal or milestone for federal agencies to attain; it is not a statutory requirement. Although this issue was not addressed during the proceeding, if Hill AFB has at least one RSA vending machine or vending stand on the base, it has complied with the aspirational goal of having one or more vending facilities located on the base, if feasible.
Finally, any remedial measures to be taken as a result of the Panel’s Decision is vested with the Secretary of the Air Force. Any suggestions of remedial measures by the Panel (“resolicit the Solicitation” and “grant Utah and the blind vendor the priority afforded”) are ultra vires. As a matter of law, the panel convened for RS 20-04 has limited authority – that is, to determine whether the Air Force violated the RSA and its implementing regulations. As such, the panel has no authority to make specific remedial measures be taken.
The Air Force procurement for cafeteria support services at Hill AFB did not violate the RSA and its implementing regulations. The procurement was fairly conducted and complied with all federal laws, regulations, and applicable case law.
