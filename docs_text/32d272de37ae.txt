SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (Section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act) and, pursuant to 
Section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by the Florida Division of 
Vocational Rehabilitation (DVR). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the process by 
which RSA carried out its activities from February 13 through February 15, 2024, was defined 
by information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, documents, and 
data submitted by DVR, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, and technical 
assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
• Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities; 
• Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

2 
achieve continuous improvement in assisting individuals with disabilities to achieve 
quality employment outcomes; 
• Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities, 
including students and youth with disabilities; 
• Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported 
Employment programs and recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the 
VR agency’s financial management of fiscal resources and strategies for maximizing the 
use of Federal grant funds;  
• Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and 
procedures, including the development or revision of policies and internal controls 
supporting effective and efficient program operations, service delivery, and data-based 
decision making; and  
• Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic 
actions to improve and maximize program and fiscal performance and the use of Federal 
funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops 
strategies to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds to 
achieve quality employment outcomes. 
 
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE  
 
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by DVR on the Case Service Report (RSA-911) 
and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 2021, 
and 2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and requested 
documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR services, 
quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as discussions 
with DVR of potential factors influencing program performance informed the analysis and 
helped to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the performance 
and management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
 
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from 
related sources, and discussions with DVR during the review, RSA identified various factors, 
which may be contributing either positively or negatively to DVR’s performance leading to 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of Federal 
funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to DVR’s 
requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by the VR agency as it 
develops strategies to ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to maximize 
the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with disabilities in 
achieving quality employment outcomes. 
 
Staff Capacity and Participant Engagement 
 
During the review, DVR reported having 884 positions within the Bureau of Field Services, of 
which 156 were vacant, representing a vacancy rate of approximately 17.65 percent for positions

4 
providing services to individuals with disabilities. DVR operates 16 field offices under a private 
vendor contract, which employs 144 staff who carry out the functions of the VR agency, other 
than those non-delegable responsibilities of the designated State unit (DSU) described in 34 
C.F.R. § 361.13(c). In addition, a DVR counselor analyst, responsible for the non-delegable 
duties of the VR counselors for the determination of eligibility, approval and amendment of 
IPEs, and closure of service records is assigned to every unit staffed by the vendor. 
 
DVR reported making progress in staff recruitment and retention through salary increases for 
counselors, rehabilitation technicians, senior counselors, consultants, and supervisors. The 
agency anticipates these pay increases, some as high as 35 to 40 percent, will result in an 
additional six million dollars in expenditures and will have a positive impact on hiring, staff 
retention, and effective and efficient service delivery to VR program participants. These 
developments are encouraging, as persistently high vacancy rates can affect the overall 
performance of DVR. Insufficient staffing often results in higher caseloads and reduced 
opportunities for meaningful and sustained engagement, resulting in low instances of 
participation, disinterest in VR services, and exits from the VR program prior to achieving 
competitive integrated employment.  
 
Data representing attrition for the period reviewed showed over 64 percent of individuals who 
exited did so after an eligibility determination, but without an employment outcome. Of the 
program exits reported, DVR reported over 46 percent as exiting after an IPE for each year. 
While the reasons for exit without an employment outcome varied, the most frequently reported 
reason during the review period was that the individual was no longer interested in receiving 
services or further services. The next highest reason for exit reported was the inability to locate 
or contact individuals. This suggests that eligible individuals with disabilities are not sufficiently 
engaged during the VR process, may not be receiving needed services, or are not receiving 
services in a timely manner. Reducing field service vacancies is a critical early step in 
addressing these issues. 
 
Technical Assistance  
 
• RSA explored staff retention strategies, beyond the agency reported pay increases, and 
encouraged staff engagement in the development of policies and training. 
• RSA clarified how frequent, meaningful, and sustained contact by VR counselors with 
individuals participating in all phases of the VR process can result in less attrition and 
suggested that DVR develop written policies outlining the frequency of meaningful 
engagement with participants. 
 
Recommendations 
 
• DVR should continue to explore methods and practices that result in sustained and 
intentional engagement for both staff and individuals with disabilities, including those 
identified in 
RSA-TAC-24-01 Promoting Meaningful and Sustained Engagement of 
Individuals with Disabilities in the State Vocational Rehabilitation Services Program to 
support staff and participant retention and satisfaction, timely provision of needed quality 
services leading to long-term success, and performance improvement.

• DVR should analyze its data and performance related to program participants’ reasons for 
exits, identify deficiencies in policies or practices, and develop a plan to address the 
deficiencies and attrition rates at various points in the VR process.  
• DVR should analyze the impact of vacant positions on the agency’s performance and 
further explore strategies to increase staff recruitment and retention, including contracted

Impact of COVID-19 Pandemic and Natural Disasters 
 
In PY 2020, of the 46,389 participants reported by the agency, 5,052 exited in competitive 
integrated employment or supported employment. The employment rate for the same period was 
38.5 percent. In PY 2021, DVR reported the highest number of participants exiting in 
competitive integrated employment or supported employment during the three program years 
reviewed, 5,669 of the 44,985 reported participants. The employment rate for PY 2021 was 38.4 
percent.  
 
DVR experienced significant challenges during the period under review, including the COVID-
19 pandemic and multiple weather events (e.g., a hurricane and major tropical storms). Although 
DVR quickly pivoted to virtual service delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic, it reported 
decreased numbers of program participants accessing and receiving postsecondary education and 
training services due to the restrictions and challenges imposed by the pandemic. DVR attributed 
its declines in performance to a loss of employers, limited access to service providers, social 
distancing practices, and the receipt of Economic Impact Payments (a possible disincentive for 
individuals with disabilities to seek or continue services). In addition, the slow recovery of 
hospitality industries, which historically provided employment opportunities, for VR program 
participants, affected DVR’s performance. 
 
While the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed partially to the declines in performance 
noted by DVR, the agency consistently reported low participation rates in training services for all 
program years in review. For example, of the 42,776 individuals who received VR services in 
PY 2020, 2,112 or 4.9 percent received bachelor’s degree training, the highest participation 
reported for that service in all three program years. In PY 2021 and PY 2022, 0.1 percent of 
individuals were reported as having participated in graduate degree training. In PY 2022, 3,893 
individuals or 9.8 percent received on-the-job-training, the highest participation reported for that 
service in all three years. In PY 2020, 466 individuals or 1.1 percent received occupational or 
vocational training. Declines or low percentages of postsecondary education and training 
services cannot be solely attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic.  
 
According to DVR, multiple weather events also impacted the agency’s performance during the 
years under review. DVR shared that when communities are impacted by major weather-related 
incidents, residents often move away or face limited resources due to the amount of time it takes 
to rebuild. The recovery process after a natural disaster also adversely affects employment 
opportunities, which are typically scarce after such events. DVR shared that, despite its efforts to 
ensure access to and delivery of services during disaster recovery through increased outreach, 
limitations beyond the agency’s control resulting from the natural disasters and recovery process 
negatively affected performance. RSA acknowledges the challenges weather events can pose as

6 
well as the potential strain on service delivery and engagement; however, unlike the pandemic, 
they typically do not adversely impact an entire state.  
 
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA explored strategies with DVR to increase quality employment outcomes, including 
the importance of supporting individuals in achieving their greatest level of employment 
and increasing the number of customers who participate in postsecondary education and 
training services that often lead to high quality outcomes with long-term labor market 
attachment. 
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends the agency evaluate its continuity of operations plan to ensure it 
includes contingency planning for a natural disaster or major event to minimize the 
interruption of services and identify alternatives for service delivery to sustain participant 
engagement and progress toward employment goals. 
 
Non-Compliant Policies and Procedures 
 
In accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.50, State VR agencies must develop and maintain written 
policies covering the nature and scope of each of the VR services specified in 34 C.F.R. § 361.48 
and the criteria under which each service is provided. RSA’s review of the DVR Counselor 
Policy Manual revealed multiple policies in need of revision including those that required 
updating to reflect current statutory and regulatory requirements and guidance, or clarification 
for implementation by VR counselors. DVR stated that it has not historically established a 
timeframe for policy review, but it typically addresses policy updates and revisions when new 
Federal guidance is issued. However, DVR reported that its recent purchase and migration to a 
new case management system in FFY 2023, with implementation in FFY 2024, dictated the need 
to revise policies and procedures. Although DVR indicated it was reviewing and revising its 
administrative rules, policies, and procedures, none of these were available for RSA review at 
the time of monitoring. DVR welcomed RSA’s suggested policy updates and feedback regarding 
the importance of timely updates and shared that as it moves forward in its policy development it 
plans to attach some policies to administrative rules to allow for greater enforcement authority. 
DVR also reported it has no established internal controls for policy review.  
  
RSA identified and discussed several areas within DVR policies that may influence the 
performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs and the full expenditure of funds, 
including those areas requiring technical assistance or revisions to ensure statutory or regulatory 
compliance. Some examples from the DVR Counselor Policy Manual include—  
  
Chapter 4, Referrals, Applications, Preliminary Assessment and Diagnosis, 4.00 Referrals, 
includes a list of information that, when available, will be provided by the referring agent 
(which could include any agency, organization, individual, self-referral or the One-Stop 
delivery system). The list includes full name, address/e-mail address, telephone number 
where referred individual may be reached, social security number (SSN), if available, date of

7 
birth, if available, and the contact person’s name, phone number and referral source. The 
policy also references a VR referral form, which is available in an electronic format (via the 
agency website, www.rehabworks.org). The Referral to Vocational Rehabilitation form 
states, “Please complete this page and mail or turn in the referral to the nearest VR office,” 
which DVR indicated could be submitted via mail, fax, or in-person delivery. While the 
agency reported it has removed language indicating that the SSN is mandatory, which was 
included in the October 2019 version of the form, the October 2022 version still collects the 
SSN along with information related to a variety of elements typically collected at application, 
including race, ethnicity, marital status, education level, types of required accommodations, 
work related impairments, and how VR can help the individual become employed. The form 
does not indicate reporting this information is optional, and in fact, the information collected 
more clearly meets the criteria of an application for services as described in 34 C.F.R. § 
361.41(b)(2). The extent of information DVR collects prior to an application for services is 
not required and is subject to the requirements in 34 C.F.R. § 361.38 Protection, use, and 
release of personal information discussed in the Findings and Corrective Actions. 
Information collected on the referral form should be limited to that needed to contact the 
individual to learn more about their individual circumstances and their interest in applying 
for services.  
  
Chapter 4, 4.18 Hearing Loss, item 3.a, states, “An ophthalmologic evaluation shall be 
obtained in all cases of deafness, retinitis pigmentosa, and [U]sher syndrome.” RSA 
understands that vision loss can be concurrent with other diagnoses; however, requiring an 
ophthalmologic evaluation in all cases of deafness should be based on individual 
circumstances and as a result of the self-report of an individual or the counseling and 
guidance process to ensure all barriers to employment are addressed rather than a one size 
fits all approach. Extensive assessment may not be necessary for the purposes of eligibility 
determination, but rather to determine significance of disability or to fully assess VR needs.  
Requiring evaluations of all individuals with deafness may result in delays in the provision of 
services and an unnecessary expenditure of funds.  
  
Chapter 9, Authorization for Services, Financial Participation Determination, Comparable 
Services and Benefits, Third Party Payers, 9.02 Financial Participation Determination, item 
1. General Policy b., states, “If an individual or family member refuses to provide the 
information necessary to determine participation, or if the individual or family refuses to 
accept responsibility for their expected contribution, DVR cannot authorize services subject 
to financial participation.” Item 4. Financial Participation Assessment (FPA), indicates 
individuals are required to complete the Financial Participation Assessment except for “a. 
Individuals who receive SSDI [Social Security Disability Insurance], SSI [Supplemental 
Security Income], public assistance (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families [TANF]), 
SNAP Food Stamps] and/or other General Assistance), or b. Any individual who has annual 
income less than 285% of the Federal Health and Human Services Poverty Guidelines, or c. 
Individuals who were not legally required to file a U.S. Tax Return in the year prior to 
application.” While there is no Federal requirement that the financial need of individuals be 
considered in the provision of VR services as described in 34 C.F.R. § 361.54(a), DVR has 
exercised its authority to consider the financial need of individuals in the provision of 
services as found in 34 C.F.R. § 361.54(b)(1) and (2). DVR’s determination for financial

8 
participation includes the requirement to provide supporting documentation described in 
item 10. Documentation, that states, “The documentation must include U.S. tax returns, 
employment security wage reports, employer generated pay stubs, proof of the impairment 
related work expenses (IRWEs), and any other information provided. IRWE cannot be self-
reported.” The agency is requiring a large amount of documentation an individual with a 
disability must produce to support their financial claims, including completion of an itemized 
budget, proof of an impairment related work expense, and tax returns, which may preclude, 
deter, or delay the provision of services. These requirements may influence a participant’s 
engagement in the VR process and achievement of a successful employment outcome.  
  
Chapter 13, Vocational Training Services, 13.05 Florida Public Postsecondary Educational 
Institutions strongly advises that individuals attend Florida local public postsecondary 
institutions and refers to these as the least cost accredited institutions. Furthermore, 13.07 
Community Colleges/Universities strongly advises that the first two years of college training 
be provided at the local State/community college level before transitioning to the four-year 
university environment. These policies may unduly influence a counselor’s decision to 
support a participant’s informed choice and attendance entirely at a four-year institution. 
While this policy may appear to support cost savings and meet accommodation needs, the 
required disability supports may not be available or appropriate and may create additional 
burden to establish required supports at multiple postsecondary institutions. Consideration of 
individualized needs when exploring and supporting postsecondary education and training is 
important in ensuring a participant’s success. 
  
Chapter 14, Rehabilitation Services: Rehabilitation Technology Services, 14.07 Vehicles and 
Drivers Training states in item 1, “Provision of accessible vehicles shall be considered only 
after every other transportation option has been explored and only after it has been 
determined that provision of an accessible vehicle is the most cost efficient and appropriate 
approach for the client and DVR. DVR shall not authorize provision of an accessible vehicle 
when the individual can effectively utilize public transportation and other means of 
transportation.” Regarding vehicle modifications, Section 14.07.25 states, “The individual is 
responsible to purchase a suitable vehicle recommended by the RE [Rehabilitation 
Engineer].” According to DVR, as written, the policy has generated questions from the field, 
and as a result, implementation is likely to vary across the State.

Limited VR Counselor Autonomy in Authorizing Services 
  
Prior to a VR counselor having independent status, which depends on a counselor’s knowledge 
and experience, supervisors must approve IPEs. RSA learned that when a VR counselor has 
independent status, the IPE can be approved and signed by the VR counselor, but the VR 
counselor does not have the autonomy to authorize the purchase of a service, no matter the cost. 
All authorizations require supervisor approval, or in the case of oversight of a contracted VR 
counselor, the contracted unit supervisor can provide one level of approval and the DVR 
Counselor Analyst has a supervisor approval designation for authorizations. While checks and 
balances offer accountability, they may also affect the efficiency and timely provision of services 
when multiple levels of approval are required for most or all services on an IPE. Additionally, 
the lack of VR counselor autonomy and high level of supervisor oversight may create an 
environment where VR counselors do not feel empowered or trusted to serve individuals. 
 
Per 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.42(a)(1)(iii) and 361.45(d)(3)(ii), a qualified VR counselor must determine 
an applicant requires VR services to prepare for, secure, retain, advance in, or regain 
employment that is consistent with the individual's unique strengths, resources, priorities, 
concerns, abilities, capabilities, interest, and informed choice. An IPE is approved and signed by 
a qualified VR counselor employed by the DSU. These sections in regulation support the 
autonomy of the qualified VR counselor and their ability to approve the required services 
included on an IPE. DVR currently maintains multiple policies related to the authorization of

10 
services. Requiring that every authorization, regardless of the amount of the expenditure, be 
approved at a level beyond the qualified VR counselor with independent status has the potential 
to delay the provision of services to VR participants. 
 
Technical Assistance  
 
• RSA provided technical assistance regarding how counselor autonomy may support 
maximizing the use of funds to provide services that will strengthen quality employment 
outcomes through the investment in VR services and streamline the timely delivery of 
services. 
 
Recommendations  
 
• RSA recommends DVR revise its Request for Approval policy to support counselor 
autonomy when purchasing services that are already on an IPE and consider increasing 
purchasing authorization thresholds due to the overall increase in the cost of VR services 
and to increase efficiency and timeliness of service delivery. 
• DVR currently maintains multiple policies related to the authorization of services. RSA 
recommends consolidating these policies into one policy that aligns with regulation and 
facilitates counselor autonomy.  
 
Limited Provision of Training Services and Career Services  
 
The types of services a VR participant receives are related to the quality of employment 
outcomes achieved. For the program years in review, the total number of individuals who 
received VR Services was 42,776 in PY 2020, 43,283 in PY 2021, and 39,652 in PY 2023. Of 
those individuals who received VR services, a high percentage received VR counseling and 
guidance, a service most often provided by agency or contracted staff (88.1 percent in PY 2020, 
91 percent in PY 2021, and 92.1 percent in PY 2022). For the same period, DVR reported low 
instances of training, career, and other services that are often purchased through authorizations. 
The highest percentage and number of individuals reported, for the program years in review, was 
0.1 percent or 30 and 37 individuals, respectively, in PYs 2021 and 2022 for graduate degree 
training, 4.9 percent or 2,112 individuals in PY 2020 for bachelor’s degree training, and 1.4 
percent or 598 individuals in PY 2020 for junior or community college training. Additionally, 
the highest reported instances for individuals receiving on-the job-training and occupational or 
vocational training were in PY 2022, 3,893 or 9.8 percent and in PY 2020, 1.1 percent or 466, 
respectively. 
 
Similar to training services, the low number of career services provided to participants affects 
the quality of employment outcomes. In general, individuals may not be receiving the types of 
services that lead to high quality employment outcomes. In PYs 2020 through 2022, the agency 
reported job search assistance as high as 18.8 percent or 7,466 individuals in PY 2022 and as 
low as 9.6 percent or 4,102 individuals in PY 2020, job placement assistance as high as 11.4 
percent or 4,947 individuals and as low as 10.2 percent or 4,050 individuals in PY 2022, 
supported employment services as high as 5.4 percent or 2,327 and 2,157, respectively, in PYs 
2021 and 2022 and as low as 3.8 percent or 1,617 individuals in PY 2020.

11 
 
DVR reported low instances of other services provided to eligible individuals. For example, 
transportation services ranged from a high of 6.1 percent or 2,434 individuals in PY 2022 to a 
low of 3.0 percent or 1,275 individuals in PY 2020. Maintenance services never exceeded 1.1 
percent in all three program years reviewed. At the time of this review, it was unclear to RSA 
whether the agency was not providing transportation and maintenance services to all eligible 
individuals, who may require those services, or if the agency was not reporting every instance of 
these services. 
 
For program years in review, DVR’s employment rate was 38.5 percent in PY 2020, 38.4 
percent in PY 2021, and 37.8 percent in PY 2022. Of the participants who exited in competitive 
integrated employment or supported employment, the highest median hourly earnings reported 
at exit was $13 per hour, two dollars higher than Florida’s minimum wage (which increased to 
$11 per hour in 2022). For those same participants, the agency reported the highest median 
hours worked per week at exit as 30 hours in both PY 2020 and PY 2021. Historically, the 
agency’s Comprehensive Statewide Needs Assessment (CSNA) has indicated the majority of 
employment outcomes for individuals receiving services from DVR result in positions that are 
classified as lower skill jobs which typically pay lower hourly wages.  
 
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA explored strategies to increase quality employment outcomes, including relaxation 
of restrictive policies to maximize service provision and increase customer participation 
in postsecondary education and training services that often lead to high quality outcomes.  
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends that DVR thoroughly examine the quality and range of employment 
outcomes achieved by its participants and take intentional steps to provide services 
resulting in higher skill positions offering higher levels of compensation. 
 
Implementation of Order of Selection 
 
DVR has been operating under an order of selection (OOS) since 2008. The agency reported that 
maintaining an OOS contributes to resource management, citing counseling staff vacancies and 
funding as justification for the OOS. According to the 2022 modification of the State Plan, each 
month the executive leadership team looks at expenditure and revenue projections, number of 
individuals in each OOS category, number of individuals on the waiting list for services, and 
staff capacity to determine whether to release individuals from the waiting list, as well as 
reviewing the number of potentially eligible students with disabilities receiving pre-employment 
transition services. 
 
At the time of the monitoring review, the agency had only one priority category closed (priority 
category three), with a waiting list of 41 eligible individuals. Numbers of individuals and average 
time on a waiting list fluctuate and DVR leadership referred to the waiting list as a “pause” prior 
to release and reported that it has, on occasion, reached single digits or no individuals on the

12 
waiting list. Additionally, DVR reported no internal controls regarding the management of the 
order of selection. Given DVR’s current resources, the low number of individuals on the waiting 
list, and its plans to increase expenditures for pre-employment transition services, DVR should 
consider opening all priority categories to serve all eligible individuals and improve its 
performance. 
 
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA reviewed with DVR the criteria described in 34 C.F.R. § 361.36 Ability to serve all 
eligible individuals; order of selection for services.  
 
Recommendations 
 
• Based on the low number of individuals on the waiting list and the availability of 
resources, RSA recommends DVR discontinue its implementation of the OOS at this 
time and develop internal controls to include programmatic and fiscal forecasting to assist 
in determining the need to implement the OOS in the future. 
 
Inaccurate Data Reporting and Insufficient Internal Controls 
  
In accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.12, State VR agencies must implement policies and 
procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of the VR program, including 
those necessary to carry out all functions for which the State is responsible. DVR is required to 
establish policies and procedures that ensure the accurate collection, retention, and timely 
reporting of all data. RSA uses the data collected through the RSA-911 to describe the

Finding 2.2 Inaccurate Data Reporting and Insufficient Internal Controls 
 
Issue: Did DVR maintain effective internal controls over the Federal award to provide a 
reasonable assurance that it was managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 
200.303 and the requirements at 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.47(a), 361.48(a) and 361.50. 
 
Requirement: Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, VR agencies are required to develop internal 
control processes to provide reasonable assurances regarding the effectiveness and efficiency of 
operations, reliability of reporting for internal and external use, and implemented as a measure of 
checks and balances to ensure proper expenditure of funds, including the evaluation, and 
monitoring of compliance with statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of Federal 
awards. Furthermore, a State VR agency must assure in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. 
 
An internal control deficiency exists when the design or operation of a control does not allow 
management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to 
prevent or correct processes that might lead to non-compliance with Federal and State 
requirements.  
 
Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.47(a), VR agencies must maintain for each applicant and eligible 
individual a record of services that includes, to the extent pertinent, documentation including, but 
not limited to, the individual’s application for VR services, the individual’s IPE, and information 
related to closing the service record of an individual who achieves an employment outcome. The 
record of service must allow for accurate and timely data reports pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.40, 
and to provide the level of detail required by the Secretary regarding applicants for and eligible 
individuals receiving services, including students with disabilities receiving pre-employment

Period of Performance  
Period of performance is the time during which the non-Federal entity (grantee) may incur new 
obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal award (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). To 
accurately account for Federal and non-Federal funds, the VR agency must ensure that allowable 
non-Federal and Federal obligations and expenditures are assigned to the correct Federal fiscal 
year award. RSA uses the financial information reported by the grantee to determine each VR 
agency’s compliance with fiscal requirements (e.g., carryover, reservation of funds, matching, 
etc.).  
 
To accurately account for Federal and non-Federal funds, the VR agency must ensure that 
allowable non-Federal and Federal obligations and expenditures are assigned to the correct FFY 
award. Therefore, if the grantee is not assigning obligations and expenditures to the correct FFY, 
in accordance with the period of performance requirements, RSA is unable to determine, with 
certainty, the grantee’s compliance with these Federal requirements. Because issues identified in 
this area significantly affect other areas of the fiscal review, if substantive issues related to period 
of performance are identified, this area will become a primary focus area for the fiscal review. 
 
RSA assessed whether DVR met the period of performance requirements related to the proper 
assignment of obligations and expenditures to the correct grant award(s). In FFY 2023, the 
agency implemented a new case management system that replaced multiple legacy systems.

The agency uses the Florida Accounting Information Resource (FLAIR) accounting system as a 
solution for budgeting, accounts payable and billing, among other applications. During the 
review, RSA discussed the Education Department General Administration Regulations

date for various cost categories, which identifies the binding written commitment to obtain

services by a contractor who is not an employee of the State)). At the same time, per 34 C.F.R.

services are performed. These data must be captured and tracked to demonstrate a bona fide need 
benefiting the period of availability of funds. At present, the agency does not have the capability 
to assign and track the obligation date of the cost and would also be unable to return refunds 
back to the original award and unable to determine whether the cost was paid with Federal or 
non-Federal funds thus undermining the period of performance requirements per 34 C.F.R.  
§ 76.707.

This area addresses the requirements related to contracting for purchased services. Uniform 
Guidance requires that States follow the same policies and procedures it uses for procurements 
from its non-Federal funds (2 C.F.R. § 200.317). Contract policies are necessary to ensure that 
contract language results in the VR agency receiving the necessary data that it must collect and

and performance expectations are being achieved (2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a)). Through the use of 
contracts, VR agencies must relate financial data and accomplishments to performance goals and 
objectives of the Federal award, consistent with the reporting of data on the Federal Financial 
Report (SF-425), Vocational Rehabilitation Financial Report (RSA-17), and the Case Service 
Report (RSA-911) (2 C.F.R. § 200.329(b)). VR agencies are required to develop and maintain 
written policies governing rates of payment for all purchased VR services (34 C.F.R.

Finding 3.1 Insufficient Accounting of Expenditures  
 
Issue: Does DVR meet the accounting requirements for the VR program and the terms and 
conditions of the award including 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.12 and 76.702; 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.302, 
200.303(a), and 200.309; and consistent with the definitions at 2 C.F.R. § 200.1. 
 
Requirement: As a recipient of Federal VR program funds, DVR must have procedures that 
ensure the proper and efficient administration of its VR program and enable DVR to carry out all 
required functions, including financial reporting (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). In accordance with the 
Uniform Guidance in 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a), which applies to DVR’s VR program, a State’s 
financial management systems, including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the 
preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and conditions; and the 
tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have been used 
according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award. 
The Uniform Guidance requires the financial management system of each non-Federal entity to 
provide for the identification, in its accounts, of all Federal awards received and expended and 
the Federal programs under which they were received (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)). In addition, 
EDGAR at 34 C.F.R. § 76.702 requires States to use fiscal control and fund accounting 
procedures that ensure proper disbursement of and accounting for Federal funds.  
 
Each grant award has a defined “period of performance,” which is the time during which the 
non-Federal entity may incur new obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal 
award (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). A non-Federal entity may only charge to the Federal award allowable 
costs incurred during the period of performance (2 C.F.R. § 200.309, see also EDGAR at 
34 C.F.R. §§ 76.703 and 76.709). Grantees must implement internal controls necessary to ensure 
obligations and expenditures for a Federal award are assigned, tracked, recorded, and reported 
within the applicable period of performance for that Federal award, thereby ensuring the grantees 
are managing the award in compliance with Federal requirements (2 C.F.R. § 200.303(a)). The 
proper accounting of Federal and non-Federal funds within the correct period of performance is 
necessary for DVR to correctly account for VR program funds so that RSA can be assured that 
the agency has satisfied requirements for, among other things, match (34 C.F.R. §§ 361.60 and 
363.23(a)(2)), MOE (34 C.F.R. § 361.62), and carryover (34 C.F.R. §§ 361.64 and 363.25).  
 
In accordance with Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(1), the financial management 
system of each non-Federal entity must provide Federal program and Federal award 
identification including, as applicable, the Assistance Listings title and number, Federal award 
identification number and year, and name of the Federal agency. The financial management 
system must provide for records that identify adequately the source and application of funds for

27 
Federally funded activities. These records must contain information pertaining to Federal 
awards, authorizations, financial obligations, unobligated balances, assets, expenditures, income, 
and interest and be supported by source documentation (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(3)).  
 
Analysis: The finding’s focus is on the accounting and reporting requirements of the VR 
program, necessary to ensure that supporting documentation, as required by applicable program 
regulations, EDGAR, and the Uniform Guidance, is sufficient to ensure all VR program 
requirements are met, including Federal reporting requirements, program income, non-Federal 
share (match), and MOE required for States to access, draw down, and disburse Federal award 
funds, as well as determine the extent to which VR awards qualify for carryover in accordance 
with Section 19 of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.64. 
 
The Federal financial reports (e.g., RSA-17) are prepared and verified by the DSU; however, 
they are signed by the designated State agency (DSA).  
 
RSA noted outdated policies and procedures that require revisions. For example, the fiscal 
policies and procedures provided to RSA were last updated in 2016 and are not consistent with 
current Federal requirements.

Finding 3.2 Insufficient Contract Administration and Monitoring 
 
Issue: Does DVR meet requirements for determining the rates of payment for services and 
ensuring that contract payments are reasonable in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.50(c). 
Additionally, whether DVR maintains effective internal controls regarding contract management 
and oversight sufficient to meet the requirements of 2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a) and of Section 4 of 
DVR’s Monitoring Procedures for Contracts, Grants, and Agreement.  
 
Requirements: Federal award recipients are required to establish and maintain written policies 
to govern the rates of payment for all purchased vocational rehabilitation services (34 C.F.R. § 
361.50(c)(1)). These policies outline the process the non-Federal entity completes to ensure the 
rates of payment for VR services are allowable, reasonable, and allocable to the award, in 
accordance with Uniform Guidance Cost Principles (2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403, 200.404, and 
200.405). 
 
Federal regulations state that the non-Federal entity is responsible for oversight of the operations 
of the Federal award supported activities (2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a)). The non-Federal entity must 
monitor its contracted activities under Federal awards to ensure compliance with applicable 
Federal requirements and that performance expectations are achieved. DVR is required to 
establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides reasonable 
assurance that DVR is managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award.

32 
DVR Monitoring Procedures for Contracts, Grants, and Agreements Policy (contract policy) 
states that the DVR Contract Manager conducts regular monitoring of contracts and contractors 
to ensure that funds are used to maximize program outcomes, and in a manner that is compliant 
with Federal and State regulations. The monitoring process is conducted throughout the term of 
the contract with monitoring activities taking place at quarterly, bi-annual, and annual intervals. 
 
Analysis: RSA requested procurement policies used by the State to determine rates of payment 
and whether rates are reasonable. DVR provided a Payment Terms and Schedule worksheet; 
however, the contracts are structured as fee-for-service due to the implementation of financial 
consequences for missed milestones. DVR was unable to sufficiently explain its fee-for-service 
methodology and the contracts provided for the review are missing details that directly impact 
the deliverables and financial consequences. For example, agreements do not state the number of 
staff to be contracted yet the number of staff vacancies is factored into a performance measure. 
Payments to contractors are provided monthly at a fixed rate with the financial consequences 
assessed in the final month of the contract. The amount paid to providers must be directly 
proportional to the benefit received by the VR program.  
 
While some contracts contain reductions due to certain contract criteria not being met, DVR is 
able to waive the reductions without any consequence to the contractor. It is unclear whether 
DVR has ever imposed reductions based on contractors not meeting the specified criteria. 
Without adjusting the amount paid under the contracts to reflect actual contractor expenditures 
for staff and services provided, DVR is unable to ensure that the costs are proportional to the 
benefit received by the program.  
 
DVR provided a monitoring plan template that included outdated requirements and did not 
properly implement its monitoring internal controls. DVR Monitoring Procedures for Contracts, 
Grants, and Agreements Policy suggests monitoring contracts either quarterly, biannually, or 
annually. However, based on the documents provided by DVR and discussions held on-site, 
DVR has not reviewed its largest contractor since FFY 2022. Nonetheless, the contract continues 
to be automatically renewed despite the absence of regular monitoring.  
 
RSA requested copies of monitoring reports for all contracts under review, but DVR did not 
provide the reports. DVR only provided monitoring outcome letters sent to contractors and failed 
to provide the documents used to conduct monitoring reviews.

satisfy the following Federal requirements:

compliance with the requirements cited in 2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a) and of Section 4 of 
DVR’s contract policy. Consequently, RSA has determined that the agency has not 
complied with Federal and State monitoring requirements.

post-service assessments completed by eligible and potentially eligible students with disabilities. 
DVR imposes financial consequences on the contractor if the pre- and post-service assessments 
are not completed (Contract 19-233 between the Florida Department of Education and Abilities, 
Inc. of Florida, Attachment A, Section IV Financial Consequences). Contractors incur additional 
financial consequences if certain forms are not submitted to DVR within prescribed timeframes. 
The contract appears to permit DVR to assess the provider consequences based on whether a 
student completes a pre- or post-service assessment. This introduces the potential for the 
provider to focus on these assessments rather than the delivery of pre-employment transition 
services. RSA has determined that an assessment instrument does not align with the direct 
service provision of a distinct pre-employment transition service. There is no requirement to 
assess a student’s knowledge of a topic or comprehension of materials when receiving any pre-
employment transition service, thus it should not be a prescribed activity for all students. It also 
may not be needed by an eligible student with an IPE to receive pre-employment transition 
services. Requiring participation in assessments through pre- and post-service tools has the 
potential to force providers to deliver an activity that is not required under pre-employment 
transition services in order to avoid financial consequences.  
 
Conclusion: Imposing financial consequences for the failure to complete assessments by 
potentially eligible and eligible students with disabilities effectively assigns a cost to those

35 
services, which cannot be paid with the reserved funds, unless determined necessary and 
identified on the IPE for only eligible students. Payments for unallowable activities (i.e., required 
pre- and post-service assessments for potentially eligible and eligible students) through the 
contract from the reserved funds for pre-employment transition services are unallowable. 
Therefore, DVR is not complying fully with the requirements under Section 113 of the 
Rehabilitation Act and the implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 361.48(a) and the 
requirements for the reservation of funds for the provision of pre-employment transition services.  
 
Corrective Action 3.3 RSA requires that DVR—  
 
3.3.1 On or before 90 calendar days following the issuance of the final monitoring report, 
provide written assurances, signed by the State agency director, to RSA that it has ceased 
requiring pre- and post-service assessments in the provision of pre-employment transition 
services and removed the requirement for financial consequences related to students’ 
completion of pre- and post-service assessments; 
3.3.2. Determine the amount of unallowable pre-employment transition service expenditures  
and adjust the FFYs 2022, 2023, and 2024 RSA-17 financial reports. Once the amounts 
are agreed to by RSA, revise the final financial reports for those years to remove the 
unallowable expenditures. This should be completed on or before 180 calendar days 
following the issuance of the final monitoring report.; and 
3.3.3  Provide written assurances, signed by the State agency director, to RSA that it has  
developed or revised internal controls related to the provision of pre-employment 
transition services to prevent the agency from entering into contracts that include 
unallowable services. This should be completed on or before 270 calendar days following 
the issuance of the final monitoring report. 
 
VR Agency Response: Florida VR disagrees with this finding and the above corrective actions. 
As RSA cited 2 C.F.R. section 200.303, the responsibility of ensuring internal controls upon the 
agency is to ensure that the agency has reasonable assurance for the expenditure of the funds. 
Additionally, RSA cited on page 25 that the state of Florida did not meet the pre-employment 
transition expenditure requirements of 15% in FFY 2020 (12.3 percent) or FFY 2021 (14.9). 
However, in this finding and corrective action, RSA cites VR, and not the State as a whole. VR 
met the 15 percent pre-ETS expenditure requirement in FFY 2017-FFY 2020, and VR exceeded 
the 15 percent requirement in FFY 2022 and FFY 2023, which included the requirement of pre- 
and post-assessment.  
 
RSA notes that the requirement of a pre- and post- assessment has the “potential” for the 
provider to focus on the assessment rather than the delivery of the services and the “potential” to 
force providers to deliver an activity that is not required under pre-employment transition 
services. If the hypothetical were true, that the requirement of a pre- and post- assessment diverts 
providers' focus away from service delivery, then VR should have seen at least some dissonance 
regarding pre-employment transition services expenditures and a decrease in applications for 
service providers. Career Camps and the requirement of pre- and post- assessments were 
established in the summer of 2019 and as noted above, outside of one FFY, VR met or exceeded 
the 15 percent requirement. VR met the requirement within two FFY after the 15 percent

36 
requirement was imposed, despite little to no guidance on how to meet the 15 percent 
requirement from RSA.  
 
Although RSA notes the following: “There is no requirement to assess a student’s knowledge of 
a topic or comprehension of materials when receiving any pre-employment transition service, 
thus it should not be a prescribed activity for all students.” The pre- and post- assessments are 
effectively assisting both the student, provider, and VR counselor in ensuring that the student has 
appropriate growth in the appropriate pre-employment transition services, which is a method of 
internal controls to ensure that appropriate dollars are being spent on the services. Additionally, 
in reviewing these pre- and post- assessments, it assists VR in monitoring the contracted 
provider.  
 
A point of clarification that the Bureau of Vendor and Contracted Services, provided during the 
monitoring was that the agency would not prohibit a provider from receiving funds for services if 
the pre- and post- assessment could not be obtained. However, VR agrees that policies can be 
revised to ensure that information is included that states that if providers can show three 
documented attempts at receiving the post-assessment with no success, that the payment will not 
be delayed, and a financial consequence will not be given. Finally, the recommendation listed 
that mentions the issue of the pre- and post- assessment is duplicative and provides no additional 
information that is beneficial to the finding.  
 
RSA Response: Payments for pre- and post-assessments from pre-employment transition 
services for potentially eligible students with disabilities is not aligned with a required pre-
employment transition services and is an unallowable expense as defined in 34 C.F.R. § 
361.48(a). RSA has determined that an assessment instrument does not align with the direct 
service provision of a distinct pre-employment transition service for a potentially eligible student 
with a disability, and is a service that, while used to assess general service needs or progress, is 
not necessarily needed by an eligible student with an IPE to participate in the receipt of pre-
employment transition services. Therefore, DVR is not complying fully with the requirements 
under Section 113 of the Rehabilitation Act and the implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 
361.48(a) in its provision of pre-employment transition services to students with disabilities and 
the requirements for the reservation of funds for pre-employment transition services and the 
allowable expenditures. 
 
Eligible students with disabilities, that is, those students who have applied and been determined 
eligible for the VR program, are able to receive any VR services, including assessment services, 
necessary to assist them in achieving their employment outcome, so long as those services are 
identified on their IPEs. Therefore, DVR may use the funds reserved for the provision of pre-
employment transition services, in accordance with section 110(d)(1) of the Rehabilitation Act to 
pay for certain assessment costs when those assessment services are provided to eligible students 
with disabilities receiving pre-employment transition services and who need such services to 
receive pre-employment transition services or other VR services under an approved IPE. To the 
extent that a portion of the costs incurred for the additional VR services fall outside the nature, 
scope, and purpose of pre-employment transition services, DVR must pay that portion with other 
VR program funds. Thus, RSA is unable to fully determine the extent of allowable funds DVR

37 
used for the 15 percent reserve requirement for the provision of pre-employment transition 
services. 
 
RSA appreciates DVR’s response and will work with the agency through its corrective action 
plan to ensure it maintains effective internal controls regarding contract management and 
oversight.
