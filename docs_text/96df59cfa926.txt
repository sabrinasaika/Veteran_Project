I. Facts
A. The Parties
James E. Cyrus is a licensed blind vendor under the Randolph Sheppard Act. He has operated vending facilities on the Health Science Campus of the University of Toledo (UT) since 1993, operating under permanent Facility #304. He operates Facility #304 pursuant to an Operator’s Agreement with the Opportunities for Ohioans with Disabilities (OOD) Bureau of Services for the Visually Impaired (BSVI).
BSVI is the State Licensing Agency (SLA) designated by the Rehabilitation Services Commission (RSC) of the United States Department of Education (DOE) to operate Ohio’s Business Enterprise (BE) Program for blind and visually impaired citizens. Responsibility for the BE Program is delegated by OOD to BSVI. Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21-01(E). As such, BSVI is responsible for complying with the obligations established under the Randolph Sheppard Act (RSA) as well as the parallel provisions of Ohio’s Mini Randolph Sheppard Act (mini-RSA), thereby expanding priority for blind vendors to state properties. BSVI enters into Vending Services Agreements with federal and state landowners called Bureau-Grantor Agreements (BGAs). These agreements authorize BSVI to operate vending services through a blind vendor who is licensed pursuant to the RSA.
B. The Randolph Sheppard Act
In 1936, the United States Congress enacted the RSA “to provide blind persons with remunerative employment, enlarge the economic opportunities of the blind, and stimulate the blind to greater efforts in striving to make themselves self-supporting.” 20 U.S.C. 107(a). The RSA created a priority for licensed blind vendors to operate vending services on federal properties. 20 U.S.C. § 107(b). The RSA divides responsibility for the blind vendor program between state and federal agencies. At the federal level, the Secretary of the DOE is responsible for implementing and enforcing the RSA’s provisions, and for designating SLAs. At the state level, the SLAs implement the program and issue licenses to blind and visually impaired persons, allowing them to operate vending facilities on federal property. OOD is Ohio’s SLA, and administers the program through BSVI, a division of OOD. Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21-01(E).
While the RSA creates a priority for licensed blind vendors to operate vending facilities on federal property, it does not require states to establish vending facilities on state property. The RSA, and its implementing regulations under 34 C.F.R. Part 395, permits states, at their discretion, to expand the program to non-federal properties through state law. The RSA requires SLAs to promulgate regulations consistent with the operation of the program in accordance with state law, and that have been approved by the Secretary of Education. These administrative rules are found in Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21. One of these rules requires BSVI to “do everything necessary and proper to carry out sections 3304.29 to 3304.34 of the Revised Code.”  ORC 3304.29(D).
Ohio has expanded the federal program to state “governmental property” through the mini-RSA. R.C. §§ 3304.28, et seq. This mini-RSA defines state “governmental property” as including all property owned, leased, or rented by the state or any board, commission, department, division, or other unit or agency thereof, with a few limited exceptions. See R.C. § 3304.28(C).  Mr. Cyrus’s Facility #304, at issue here, on the UT’s Health Science Campus, is a state facility governed by the mini-RSA.
The mini-RSA requires persons in charge of governmental properties to consult with the Director of BSVI to determine if the property would be suitable for a vending facility. R.C. § 3304.30. For state universities, the decision to establish a new vending facility must be made jointly by BSVI and the college or university. Id. If a decision is made that a vending facility is suitable, BSVI provides each facility with equipment and an adequate initial stock of items to be sold by the blind licensee. Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21-01(R), 3304:1-21-05(E). The blind licensee becomes a business owner and has full authority to operate the vending facility. See id. at 3304:1-21-04.
The RSA provides a process for blind licensees to resolve disputes regarding the vending program. If a dispute arises, a blind licensee dissatisfied with any action arising from the operation or administration of the vending facility program may grieve to the SLA and is entitled to a full evidentiary hearing. See 20 U.S.C. §§ 107b(6), 107d-1(a); Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21-14. A licensee dissatisfied with the results of this hearing may file a complaint with the Secretary of Education, who then will convene a panel to arbitrate the dispute. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-1(a). A party dissatisfied with the decision of the arbitration panel may then appeal pursuant to the Administrative Procedure Act. Id. at 107d-2(a).
C. The Complaints
Mr. Cyrus has filed four Complaints with the Secretary of Education seeking arbitration of disputes against BSVI. These Complaints will be referred to as Complaints #1-4, in chronological order of filing. Each complaint began with a grievance filed with BSVI, went to a hearing (either evidentiary or by motion) that resulted in a report and recommendation denying Cyrus relief that was adopted by OOD over objection by Mr. Cyrus, and now has been sent to Arbitration through the Secretary of Education.
1. Complaint #1, filed February 10, 2016 – 2015-02BE
Complaint #1 arises from a grievance filed with BSVI on November 12, 2014. This grievance alleged that BSVI failed to advocate on behalf of Mr. Cyrus’s efforts to expand his operations beyond his existing vending machines on the UT Health Science Campus. Mr. Cyrus sought to expand (1) geographically to the UT Main Campus, and (2) operationally to cafeterias, kiosks, ATM machines, free coffee services, gift shops, beverage pouring rights, and a privately owned Radisson hotel.
An administrative hearing was held on June 8, July 23, and August 11, 2015. Hearing Officer Linda Mosbacher issued a decision on November 22, 2015. This decision, including its findings of fact and conclusions of law, is incorporated by reference into this Award as Attachment 1. Hearing Officer Mosbacher found:
As a matter of law, decisions to establish new vending facilities on Ohio universities must be made jointly by BSVI and the college or university.
“UT is not interested in expanding its agreement with BSVI-BE beyond the current vending of food, food products, candy, snacks and beverages at UT’s Health Science Campus.”
Should vending facilities at the UT Main Campus later become available to blind vendors, the location would be offered to all such vendors, and not reserved for Mr. Cyrus.
Mr. Cyrus’s earnings indicated BSVI had not failed to advocate on Mr. Cyrus’s behalf.
Mr. Cyrus had “not proven any action … or inaction” by BSVI against him.
Through counsel Mr. Cyrus filed objections. On December 14, 2015, BSVI issued an Adjudication Order adopting Hearing Officer Mosbacher’s Decision. Mr. Cyrus then filed Complaint #1 with the Secretary of Education.
2. Complaint #2, filed August 24, 2016 – 2015-04BE
Complaint #2 arises from a grievance filed with BSVI on September 30, 2015. This grievance alleged that BSVI failed to advocate on behalf of Mr. Cyrus’s efforts to secure the “pouring rights” contract – then held by Coca-Cola – to supply all beverages on UT’s Main Campus.
BSVI moved to dismiss the grievance on the ground that the parties and issues in this grievance were identical to the parties and issues in the grievance described above, and therefore were barred by res judicata. The parties submitted written briefs and made oral arguments. Hearing Officer David G. Hasselback (one of the undersigned Arbitrators on this Award) issued a decision on April 7, 2016. This decision, including its findings of fact and conclusions of law, is incorporated by reference into this Award as Attachment 2. Hearing Officer Hasselback found:
The same parties were involved in both grievances.
The issues in both grievances were “virtually the same”.
“Since a final adjudication has been made in the earlier grievance (15-02BE) the doctrine of res judicata or collateral estoppel is applicable to the second grievance (15-04BE) and bars litigation of those issues in the second grievance.”
Mr. Cyrus’s grievance regarding the UT “pouring rights contract” should be denied.
On April 7, 2016, BSVI issued an Adjudication Order adopting Hearing Officer Hasselback’s Decision. Mr. Cyrus then filed Complaint #2 with the Secretary of Education.
3. Complaint #3, filed August 24, 2016 – 2015-05BE
Complaint #3 arises from an initial grievance filed with BSVI on April 29, 2014, and an amendment of that grievance filed on May 21, 2014. This grievance alleged that BSVI failed to advocate on behalf of Mr. Cyrus regarding UT’s “Healthy Beverage Policy” which UT implemented for the Health Science Campus effective January 1, 2014. This Policy forbade all or nearly all vendors on the Health Science Campus from selling sugared beverages. Mr. Cyrus alleged that UT failed to enforce this Policy against other vendors, that this inequitable enforcement negatively affected his sales, and that BSVI was at fault for failing to force UT to enforce the Policy against other vendors.
An administrative hearing was held on May 25-26, 2016. Hearing Officer David G. Hasselback (one of the undersigned Arbitrators on this Award, and the author of the administrative decision in 2015-04BE described above) issued a decision on June 10, 2016. This Policy forbade all or nearly all vendors on the Health Science Campus from selling sugared beverages. Mr. Cyrus alleged that UT failed to enforce this Policy against other vendors, that this inequitable enforcement negatively affected his sales, and that BSVI was at fault for failing to timely assert that the inequitable enforcement violated the BSVI-UT BOA.
“[T]here is ample evidence that BE-BSVI did advocate for Mr. Cyrus in reference to the Healthy Beverage Policy. Representatives of BE-BSVI approached UT regarding not only the policy itself but problems that Mr. Cyrus brought to the attention of BE-BSVI.”
“As a direct result of the efforts of BE-BSVI the eventual result was that UT exempted Mr. Cyrus and his operation … from that policy. In other words, as a direct result of actions by BE-BSVI Mr. Cyrus eventually was and is free to sell beverages that contain more sugar.”
“While Mr. Cyrus is of the opinion that BE-BSVI did not advocate on his behalf in regards to this issue either quickly enough or strenuously enough … I disagree. Examples of efforts to assist Mr. Cyrus in regards to the Health Beverage Policy include … communications between BE-BSVI and UT regarding (1) the policy and the Vending Agreement; and (2) The effect of the policy upon Mr. Cyrus’s operations; and (3) BE-BSVI bringing to UT’s attention concerns expressed by Mr. Cyrus that UT was not being uniform in enforcement of the policy; and (4) Placing new equipment (Bev Max Machines) in place to better market the reduced or sugar free products.”
BSVI’s actions met all its statutory obligations.
Mr. Cyrus’s grievance regarding the UT Healthy Beverage Policy should be denied.
Through counsel Mr. Cyrus filed objections. On July 14, 2016, BSVI issued an Adjudication Order adopting Hearing Officer Hasselback’s Decision. Mr. Cyrus then filed Complaint #3 with the Secretary of Education.

III. Analysis and Conclusions of Law
Three issues are before this Arbitration Panel. The first is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligation to Mr. Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and Mini-RSA, in his efforts to secure vending rights (including pouring rights, food-service contracts, the right to install ATM machine, et cetera), at the UT Main Campus. The second is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligations to Mr. Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and Mini-RSA, regarding UT’s alleged selective enforcement of its “Healthy Beverage Policy”. The third is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligations to Mr. Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and Mini-RSA, when Mr. Cyrus decided to close the Market Café.
All three issues, as characterized by the parties, raise the threshold issue of whether the RSA and Mini-RSA impose on BSVI a “duty to advocate” on behalf of licensed blind vendors such as Mr. Cyrus. Mr. Cyrus argues vigorously in both his Response to Motion for Summary Judgment and his Post-Hearing Brief that such a duty is implied by statutory provisions requiring BSVI to administer the RSA and Mini-RSA consistent with their purpose of promoting the economic interests of blind licensed vendors. The statutory basis for his argument seems to be ORC 3304.29(D), which charges BSVI with doing “everything necessary and proper to carry out sections 3304.29 to 3304.34 of the Revised Code.” BSVI argues equally vigorously, in both its Motion for Summary Judgment and its Post-Hearing Brief, that neither the RSA nor the Mini-RSA imposes any such duty. This Arbitration Panel need not address the issue of whether a “duty to advocate” exists because we find that, under any conceivable definition of such a duty, BSVI has consistently and vigorously advocated for Mr. Cyrus.
A.   BSVI’s Alleged Failure to Secure for Mr. Cyrus UT Main Campus Pouring Rights, Food-
Service Contracts, ATMs, etc.
Mr. Cyrus argues that BSVI has failed to advocate on behalf of his efforts to expand his operations beyond his existing vending machines on the UT Health Science Campus. Mr. Cyrus wants to force BSVI to assert its statutory “right of priority” over UT Main Campus “pouring rights” for beverages, food services contracts, ATM machines, free coffee services, gift shops, coin-op laundry machines, and copy machines, including food and beverage vending through counter service, cafeterias, and athletic concessions. Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 14, 20. All these vending operations currently are operated by private contractors. Id. at 15. These contractors pay UT millions of dollars annually – in the form of cash, student scholarships, and campus infrastructure such as scoreboards – for the privilege of exclusive access to UT students, faculty, and guests. Id. at 19 ($450,000 per year plus 50% gross revenue for pouring rights contract), 27 ($750,000 for ATM contract); Transcript at 354, 387-88.
Mr. Cyrus’s argument fails for four independent reasons. First, BSVI is forbidden by statute and applicable caselaw from spending money on royalties, advertising, licensing, student scholarships, or campus infrastructure – all of which are integral parts of the vending contracts Mr. Cyrus wants BSVI to assert control over. See Transcript at 353.  The RSA Section 107b(3) provides that RSA funds may be used only for the following purposes:
(A) maintenance and replacement of equipment;
(B) the purchase of new equipment;
(C) management services;
(D) assuring a fair minimum return to operators of vending facilities; and
(E) retirement or pension funds, health insurance contributions, and provision for paid sick leave and vacation time, [under certain circumstances].
20 U.S.C. § 107b(3). The Ohio Mini-RSA, in turn, provides that “No funds derived from the federal government or from blind licensees . . . shall be spent for purposes other than those set forth in [the Randolph-Sheppard Vending Stand Act]”). R.C. 3304.35. In addition, the U.S. Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals has held that, pursuant to the RSA, BSVI cannot pay commissions or cause operators to pay commissions because RSA funds can only be used for “enumerated purposes.” See Ohio v. U.S. Dept. of Educ., 986 F.3d 618, 625 (6th Cir. 2021).
Mr. Cyrus does not own a beverage production company. He does not own a bank, or a food-service provider, or a chain of restaurants, or a coin-operated laundry company. Were BSVI to secure for him the pouring rights or food-service contracts or ATM rights or laundry-machine rights he demands, he (through BSVI) necessarily would have to contract with companies such as Coca-Cola or Bank of America or Aramark to provide the appropriate products and services. Yet BSVI is statutorily prohibited from entering into such contracts. And Mr. Cyrus would serve no purpose other than a rent-extracting intermediary between UT and the vendors with which it currently contracts.
Mr. Cyrus has no answer to this. His Post-Hearing Brief makes no mention of § 107b(3). This reason alone warrants rejection of his argument that BSVI should assert control over the myriad vending opportunities to which Mr. Cyrus demands access.
The second reason Mr. Cyrus’s argument fails is that BSVI does not have a unilateral “right of priority” with which it can force UT to cede its vending contracts. R.C. 3304.30 states: “In the case of a . . . state-affiliated college or university, the decision to establish a suitable vending facility shall be made jointly by [BSVI] and proper administrative authorities of the state or state-affiliated college or university.” [Emphasis added.] Mr. Cyrus acknowledges that Ohio universities such as UT make millions of dollars each year from existing vending contracts. Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 19, 27. It therefore should come as no surprise that such universities are reluctant to unilaterally forgo these revenue streams, especially if the only thing they would get in return is a rent-extracting intermediary between them and the vendors with which they currently contract. Consistent with this, Hearing Officer Mosbacher specifically found in 2015-02BE that “UT is not interested in expanding its agreement with BSVI-BE beyond the current vending of food, food products, candy, snacks and beverages at UT’s Health Science Campus.”
Mr. Cyrus argues that BSVI has a statutory duty to seek and secure the vending facilities on the UT Main Campus, and asserts that BSVI has “an unqualified right of priority” to these facilities. Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 12. That is incorrect as a matter of law. The statutory requirement that such decisions be made jointly by universities and BSVI gives universities an effective veto. BSVI cannot be said to have violated a statutory “obligation” to secure these contract rights if it lacks the statutory ability to do so.
Mr. Cyrus cites in response Ohio Attorney General Opinion 2014-008 (March 4, 2014), at 5 (“a state or state-affiliated college or university may not establish vending facilities of any kind, whether in-house or through private contract, if there is no joint decision regarding the establishment of a suitable vending facility by the college or university and the Director of BSVI.”), and at 7 (“R.C. 3304.30 gives only one criterion to be considered in determining whether a suitable vending facility will be established: whether sufficient numbers of persons will be using such property to support a suitable vending facility.  The inquiry ends there.”). This Opinion, however, does not change the analysis, for two reasons. First, it does not change the statutory requirement that “the decision to establish a suitable vending facility shall be made jointly…”. Nothing in the Opinion authorizes or empowers BSVI to compel state universities to cede their vending contracts. Second, to the extent that the Opinion creates or recognizes any sort of legal duty, it refers only to legal duties owed by state universities, not by BSVI. All the questions posed in the Opinion are directed to such universities, not to BSVI. As such, Mr. Cyrus’s complaint is misdirected toward BSVI, and instead should be directed to UT.
The third reason Mr. Cyrus’s argument fails is that BSVI has advocated for Mr. Cyrus to obtain vending rights on the Main Campus. As discussed above, BSVI lacks the statutory authority to acquire the pouring rights, food-service contracts, ATM contracts, etc., that Mr.  Cyrus covets. BSVI does have the statutory authority to acquire “follow-on” pouring rights – i.e., the right of a blind vendor to sell beverages and operate vending machines notwithstanding the otherwise exclusive right of the pouring-rights vendor to do so. Transcript at 353-54, 377, 386. BSVI negotiated precisely such a follow-on exemption for Mr. Cyrus, allowing him to sell beverages on the Main Campus notwithstanding an existing exclusive pouring-rights contract. Id. at 166, 389-91. Better yet, BSVI negotiated for the vendor with the pouring-rights contract to pay for Mr. Cyrus’s vending machines. Id. at 377.
Mr. Cyrus seems to want BSVI to “advocate” for him in the legal sense of suing UT to force it to cede its Main Campus vending contracts. Nothing in the statutes, administrative regulations, or case law requires BSVI to do so. Moreover, BSVI has made the deliberate and reasonable decision that any such litigation would be counterproductive. See BSVI’s Post-Hearing Brief at 32-34. Such suits almost certainly would be unsuccessful given the statutory requirement that decisions to establish vending facilities be made jointly. Moreover, given that same requirement, it is unlikely that universities would voluntarily acquiesce to such decisions if BSVI develops a reputation for suing them. Transcript at 394. Such suits also could lead Ohio universities to lobby for legislative changes to exempt state universities from the Mini-RSA entirely, to the detriment of Mr. Cyrus and all Ohio blind vendors. Id. at 385-86. BSVI therefore is reasonably unwilling to put opportunities for all blind vendors at universities at risk for the extraordinarily low probability of further expanding Mr.  Cyrus’s access to the Main Campus.
The fourth reason Mr. Cyrus’s argument fails is that even if BSVI were to obtain one or more contracts to provide vending on the Main Campus, such contracts would not necessarily be awarded to Mr. Cyrus. BSVI’s BGA with UT defines the property included in the Facility, and therefore where a blind vendor can operate. It provides that Mr. Cyrus’s facility – Facility 304 – includes the Health Science Campus only. See BGA ¶ B, 15-02BE State’s Exhibit 6. Expanding BSVI’s reach to the Main Campus would require a new BGA (and UT’s acquiescence). Ohio Administrative Code 3304:1-21 describes how such facilities are awarded. BSVI may place a temporary vendor at the facility while it makes an announcement to allow blind vendors to apply to be assigned as the operator. Id. at 3304:1-21-03(A) and (B). A selection committee then reviews the applications and decides which applicant will be awarded the site based upon a scoring system. Id. at 3304:1-21-03(G).
Mr. Cyrus therefore has no basis for asserting that he has been harmed by any alleged failure of BSVI to vigorously pursue vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus. His standing even to assert this issue is questionable.
B.   BSVI’s Alleged Failure to Advocate for Mr. Cyrus Regarding UT’s “Healthy Beverage Policy”.
As described above, BSVI signs Bureau-Grantor Agreements (BGAs) with state landowners, including universities. These BGAs authorize BSVI to operate on-site vending services through a blind operator. BSVI’s BGA with UT for the Health Science Center provides that the operator placed by BSVI will offer “mutually agreed products.” BGA at ¶1(a); Transcript at 126. Thus, neither BSVI nor an operator can insist on selling alcohol or cigarettes in the Hospital, no matter how profitable such sales might be.
When Mr. Cyrus became BSVI’s blind operator for the UT Health Science Center, he signed a Bureau-Operator Agreement (BOA) with BSVI. One term of that BOA is that Mr. Cyrus must comply with the BGA between BSVI and UT. BOA at ¶ 1.2.1. Thus, according to the agreement Mr. Cyrus signed, he can sell only products that are “mutually agreed” by UT and BSVI.
In 2013, the Health Science Center of the University of Toledo announced that it would initiate a Healthy [Sugar Free] Beverage Policy throughout the Health Science Campus, effective January 1, 2014.  BSVI Exhibit I at 144; Transcript 15-05BE, Vol. III, pp. 90-91 (p.63 of PDF). By its terms, this Policy applied throughout the Health Science Center to “the cafeteria, patient menu, catering menu, and any other independent contracted vendors [including Mr. Cyrus] that supply beverages to Health Science Campus.” Id. It basically forbade sugar-sweetened beverages. Id.
At the time, roughly 75% of Mr. Cyrus’s beverages were sugared. After the Policy went into effect, his sales dropped 40%. He complained vigorously and often both to UT and to BSVI. See Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief at 35-37. He particularly complained that UT was selectively enforcing the Policy and that a privately owned coffee and sandwich kiosk was continuing to sell sugared beverages even as he was prohibited from doing so.
On April 18, 2016, in response to an email from BSVI Area Manager Victoria Smith, UT exempted Mr. Cyrus from the Policy and permitted him to sell sugared beverages in all his vending machines. 15-05BE State’s Exhibit 8 at 113. UT withdrew the Policy later in 2016 and the Policy no longer exists. Transcript at 144, 255. Mr. Cyrus believes that during the period the Policy applied to him, it caused him to lose sales of approximately $111,000. Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 37.
Mr. Cyrus argues that BSVI “refused to enforce the BGA and advocate on [his] behalf.” Id. at 34. This argument, however, fails for five independent reasons.
First, though Mr. Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief alleges that BSVI refused to enforce its BGA with UT, it never identifies which provision in the BGA Mr. Cyrus accuses BSVI of refusing to enforce. In fact, the Panel can find nothing in any part of the record indicating which provision of the BGA Mr. Cyrus believes BSVI refused to enforce. Instead, it appears that precisely the opposite is true – that BSVI correctly recognized that the UT BGA gives UT the right to restrict the products that Mr. Cyrus can sell, and that Mr. Cyrus wanted to disregard that provision of the contract as well as his own contractual promise to comply with the BGA.
Second, BSVI has no legal or contractual right to tell UT what it can or cannot permit to be sold on its campus. BSVI could not have compelled UT to permit the sale of sugared drinks any more than it could have compelled UT to permit the sale of cigarettes or alcohol. Perhaps this is why both Mr. Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief and the record as a whole are devoid of what, specifically, he wanted BSVI to do, other than to “advocate on his behalf” to make the Policy go away. But what, specifically, does this mean? Send more emails complaining about the Policy? As described below, BSVI sent plenty of those. Sue UT? BSVI would lose on a 12(b)(6) or summary judgment motion because the BGA gave UT the contractual right to restrict the sale of products on its campus, and UT would never have signed a BGA without such a provision. And in addition to gaining nothing, such a suit would poison the long-term relationship between BSVI and UT.
Third, assuming Mr. Cyrus is correct about UT having selectively enforced the Policy, BSVI had no legal way of compelling UT to enforce the Policy against other vendors. Again, the record is devoid of precisely what Mr. Cyrus wanted BSVI to do, and on what authority BSVI could have done it. Nothing in the BGA gives BSVI the authority to compel UT to enforce the Policy against other vendors. Quite the opposite. As Mr. Cyrus acknowledged at the Arbitration hearing, neither the BGA nor the BOA provided that the “mutually agreed products” had to be the same products offered by other campus vendors. Transcript at 126. Thus, UT had a contractual right to forbid Mr. Cyrus from selling sugared beverages even if it sold sugar-laden soft drinks in its own cafeteria or allowed the coffee kiosk down the hall to sell such drinks.
Fourth, despite Mr. Cyrus’s protestations to the contrary, the record demonstrates that BSVI advocated vigorously on his behalf regarding the Policy. See, e.g., BSVI Exhibit I at 139, 140-43, 145-48; 15-05BE BSVI Exhibit 8 at 114-19, 141-431, 152, 154-65; Cyrus Exhibit C-47, Part 4 at 8-9; Cyrus Ex. C-53, Part 4 at 18; Cyrus Exhibit C-62, Part 4 at 29.
The basis of Mr. Cyrus’s claim that UT selectively enforced the Policy is that a nearby kiosk was selling sugared frappuccinos. Transcript at 140-43 (Cyrus). Mr. Cyrus emailed BSVI Area Manager Victoria Smith on the afternoon of May 20, 2014, to complain that a coffee kiosk was selling sugared beverages. See 15-05BE BSVI Exhibit 8 at 157-58. Before 10:00 the next morning, Ms. Smith had obtained a commitment from UT to investigate the matter and enforce the Policy equitably – apparently, UT had simply forgotten to tell the owner of that kiosk about the Policy. Id. Even more significantly, on April 18, 2016, in response to an email from Ms. Smith, UT exempted Mr. Cyrus from the Policy and permitted him to sell sugared beverages in all his vending machines. 15-05BE State’s Exhibit 8 at 113.
Hearing Officer Hasselback specifically found in 15-05BE that BSVI advocated for Mr. Cyrus in reference to the Healthy Beverage Policy, that BSVI’s advocacy resulted in Mr. Cyrus receiving an exemption to that Policy unique to him, and that BSVI met its statutory obligations. Mr. Cyrus may believe that his exemption did not come fast enough, but both the Policy itself and UT’s enforcement of the Policy were at all times beyond BSVI’s control. Hearing Officer Hasselback’s findings in 15-05BE are fully supported by the evidence in the Arbitration record.
Fifth, Mr. Cyrus’s complaints concerning the Market Café became moot following (1) the exemption from the Policy, described above, that Mr. Cyrus received on April 18, 2016, and (2) the fact that the entire Policy was rescinded in late 2016. Because the parties have stipulated that no damages will be awarded in this Arbitration, there is nothing left regarding the Market Café that the Panel can do for Mr. Cyrus.
C.   BSVI’s Alleged Failure to Advocate for Mr. Cyrus Regarding the “Market Café”
In late 2016, BSVI secured a new site for the RSA by signing a BGA with UT for a coffee-and-sandwich kiosk, known as the “Market Café”, in the lobby of the hospital at the Health Science Center. BSVI’s BGA with UT:
Specified that the hours of operation would be 6:15 a.m. to 8:30 p.m. Monday through Friday, but could be adjusted upon written request.
Required the parties to take reasonable measures to ensure that no unplanned interruptions to the hours of operation occurred.
Contained the following force majeure provision:
Neither party shall in be default pursuant to the terms of this Agreement or liable to the other party for any failure to perform or for delay in performance with respect to any obligations under or pursuant to the terms thereof, except obligations to make payment of amounts due and owing through the dale of cession of service, due to acts of God, acts of public authorities, wars, insurrections, riots, civil disturbances, strikes, lock-outs or labor disturbances, fires, floods, explosions, earthquakes, breakage or accidents to equipment, or failures or delays of suppliers or carriers, or any other cause, whether the kind enumerated in this section or otherwise, not reasonably within the control of the party claiming inability to perform. Each party agrees to endeavor to resume its performance under this Agreement if such performance is interrupted by reason of the force majeure.
BSVI awarded that site to Mr. Cyrus as part of his pre-existing BOA for Facility #304. That BOA required Mr. Cyrus to operate in compliance with any existing BGA. See 20-23BE Cyrus Exhibit 1.
Over the next two years, BSVI spent more than $350,000 to build, equip, and stock the Market Café. It opened in fall 2018.
In March 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic hit. Access to the Health Science Center hospital was severely restricted, many staff began working from home, and student instruction went online. Unsurprisingly, the Market Café’s customer base plummeted.
Throughout March and April, Mr. Cyrus communicated regularly with UT Food Services Director Joshua Krupinski. See 2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibits 4 - 15. Mr. Cyrus described his plummeting sales and proposed that UT subsidize his operation to allow it to continue normal hours of operation. UT declined. 2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit E.
Mr. Cyrus also floated the idea of closing the Market Café. Id., Cyrus Exhibits 5-6. Mr. Krupinski concurred, noting that University policy required PPE masks in all common areas, but that such masks were scarce, and that UT could not supply them to Mr. Cyrus or his staff.
On April 7, 2023, Mr. Krupinski emailed Mr. Cyrus and BSVI representative Kelsi Burrier:
Jim,
[A]s of last night/this morning, the University has updated their PPE to include mandatory mask use in all common areas, which includes the Market Café…. We would never ask that you operate at a loss…. I would highly recommend that you [and] Kelsi review operations and begin planning the closure of Market Café service. We cannot justify non-essential staff reporting to the hospital for potential exposure, and we cannot guarantee that UTMC PPE could be allocated to your team. …
Kelsi, … please note that UTMC is operating with the understanding that any closure due to the Covid-19 response falls under Article 11: Force Majeure, with the expectation that once operations may safely resume they do as such.
2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibit 7 (emphasis added).
Over the next ten days, the parties continued to communicate. Mr. Cyrus emailed Mr. Krupinski that he had applied for and received a Payroll Protection Program (PPP) loan [later converted to a grant – Transcript at 464-65 (Cyrus)]. 2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibits 11-12. On April 17, 2020, UT Medical Center CEO Richard Swaine wrote to Ms. Hauck at BSVI:
As you are aware, COVID-19 has resulted in dramatic changes…. Attached please find the current expectations for personal protective equipment (PPE)…. UTMC will require Vendor and Vendor’s employees to comply…. The requirements to supply this PPE is your responsibility and UTMC will not provide PPE to OOD/BSVI or Vendor or Vendor’s employees.
Should Vendor be unable to comply with these measures, or should Vendor determine that it is not in his interest to continue operating, UTMC certainly understands and does not expect OOD/BSVI nor Vendor to continue operations under the BGA if doing so would pose a threat to Vendor or Vendor’s employees or would cause unreasonable business losses for Vendor.
2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibit 7 (emphasis added).
The bolded text in these emails from UT clearly indicates that in April 2020, UT would not have considered it a violation of the BGA if Mr. Cyrus were to have temporarily closed the Market Café. However, the emails arguably sent a mixed message about precisely why. The italicized text indicates that closure would have been justified because PPE was required but not available. The underlined text indicates that closure would have been justified because of decreased demand and consequent business losses.
At about the same time, Mr. Cyrus’s PPP loan money arrived. This both allowed and required him to remain open and to maintain all his staff at their previous levels of employment. See Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 43. By the end of June, however, that money was running out. At that point, restrictions on hospital access remained in place, students and faculty were holding classes online, and the end of the COVID-19 pandemic was nowhere in sight. Transcript 2020-23BE, Vol. II, pp. 28-30 (p.61-62 of PDF), 46 (p.66 of PDF), 121 (p.85 of PDF); Cyrus Exhibits 58-60.  Mr. Cyrus began to reduce staff and (with acquiescence from UT) cut operating hours, hoping that things would turn around by the start of the fall semester in mid-August.  Transcript 2020-23BE, Vol. II, pp. 28-29 (p.61-62 of PDF), 37-39 (p.64 of PDF); 43-44 (p.65 of PDF).
To plan his operations for the fall semester, however, Mr. Cyrus needed to know UT’s re-opening plans. Both he and BSVI Representative Lynn Hustwick made repeated requests to various UT officials requesting information about the University’s plans to re-open. The University’s responses were frustratingly slow and vague, probably because the University itself was not yet certain exactly what the fall re-opening would look like. See Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief at 43-44 and Exhibits cited therein.
All evidence in the record indicates that Mr. Cyrus faults UT – not BSVI – for the lack of information in summer 2020 about UT’s plans to re-open in the fall. See Mr. Cyrus’s testimony, Transcript at 154, 236; Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief at 43 (“Cyrus, along with his BSVI local representative Lynn Hustwick, made repeated efforts to secure concrete information about what to expect when the fall semester would begin…”). The Arbitration Panel does not take Mr. Cyrus to be arguing in this Arbitration that BSVI failed to adequately advocate for him in this regard. If that is his argument, we find it inconsistent with the evidence presented at the Hearing and with his own testimony.
On August 11, 14, 17, 18, and 19, 2020, Mr. Cyrus sent emails to Ms. Hustwick and others describing his financial losses. 2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibits 28-35. None of these emails, however, gave any indication that Mr. Cyrus was considering closing the Market Café. At no time between May and August 2022 did Mr. Cyrus enquire of either UT or BSVI whether they would consider closure of the Market Café at that time to be permitted under the BGA force majeure clause.
On August 27, 2020, Mr. Cyrus sent Ms. Hustwick an email announcing his decision to close the Market Café effective September 2, 2020. 2020-23BE Cyrus Exhibit 36. Ms. Hustwick forwarded Mr. Cyrus’s email to her supervisor, who forwarded it to UT. 2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit V.
On September 4, 2020, UT’s Associate General Counsel Jessica Visser notified BSVI that UT considered Mr. Cyrus’s decision to close the Market Café a breach of the BGA, and threatened to terminate the BGA unless service was reinstated within 14 days. 2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit X. Matthew Lampke, OOD’s Chief Legal Counsel, wrote Ms. Visser on September 10, 2020, to remind her of the April 2020, correspondence from UT indicating that closing the Market Café would be permitted under the BGA force majeure clause. Ms. Visser responded that same day:
Richard [Swaine] and Josh [Krupinski] chose to allow the Market Café to close at the height of the pandemic since Mr. Cyrus was stating he couldn’t obtain any PPE. Mr. Cyrus said he would stay open…. In no way did the University intend or construe that 5 months later after the Governor reopened services Mr. Cyrus would use Richard’s letter as an approval to close. Even since Mr. Cyrus decided to remain open, every time OOD/BSVI submitted a request to adjust current hours the University agreed, allowing adjustments of 615-noon, then 615-11a, and lastly 615-1030a. Additionally, although the vendor alluded to closing, no written request was submitted to the University for review. The Agreement was not amended, and it is not reasonable per the Agreement for the space to remain closed with no plans of reopening when a different vendor, or the University could secure different services.
Food service is allowed through the kiosk and our workforce has been returned entirely to work. It is not the University’s responsibility to guarantee the vendor’s sales. It falls on the vendor to adjust supply and labor costs to business needs in order to operate profitably.
2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit Y.
On September 24, 2020, Mr. Lampke relayed to Ms. Visser a proposal from Mr. Cyrus to reopen the Market Café on January 18, 2021, serving only coffee and pastries, with operating hours only between 7:00 – 10:30 a.m., “on condition that he could operate without sustaining a loss.” 2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit BB. On September 28, 2020, Ms. Visser rejected Mr. Cyrus’s offer, stating that “leaving the facility vacate [sic] for two additional months past the period in which the breach must be cured is not reasonable.” Id.
The next day, Mr. Lampke wrote to Mr. Cyrus:
… No accepted solutions have been presented to cure your breach of the BGA when you closed the coffee kiosk without prior notification to or authorization by UT and OOD/BE. There is no impossibility of performance present as you can simply reopen the coffee kiosk. UT has demanded you reopen the coffee kiosk. You have refused to reopen the coffee kiosk until January 18, 2021, and under two conditions. The best way to protect your interest is to reopen the coffee kiosk on or before October 4, 2020.
2020-23BE BSVI Exhibit DD (emphasis added).
Mr. Cyrus argues that BSVI failed to advocate for him to UT regarding his closure of the Market Café. The Panel finds no basis for that allegation, for five independent reasons. First, Mr. Cyrus breached the BGA, leaving BSVI with no real argument it could have made to UT. Second, Mr. Cyrus’s reliance on the force majeure clause was misplaced, so it did not excuse his performance. Third, Mr. Cyrus did not prove significant business losses, so even if the force majeure clause covered such losses, it would not have excused his performance. Fourth,
BSVI vigorously advocated for Mr. Cyrus notwithstanding the precarious position his breach put BSVI in. Fifth, BSVI’s post-hearing re-award of the Market Café back to Mr. Cyrus has made the issue moot.
1. Mr. Cyrus Breached the BGA
Mr. Cyrus’s BOA with BSVI required him to adhere to the BGA between UT and BSVI. That BGA set the hours of operation, required that they be maintained, permitted adjustment upon written request, and required the parties to take reasonable measures to ensure that no unplanned interruptions to the hours of operation occurred. Mr. Cyrus knew of the requirement of written notice and acquiescence for making adjustments because, as Ms. Visser’s letter to Mr. Lampke on September 10, 2020, pointed out, Mr. Cyrus had successfully requested permission to adjust his hours three times over the previous two months. Nonetheless, he unilaterally closed the Market Café without first obtaining UT’s acquiescence. This breached the UT-BSVI BGA, which in turn breached the Cyrus-BSVI BOA. No amount of BSVI “advocating” could change the fact that Mr. Cyrus had breached, and that there were significant legal consequences (to both BSVI and Mr. Cyrus) resulting from that breach.
2. Mr. Cyrus’s Reliance on the Force Majeure Clause Was Misplaced
Correspondence from Mssrs. Swaine and Krupinski in April 2020 were ambiguous as to whether UT considered pandemic-caused economic losses alone sufficient to trigger the BGA’s force majeure clause. Given that ambiguity, a reasonable business owner concerned, in August 2020, about the possibility of breaching would have sought clarification from either UT or BSVI before risking a breach. Mr. Cyrus, however, did not do that.
By late August 2020, the pandemic situation at the hospital had changed significantly. Though life was far from pre-pandemic normal, PPE was widely available, Ohio’s stay-at-home orders had been lifted, and most staff had returned to the hospital. Under these circumstances, it was not reasonable for Mr. Cyrus to rely on Mssrs. Swaine and Krupinski’s statements from four months prior without enquiring further. Regardless, however, any confusion over the meaning of their April statements was dispelled by Ms. Visser’s notice of breach on September 4, 2020, and her follow-up email of September 10, 2020. Reliance on Mssrs. Swaine and Krupinski’s statements about the force majeure clause at that point – when UT’s position was crystal-clear and there was still time to cure the breach – was per se unreasonable.
Moreover, the plain language of the force majeure clause did not excuse Mr. Cyrus from performance. The clause excuses performance only if performance is “not reasonably within the control of the party claiming inability to perform.” As Mr. Lampke informed Mr. Cyrus on September 29, 2020 – again, while there was still time to cure the breach – “[there was] no impossibility of performance present as you can simply reopen the coffee kiosk.” BSVI’s Post-Hearing Brief cites ample authority for the proposition that a loss of profit or reduced income because of COVID-19 does not satisfy a force majeure clause unless the clause specifically provides that loss of profit is sufficient. See 55 Oak St. LLC v. RDR Enters., 2022 Me. LEXIS 29, 2022 ME 28, ¶ 22 (Me. S. Ct.); KB Salt Lake III, LLC v. Fitness Int’l, LLC, 95 Cal. App. 5th 1032, 1053-54 (Calif. 2nd Dist. Ct. App. 2023). Mr. Cyrus cites no contrary authority.
The force majeure clause therefore did not excuse Mr. Cyrus’s nonperformance of the BGA/BOA agreements. No amount of BSVI “advocating” could change the fact that Mr. Cyrus had breached and that the force majeure clause did not excuse his nonperformance.
3. Even if the Force Majeure Excused Nonperformance Because of Business Losses,
Mr. Cyrus Failed to Prove Business Losses
Mr. Cyrus testified repeatedly that he was operating at a loss when he closed the Market Café. See citations to the record in Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief at 43-46. Nothing in the documentary record, however, demonstrates that he was losing any more money than he was before the pandemic, especially in light of the federal grant relief he obtained. Transcript at 505-13 (Cyrus). He did not produce any bank records to substantiate his cash-flow claims. Id. And he apparently continued to take an operator draw each month. BSVI Exhibit K. Thus, even if the force majeure permitted Mr. Cyrus to close the Market Café because of business losses, Mr. Cyrus has not met his burden of proving that any such business losses were significant enough to trigger the force majeure clause.
4. BSVI Did Not Fail to Advocate for Mr. Cyrus
BSVI, having spent more than $350,000 to build, equip, and stock the Market Café, was heavily invested in its success. When COVID-19 hit, BSVI repeatedly helped secure UT’s permission for Mr. Cyrus to reduce his hours, then worked closely (albeit with limited success) with Mr. Cyrus to obtain information about UT’s plans to re-open in fall 2020.
Mr. Cyrus did not have the courtesy to notify either BSVI or UT before he made the decision in late August to close the Market Café in early September. Nonetheless, and with significant reservations, BSVI argued to UT on behalf of Mr. Cyrus that Mssrs. Swaine and Krupinski in April 2020 had indicated that a closure would not violate the BGA because of the force majeure clause. When it became obvious that that argument was untenable, BSVI explored with Mr. Cyrus and UT options for re-opening. But Mr. Cyrus was unwilling to reopen before 2021, and then only on condition that UT guarantee his profitability – conditions UT was unwilling to accept.
At this point, BSVI was, as BSVI’s Post-Hearing Brief points out, between a rock and a hard place. BSVI’s Post-Hearing Brief at 48, citing Transcript at 403-05, 408-09 (Hauck). BSVI had done everything it could to support Mr. Cyrus, one of BSVI’s blind operators. But it was under a contractual obligation to provide food and beverage services to UT, and Mr. Cyrus’s refusal to do so entitled UT to terminate the BGA with BSVI which would wipe out BSVI’s $350,000 investment and end BSVI’s opportunity for a blind vendor to operate the Market Café. In unilaterally closing the Market Café on the uncertain expectation that he could later re-open, Mr. Cyrus was literally gambling with BSVI’s money.
Under these circumstances, the Panel agrees with Hearing Officer Mosbacher’s conclusions that “[t]here is no substance to the claim that BE took no meaningful action to resolve Mr. Cyrus’ business losses or to advocate on his behalf” and that BSVI has met its obligations to Mr. Cyrus under the Act and the Mini-Act.”
5. The Issue Has Become Moot
On January 4, 2024, BSVI re-awarded the Market Café to Mr. Cyrus, apparently as part of its normal award procedures. See Award Letter of January 4, 2024, submitted with Post-Hearing Briefs. Because the parties have stipulated that no damages will be awarded in this Arbitration, this moots Mr. Cyrus’s claims regarding the Market Café.
IV. Conclusion
The premise of the RSA is that blind persons are as capable as anyone else of building, running, and owning a business if given an opportunity and some capital investment. Owning any business comes with both rewards and risks. The reward is that the business owner gets to keep the profits. The risk is that s/he must accept the losses.
For any business owner, losses often result from circumstances beyond the business owner’s control. Both UT’s Healthy Beverage Policy and the COVID-19 pandemic confronted Mr. Cyrus with the prospect of sustaining business losses because of circumstances beyond his (or BSVI’s) control. Rather than accepting that such risk is part-and-parcel of being a businessowner, Mr. Cyrus has tried to shift all his business risk to BSVI, arguing essentially that its “duty to advocate” for him obliged BSVI to somehow make those circumstances go away or assume the risk itself. That is not how the RSA – or how being a business owner – works.
Similarly, Mr. Cyrus seems to believe that BSVI’s “duty to advocate” for him extends to somehow erasing statutory provisions (e.g., restrictions on what BSVI can spend money on; joint determinations of blind-vendor sites at Ohio universities) and contract clauses (e.g., giving universities the right to decide which products are sold on their campuses; requiring performance of a contract notwithstanding short-term economic losses) he does not like. Whatever BSVI’s “duty to advocate” for blind vendors might mean, it does not mean this.
Mr. Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief accuses BSVI of treating “blind vendors as charity cases … rather than dignified and respected partners in a business enterprise.” Cyrus’s Post-Hearing Brief at 4. The Panel, however, finds that treating Mr. Cyrus as a “dignified and respected partner[] in a business enterprise” is precisely what BSVI has consistently done. Business owners comply with laws, respect contracts, and deal with adverse market conditions. BSVI’s expectation that Mr.  Cyrus will do the same does not mean it has failed to advocate for him.
The findings of fact and conclusions of law in 2015-02BE, 2015-04BE, 2015-05BE, and 2020-23BE are adopted by this Panel in their entirety. The Panel Majority finds no basis for the Complaints filed in these cases.

I also concur, for the limited reasons stated below, in the Panel Award finding that Cyrus is entitled to no relief on his claims respecting the termination of the Market Café, presented in Section III.C of the Panel Award.
For clarity, I do not join the Majority in adopting wholesale the findings of fact and conclusions of law in 2015-02BE, 2015-04BE, 2015-05BE or 2020-23BE.  I agree, however, that the Majority has correctly summarized or quoted the findings of fact and conclusions of law in the Report and Recommendations of the Hearing Officers, adopted by OOD, in those respective grievances.
I dissent, for the reasons stated below, from the Majority’s findings and conclusions on Cyrus’ claims regarding BSVI’s performance of its statutory duties to seek and secure vending opportunities for licensed blind vendors on the UT Main campus, presented in Section III.A.  of the Panel Award.
Discussion
Sections I and II of the Panel Award are generally accurate and complete statements of the Facts and Posture of the issues before the Panel. However, I not find it useful or sufficient to simply characterize Cyrus’s claims against BSVI as a “failure to advocate”.  Therefore, I would restate the three issues before the Panel this way:
The first is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligations to Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and mini-RSA, in his efforts to secure vending rights (including but not limited to pouring rights), food-service contracts, the right to install ATM machine, et cetera, at the UT Main Campus. The second is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligations to Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and mini-RSA, regarding UT’s alleged disparate enforcement of its “Healthy Beverage Policy”. The third is whether BSVI failed to fulfill its obligations to Cyrus, consistent with its responsibilities under the RSA and mini-RSA, when Cyrus decided to close the Market Café.
I first address Cyrus’ claims regarding the Healthy Beverage Policy and the Market Café. As to these claims I concur in the Majority’s denial of relief. But I do not concur in all of the Majority’s findings or conclusions of law.
The Healthy Beverage Policy at UT HSC.
The Majority accurately finds that, after nearly two years of regular and persistent complaints by Cyrus to both UT and BSVI regarding UT’s beverage policy and its disparate application, UT exempted Cyrus from compliance with the policy. And shortly thereafter UT discontinued the policy entirely, and it has not been reinstated.
It is undisputed that over the two years the policy was in effect UT enforced
the policy consistently against Cyrus, but inconsistently enforced the policy against other vendors on the UT HSC.  Cyrus’ claim that as a result of the policy he lost sales, which he estimated to be in excess of $100,000, is also undisputed.   But Cyrus has conceded, as he must, that this Panel has no authority to award damages, and he accordingly does not seek damages.  With the beverage policy terminated by UT, there is no relief this Panel could grant.  And it cannot be said that this issue is ripe to reoccur.  Therefore, I concur in the Majority’s conclusion that the issue is moot, as stated as its “fifth” basis (Award at 16), and would deny the claim.
I do not concur in the Majority’s findings stated as “first” through “third”.  Collectively, those three findings conclude that BSVI had no basis or authority to contest UT’s demonstrated and undisputed failures to require other vendors to follow the beverage policy while requiring that Cyrus do so.  These findings are overbroad and unnecessary to resolution of this issue.  A grantor’s conduct enforcing a policy against a licensed blind vendor, but not other vendors, to the detriment of the licensed blind vendor, on its face erodes the licensed blind vendors’ priority.  I would find that BSVI has a duty to enforce the licensed blind vendor’s priority underlying the BGA agreement, and the dispute resolution provisions afford BSVI a forum to resolve such a dispute. It also appears that BSVI believes it has a basis for contesting a grantor’s differential treatment of its vendors, as evidences by the Vicki Smith March 26, 2016 email to UT referenced in the Majority opinion and discussed below.
I also do not concur with Majority’s “fourth” basis for denying relief, which rests on its conclusion that “the record demonstrates that BSVI advocated vigorously on [Cyrus’] behalf regarding the Policy.”  Award at 16.  Objectively viewed, for nearly two years BSVI’s advocacy consisted of forwarding Cyrus’ emails reporting violations of the policy by other vendors and asserting his losses resulting from the inconsistent application of the policy to other vendors.  This is not “vigorous” advocacy by any measure.
The Majority also relies on the fact that UT’s eventual decision to exempt Cyrus from the policy followed an email to UT from a BSVI representative.  Specifically, on March 26, 2016, shortly before the administrative hearing on Cyrus’ grievance regarding BSVI’s failure to challenge UT’s disparate application of the policy, BVSI Area Manager Victoria Smith formally notified UT that the application of the beverage policy to Cyrus violated the Vending Service Agreement. Within days, on April 18, 2016, UT exempted Cyrus from the Policy and permitted him to sell sugared beverages in all his vending machines. 15-05BE State’s Exhibit 8 at 113. But the fact that UT’s agreement to exempt Cyrus followed a much more forceful and direct communication from Ms. Smith, made nearly two years after Cyrus first invoked BSVI’s assistance (and just before the grievance hearing), also supports Cyrus’ contention that BSVI failed over several month in its duty to do everything necessary and appropriate to support his facility.
Because the issue is moot, the Panel need not, and should not, decide whether BSVI’s conduct violated a duty to Cyrus.  But if the issue is to be decided, I would not find that BSVI’s conduct demonstrated “vigorous advocacy” for Cyrus in this matter.
The Closure of the Market Cafe
The facts surrounding this issue are accurately presented in detail in the Panel Award. Award at 16-20.
The Majority finds five “independent” bases for rejecting Cyrus’ claim that BSVI refused to enforce the BGA with UT and failed to advocate for him to UT regarding his closure of the Market Café.   I concur in four of the five.
Cyrus’ Closure of the Market Café and failure to reopen during the cure period afforded to him by UT breached the BGA. While Cyrus’ initial decision to close was reasonable in light of the prior communications from UT, Cyrus’ continued reliance on the force majeure clause during the cure period was not reasonable based on the clear weight of authority regarding the scope and meaning of such clauses.
I do not join the Majority’s verbiage castigating Cyrus, and I do not agree that Cyrus’ conduct would (or did) wipe out BSVI’s investment in the Café, or deny any opportunity for a licensed blind vendor to operate the Market Café. Under the BGA, UT was required to reimburse BSVI if it terminated the agreement, and the record is clear that a new vendor was placed in the Café.
But I join the Majority in finding that BSVI’s advocacy on behalf of Cyrus was sufficient under the circumstances.   And in any event, the post-hearing award of the Market Café to Cyrus, however it occurred, renders the issue moot. See Award Letter of January 4, 2024, submitted with Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief.
I do not concur in the Majority‘s holding that Cyrus’ claims regarding closure of the Market Café fails because he has failed to prove business losses.  The Majority makes this finding based on the failure of Cyrus to produce bank records; and the fact that Cyrus “apparently” took an operator draw each month.  Award at 22.  But neither point refutes Cyrus’ clear testimony that the Market Café was operating at a loss.  Moreover, BSVI has never disputed that the Market Café was operating at a loss during the pandemic and continuing until Cyrus closed it.  Indeed, part of BSVI’s “advocacy” for Cyrus with UT was to affirm that the Café was operating at a loss during the pandemic. The existence of such losses was a featured point in BSVI’s advocacy to UT.  Likewise, UT never contested Cyrus’ claim that the Market Café was losing money with the greatly diminished level of activity at the site.   On this record, there is no basis to deny Cyrus’ claim due to a failure to prove business losses.
Vending opportunities at the UT Main Campus
In each of the four grievances that underlie the Complaints before the Panel Cyrus has asserted that, over several years and continuing, BSVI has (despite his repeated requests) failed to seek and secure suitable vending facilities on behalf of licensed blind vendors generally, and himself specifically, on the UT Main Campus.  In his post-hearing briefs, Cyrus asks the Panel to impose broad and specific injunctive relief directed at BSVI’s administration of the RSA and mini-RSA at all Ohio state supported colleges, universities and community colleges, including but not limited to UT.  Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 63-65 and Cyrus Reply Addressing Requested Remedies.
As a threshold matter, like the Majority, I consider only Cyrus’ claims regarding BSVI’s actions and inactions with respect to vending operations at UT, and do not consider Cyrus’ requests for relief concerning other state universities and colleges. The Preliminary Statements of each of the two Complaints before the panel state: Cyrus “brings [the] complaint against ….[BSVI’s] … for its acts and omissions in connection with vending operations at the University of Toledo.” (emphasis added). Similarly, the factual assertions in each Complaint relate only to BSVI’s conduct in relation to UT.  And the record shows that Cyrus has only requested that BSVI obtain vending opportunities for him at UT.
Like the Majority, I also do not address the actions or inactions of BSVI regarding the proposed Starbucks facility at the UT HSC.  It is undisputed that the proposed Starbucks facility did not come under consideration until 2023, roughly two years after the last consolidate Complaint was filed. For obvious reasons, BSVI’s actions regarding that facility were not at issue, or addressed, in any of the four grievances that underlie the two Complaints before the Panel.  And they of course were not pleaded in either of the Complaints referred to the Panel.
Cyrus asserts that licensed blind vendors have a statutory right to the lucrative vending opportunities on UT’s Main Campus, and asserts that BSVI’s acts and omissions constitute a failure to enforce a statutory right of priority to blind vendors.  The alleged failures include, but are not limited to, BSVI’s failure to secure multimillion dollar “pouring rights” contracts that have always been given to large private contractors, and BSVI’s failure to secure expansive multimillion dollar food services vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus that have also always been awarded to large corporate entities.
Cyrus’ claim that BSVI’s acts and omissions violate a statutory obligation is based first on the mini-RSA provision in O.R.C 3304.29(D), which states that BSVI is required to “do everything necessary and proper to carry out sections 3304.29 and to 3304.34 of the Revised Code.”  The priority provisions of the mini-RSA, in pertinent part, provide as follows:
No private contract or concession to operate a vending facility on governmental property shall be granted unless the bureau of services for the visually impaired has determined that such facility is not a satisfactory site for a suitable vending facility operated by a blind licensee.  O.R.C. § 3304.33.
Every person in charge of governmental property to be substantially renovated or who is responsible for the acquisition, lease, or rental of such property shall consult with the director of the bureau of services for the visually impaired prior to such renovation, acquisition, lease, or rental to determine if sufficient numbers of persons will be using such property to support a suitable vending facility. If the director determines that such property would be a satisfactory site for a suitable vending facility, provision shall be made for electrical outlets, plumbing fixtures, and other requirements for the installation and operation of a suitable vending facility. In the case of a state university, medical university, technical college, state community college, community college, university branch district, or state-affiliated college or university, the decision to establish a suitable vending facility shall be made jointly by the director of services for the visually impaired and proper administrative authorities of the state or state-affiliated college or university.  O.R.C. § 3304.30.
“Government property” is defined by the Ohio Revised Code to include:
… any real property, building, or facility owned, leased, or rented by the state or any board, commission, department, division, or other unit or agency thereof [but excluding adult and juvenile correctional facilities].  O.R.C. § 3304.28.
A “suitable vending facility” is broadly defined to include:
… automatic vending machines, cafeterias, snack bars, cart services, shelters, counters, and other appropriate auxiliary equipment which may be operated by licensees who are blind which is necessary for the sale of newspapers, periodicals, confections, tobacco products, food, beverages, and other articles or services dispensed automatically or manually and prepared on or off the premises, including the vending or exchange of chances for the state lottery.
See, 34 C.F.R. 395.1(x); O.R.C. § 3304.28(A); O.A.C. 3304:1-21-1(S)).
In order to identify vending opportunities for blind vendors, state law directs BSVI to
“Survey suitable vending facility concession opportunities for individuals who are
blind on governmental property”
“[S]eek and secure suitable vending facilities”
“Obtain and make public, information concerning employment opportunities for
individuals who are blind in suitable vending facilities
O.R.C. 3304.29; O.A.C. 3304:1-21-11(E), and (N) (2010 Supplement); 34 C.F.R. 395.3(A)(11)(vi).
The Ohio Attorney General has opined that the foregoing priority provisions of Ohio law apply to all suitable vending facilities, regardless of whether they will be operated by a private contractor or whether operated “in house” by the government entity in control of the property. See, Ohio Attorney General Opinion 2014-008 (March 4, 2014), p. 5, stating that “a state or state-affiliated college or university may not establish vending facilities of any kind, whether in-house or through private contract, if there is no joint decision regarding the establishment of a suitable vending facility by the college or university and the Director of BSVI.”
BSVI conducts a separate “suitability” determination by assessing whether the potential vending operation has sufficient foot traffic to generate sufficient revenue as a separate facility, or at least sufficient revenue to be attached to an existing facility as an additional “site.”  The Director of OOD has expressly determined that “the only statutory criterion for a determination to establish a suitable facility is whether there will be sufficient numbers of persons … using the property to support a suitable vending facility. See, 15-02BE, Cyrus Exh. 2, p. 4.  That determination is in accord with Attorney General of Ohio Opinion 2014-008, which states:
R.C. 3304.30 gives only one criterion to be considered in determining whether a suitable vending facility will be established: whether sufficient numbers of persons will be using such property to support a suitable vending facility.  The inquiry ends there.  If the property is determined to be a satisfactory site, R.C. 3304.30 requires the installation and operation of a suitable vending facility.
See Ohio Attorney General Opinion 2014-008, supra, at p. 7.
Similarly, in an earlier formal opinion, the Ohio Attorney General concluded, based on the explicit language of the Mini-RSA, that:
[BSVI] cannot decline to establish a suitable vending facility and permit the college or university to proceed with a private contract or concession without effectively determining that the facility is not a satisfactory site for a blind licensee and, thus, that [BSVI] has no further interest in locating a facility on the property.
See, 15-05BE, Cyrus Exh. 62 (Ohio Attorney General Opinion 2002-037, December 11, 2002), p. 8.
Further, the Ohio Attorney General has advised:
[a] state or state-affiliated college or university is not permitted to enter into a private contract or concession for the operation of a vending facility on its property so long as the Bureau maintains that the facility is a satisfactory site for a suitable vending facility operated by a blind licensee.
Ohio Attorney General Opinion, 2002-037, p. 8, 15-05BE, Exh. 62 (Emphasis supplied); See also, Ohio Attorney General Opinion, 2014-008, 15-02BE, p. 5.   The attorney general has also advised that:
a state or state-affiliated college or university may not establish vending facilities of any kind, whether in-house or through private contract, if there is no joint decision regarding the establishment of a suitable vending facility by the college or university and the Director of BSVI.
Ohio Attorney General Opinion 2014-008 (March 4, 2014), p. 5. And, “[t]he absence of a joint decision does not constitute a determination by BSVI that a property is not suitable for a vending facility operated by a blind vendor.” Ohio Attorney General Opinion, 2002-037, Id. at p. 5.
Finally, the Ohio Attorney General has made clear, based on the statutory language, that
[t]he Bureau cannot decline to establish a suitable vending facility and permit the college or university to proceed with a private contract or concession without effectively determining that the facility is not a satisfactory site for a blind licensee and, thus, that the Bureau has no further interest in locating a facility on the property.
Ohio Attorney General Opinion, 2002-037, p. 8, 15-05BE, Ex. 62 (emphasis supplied); see also, Ohio Attorney General Opinion, 2014-008, 15-02BE, p. 5.   And in the event of a dispute, BSVI is required to exercise its authority to enforce these statutory directives through arbitration. R.C.§ 3304.32 (“If a dispute concerning the establishment of a suitable vending facility arises or if the bureau of services for the visually impaired determines that a department, agency, or governmental unit in control of governmental property has not complied with sections 3304.29 to 3304.34 of the Revised Code, an administrative hearing shall be held.”) (Emphasis supplied).
It is against these statutory provisions, as interpreted by the Ohio Attorney General, that the Panel must assess the sufficiency of BSVI’s actions and inaction to seek and secure vending opportunities for licenses blind vendors at the UT Main Campus.
BSVI does not dispute Cyrus’ assertion that the most lucrative and wide-ranging vending opportunities at UT are on the Main Campus.  Nor does BSVI dispute that since the enactment of the mini-RSA in July 1980, there has not been a blind vendor operated vending facility on the UT Main Campus; all vending opportunities have gone to private contractors not licensed as blind vendors.  Tr., 20-23BE, Vol II, pp. 148-149 (p.91-92 of PDF).
BSVI’s actions related to seeking or securing vending opportunities for blind vendors on the UT Main Campus (whether taken in response to Cyrus’ requests or otherwise) are described in detail in Cyrus’ post-hearing brief. Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 20-24, 28-32.  The record supports Cyrus’ factual description, which relies largely on the testimony of BSVI representatives.  BSVI’s post-hearing brief does not challenge the accuracy or completeness of Cyrus’ recitation of facts in this regard.
The record is most telling for its demonstration of what BSVI has not done. It is undisputed that, despite repeated requests from Cyrus commencing at least as early as 2014, BSVI has:
never required or demanded that UT notify BSVI of all of its vending opportunities;
never required or demanded that UT notify BSVI of all of its vending contracts with private parties or their expirations dates;
never required or demanded that UT participate in a “suitability” determination regarding vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus;
never objected to the instances in which UT has granted vending contracts to private contractors not licensed as blind vendors without first notifying BSVI of the vending opportunity or obtaining a joint determination that a vending opportunity was not “suitable”; and
never invoked (or even threatened to invoke) BSVI’s statutory right to an administrative hearing on the occasions when UT awarded vending contracts to private parties without obtaining a determination of suitability.
The totality of the record compels the conclusion that BSVI’s actions to seek and secure vending opportunities for blind vendors on the UT Main Campus from 2014 to the present have been sporadic, few in number and were halfhearted at best, with virtual no follow up.
The Majority excuses BVSI’s failure to make even minimal efforts to even seek lucrative vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus for licensed blind vendors on two grounds.
First, the Majority relies on the fact that BSVI is not permitted by statute to pay the large sums that state universities typically receive when they contract with large private corporate entities.  Indeed, it is undisputed that UT has received significant sums when it has awarded such contracts to large private vendors without seeking or participating in a suitability determination; and for that reason UT has stated that is not interested in giving vending opportunities on its Main Campus to licensed blind vendors. Award at 12 and 13.  The Majority concludes that BSVI’s inability to make such payments to universities essentially negates, or overrides, any duty of BSVI to exercise its statutory duties to seek and secure such vending contracts for blind vendors.
Clearly, the benefit to UT of contracting with private entities is significant. As the Majority notes, it is not surprising that UT does not wish to relinquish the ability to contract with private corporate entities that are able and willing to pay millions of dollars for the opportunity to control all vending opportunities on the campus.  The problem with this defense, however, is that it runs afoul of the RSA and mini-RSA. The statutory provisions cited above, and the directly on point opinions of the Ohio Attorney General rendered upon inquiry from the Director of OOD, make it clear that the mini-RSA imposes requirements on UT, as a state university, to determine jointly with BSVI whether vending facilities are suitable for blind vendor, and when so determined to afford them to blind vendors. There is no authority that allows Ohio universities to place its financial interests ahead of the rights of blind vendors.  Universities may not undercut the RSA or mini-RSA by negotiating with private vendors to receive payments that BSVI may not make.
The Majority also excuses BSVI’s failure to make any meaningful effort to seek and secure vending contracts for licensed blind vendors by concluding that the Ohio statutory requirement of a joint determination of suitability gives universities “an effective veto”.  Award at 13.  This conclusion must be rejected because it is directly at odds with the opinions rendered by the Ohio Attorney General, discussed above.  The Majority discounts and disregards the OAG Opinions, asserting that the opinions establish “only … legal duties owed by state universities, not by BVSI.”  Id.   That is not a fair or correct understanding of the opinions.  While the questions posed by the Director of OOD in seeking the opinions were directed to the obligations of universities, it cannot be doubted that the OOD was seeking guidance regarding its enforcement duties under the min-RSA.  The opinions specifically state that disputes over suitability, as well as a universities decision to issue a vending contract without, or in disregard of, a joint suitability determination, give rise to a mandatory duty by BSVI to seek an administrative hearing to resolve the dispute.
For purposes of the present Complaints, the conclusive fact is that BSVI has made virtually no effort to even seek vending opportunities for blind vendors on the UT Campus.  The record demonstrates that beyond “pouring rights” contracts, there are opportunities for BSVI to secure lucrative “following” rights for blind vendors.  There exist multiple discrete food vending opportunities, and the Ohio Attorney General has made it clear that state universities cannot defeat opportunities for blind vendors by “bundling” all food services. There are many individual food venues that may be determined separately to constitute suitable facilities for one or more blind vendors, including dining halls, sandwich shops, catering services, and athletic concessions. See, 15-02BE, Cyrus Exh. 40, pp, 27-30.   This is demonstrated by the fact that even on the far smaller UT HSC campus of UT, both the Market Café and the newly proposed Starbucks were found to be suitable facilities when evaluated by BSVI.   Tr., 20-23BE, Vol. I, p. 106 (p.27 of PDF); Arb. Exh. C-20, p. 21 (pdf p. 90); Arb. Tr., p. 307.    BSVI has done virtually nothing to seek and secure similar such opportunities for licensed blind vendors on the UT Main Campus.
I also dissent from the Majority’s third independent reason for denying Cyrus’ claim regarding BSVI’s action and inaction regarding vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus.  The Majority finds that BSVI has, in fact, advocated for Cyrus to obtain vending rights on the UT Main Campus on the basis that BSVI negotiated “a follow-on exemption for Mr. Cyrus, allowing him to sell beverages on the Main Campus notwithstanding an existing exclusive pouring- rights contract.”  Award at 13. This factual finding is based on a misunderstanding of the record evidence regarding the exemption that UT negotiated for Cyrus. The record is clear that neither Cyrus nor any other blind vendor has ever has had vending rights on the UT Main Campus. More to the point, the record is clear that the “exemption” that was negotiated for Cyrus applied only to his Facility 304, which is only the UT HSC. BSVI Post-Hearing Brief at 28; Tr. Vol I at 166. ; Tr. Vol. II at 389-91; 15-05 State’s Ex. 13 at 230.   The pouring rights exemption that BSVI negotiated for Facility 304 at the UT HSC has no relevance to, and provides no basis to deny, Cyrus’ claims respecting BSVI’s actions and inactions to seek and secure vending opportunities at the UT Main Campus.
I also dissent from the Majority’s holding that Cyrus’ challenge to BVSI’s actions and inaction to seek and secure vending rights for licensed blind vendors on the UT Main Campus fails for the “independent reason” that, even if BSVI were to obtain one or more contracts to provide vending on the Main Campus, such contracts would not necessarily be awarded to Cyrus. Award at 14.  The Majority concludes that because Cyrus is not guaranteed to be awarded any new vending contract on the UT Main Campus, he “has no basis for asserting that he has been harmed by any alleged failure of BSVI to vigorously pursue vending on the UT Main Campus.” Id.
I first note that the Majority’s legal conclusion that Cyrus’ claim that BSVI has breached its duty to seek and secure vending contracts on the UT Main Campus fails simply because Cyrus is not guaranteed the award of any vending contract that BSVI may secure on the UT Main Campus has not been advanced by BSVI in these proceedings. BSVI filed a lengthy pre-hearing motion for summary judgment on all claims, vigorously asserting many and varied grounds for denying Cyrus’ claims. See Respondent Opportunities for Ohioans with Disabilities Bureau of Services for the Visually Impaired Motion for Summary Judgment, September 15, 2023, and Reply in Support of Motion for Summary Judgment, October 31, 2023.   But BSVI did not argue that the mere fact that Cyrus was not assured the award of any future vending contracts on the UT Main Campus defeated Cyrus’ claims. In its Closing Brief, BSVI renewed the arguments made in its Motion and Reply for Summary Judgment (BSVI Post-Hearing Brief at 1) and advanced many others. But once again, BSVI did not assert that the mere fact Cyrus is not guaranteed the award of any contract that BSVI may secure for the UT Main Campus is, of itself, an “independent basis” (or any basis) for denying Cyrus’ claims that BSVI has breached a duty to seek and secure vending contracts on the UT Main Campus.
BSVI only introduces this issue in in its post-hearing brief with the statement: “It must be said that even if BSVI obtained a contract for vending on the main campus, it would not necessarily be awarded to Cyrus.” BSVI Post–Hearing Brief at 40.  It then concludes: “So the Panel must reject any notion that seeking a contract on main campus is an effort on behalf of Cyrus. Id at 41.   BSVI clearly does not, however, contend that the fact that Cyrus is not guaranteed any contract on the UT Main Campus defeats his right to challenge the sufficiency of BSVI’s action to seek and secure vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus.   Thus, by implication, BVSVI has conceded, as it must, that Cyrus’ is not required to prove that he would be awarded any future contract at the UT Main campus to pursue his claims that BSVI’s efforts fail to satisfy it its statutory duties.
In any event, the Majority conclusion is wrong as a matter of law. As a condition of participation in the Randolph-Sheppard program, an SLA such as BSVI, must agree “to provide any blind licensee dissatisfied with any action arising from operation or administration of the vending facility program an opportunity for a fair hearing and … to submit the grievances of any blind licensee not otherwise resolved by such hearing to arbitration.” (Emphasis added). 20 U.S.C 107b(6); 20 U.S.C 107d-1(a), 107d-2(a) and 107d-2(b)(1). In conformity with this obligation, Ohio’s min-RSA regulations, O.A.C. Section 3304:1-21-14, establish precisely this grievance process.  This broad and unqualified mandatory grievance process clearly permits any licensed blind vendor to challenge BSVI’s administration of the blind vending program, including the sufficiency of its efforts to seek and secure vending opportunities.  There is no requirement that a licensed blind vendor demonstrate that he/she would be entitled to any future vending opportunity, and it is error for the Majority to require such proof.
It bears noting that the Majority’s legal conclusion in this regard effectively means that the sufficiency of BSVI’s performance of its statutory duties to seek and secure new vending contracts for licensed blind vendors would never be subject to administrative or judicial scrutiny. Because no specific licensed blind vendor is guaranteed the award of a new a contract, no vendor could, under the Majority holding, prove “harm” from BSVI’s failure to seek and secure such contracts.   Licensed blind vendors would effectively have no right to dispute the sufficiency of BSVI’s efforts (a position which even BSVI does not take).  This holding completely undermines the mandatory dispute resolution provisions of both the RSA and mini-RSA, and therefore must be rejected.
As shown above, neither the RSA nor the mini-RSA require a licensed blind vendor to prove “harm” by way of entitlement to a new vending opportunity in order to dispute the sufficiency of BSVI’s efforts to seek and secure such opportunities for licensed blind vendors. But even if proof of “harm” were required, in this case “harm” to Cyrus is demonstrated in two ways.
First, the record shows that the establishment of vending contracts for blind vendors on governmental property (federal and state) is a significant source of the funds that BSVI uses to develop and support vending opportunities for blind vendors.  Licensed blind vendors pay a service charge to BVSI. For every dollar of service charge received by BSVI, it draws down $3.69 in federal matching dollars.  Those service charges and matching dollars are used by BSVI to purchase equipment and otherwise support licensed blind vendors.  The more service charges and matching federal dollars generated by BGA agreements, the more funds available to BSVI to perform it statutory duties and expand opportunities for licensed blind vendors. TR.15-02BE, Vol. II, p.195-197; TR 20-23BE, Vol. I, pp.140-142. Thus, BSVI’s failure to seek and secure vending opportunities harms to all licensed blind vendors.
Second, while Cyrus may not be “entitled” to any future vending contracts BSVI may secure for the UT Main Campus, he has repeatedly expressed his interest in securing such a contract. And the record shows that Cyrus would be a qualified and worthy applicant such a contract. Cyrus’ career as a licensed blind vendor is described in detail in his post-hearing brief, Cyrus Post-Hearing Brief at 9-12 (with record citations). Over his 30 year career as a licensed blind vendor Cyrus has been regarded as one of the most successful vendors in Ohio’s blind vendor program. He has been characterized as an “exemplary” operator by UT’s Vice President in charge of food services. His evaluations by BSVI have also been among the highest in the state during his entire career, and that has never not been the case. Further, a BSVI representative testified that Cyrus has consistently been among the top operators in the state; his vending operations have always been successful; and his profitability has been among the highest in the state. On that basis Cyrus has established sufficient “harm” to challenge the sufficiency of BSVI’s action and inactions in seeking and securing contracts for blind vendors on the UT Main Campus.
For clarity, I do not join the Majority position, espoused in what appears to be dicta at page 13 and 14 of the Award, which appears to accept BSVI’s contention that its duty to seek and secure vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus is excused by BSVI administrators’ fears that to achieve vending rights it may need to exercise its rights via administrative hearings or judicial challenge, and such challenges might deter “cooperation” from other state universities.  The Majority also appears to accept BSVI’s suggestion that it is justified in failing to take any action to secure vending rights at the UT Main Campus for fear that such action might lead state universities to seek legislative changes to exempt them from the mini-RSA. Id at 14. See BSVI Post-Hearing Brief at 32-34.
This post hoc defense fails for three reasons.  First, the fundamental failure of BSVI regarding vending opportunities on the UT Main Campus in this case is not a failure to commence administrative hearings or bring lawsuits, but rather to take even the simplest and most preliminary steps to fulfill its statutory obligation to take action “necessary and proper” to seek and secure vending opportunities for blind vendors.  Second, the cynical suggestion that the pursuit of vending rights at the UT Main Campus may prompt repeal of certain rights is entirely speculative. There is no record evidence that UT, or any state university, has threatened such action; and no evidence that such legislative action has even been suggested to or by the Ohio legislature.  And there is also no evidence that this “risk” actually informed or motivated BSVI’s failure to seek vending opportunities on the UT main campus.   Finally, BSVI cites no legal authority for the proposition that a state agency may decline to fulfill clear legal obligations for fear of some form of retaliation for exercising its obligations.
For the reasons stated above, I find that Cyrus has proven that BSVI has failed to fulfill its statutory duty to seek and secure vending opportunities for licensed blind vendors on the UT Main Campus.  BSVI, objectively, has failed to satisfy this basic purpose of the RSA and Mini-RSA by continuing, for decades, to allow UT to contract with private entities and exclude blind vendors from the most lucrative vending opportunities without taking even the most elementary steps to exercise its statutory rights with respect to those opportunities.
For remedy, I would require BSVI to do the following:
Notify UT of its obligation under R.C. 3304.28 et seq. to notify BSVI of all vending opportunities, as defined by OAC O.A.C. 3304:1-21-1(S), and to cooperate with BSVI in jointly assessing the suitability of all such vending for operation by a licensed blind vendor on all of its campuses, including the main campus.
Advise UT that it may not proceed to establish any vending unless there has been a determination that it is not suitable as a blind vending facility.
Take all steps necessary and proper to enforce the UT’s compliance with these obligations.
Take all steps necessary and proper to assess the suitability of any identified vending opportunity on the UT campus and exercise the right of priority over any such opportunity as a facility and/or site attachment.
