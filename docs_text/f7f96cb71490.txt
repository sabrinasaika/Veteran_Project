POSITIONS OF THE PARTIES:
The parties filed comprehensive well thought out briefs setting forth their positions and arguments in detail.  What follows does not purport to be a detailed review of the parties’ positions, but a summary thereof.
Summary of Complainants (Plaintiffs) Position and Arguments
Plaintiff’s brief, including evidence, was reviewed for its laws, regulations and codes associated with the argument of administrative malfeasance regarding Illinois Blind Services Bid #435 – The Chicago Main Post Office.  Plaintiff alleges that Illinois administrators (Defendants) intentionally created opportunities for one vendor Donnie Anderson who happens to be an administrative counterpart in his administrative role on the Committee of Blind Vendors.  That vendor is also an Illinois blind vendor who won Bid #435.  Plaintiff alleges that Donnie Anderson’s dual role encouraged actions by Illinois administrators to circumvent prescriptive practices established for blind vendors.  Plaintiff alleges that instead of resolving a well-documented issue with her existing vending facility, one severely impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, administrators caused her further harm by not using their position of authority to effectively promote enumerative experiences to her operation as dictated by both the Randolph-Sheppard and Illinois Blind Services Acts.  Plaintiff raised the arguments 1) the Main Post Office was illegally consolidated, 2) measures used to evaluate bidders was erroneous and outdated, 3) an Administrative Code designed to create ethical compliance was circumvented, 4) an administrator created perjury when questioned about facts of the case.  Plaintiff provided detailed expense contracts and reports, including official blind vendor documents that undisputedly provided meaningful data about the cost to bring light to the malfeasance and address lost revenues.  The spirit of Plaintiff’s argument encapsulates one where, despite her ranking in the evaluation process, her role as a Plaintiff is legit because she has suffered injury as a certified Randolph-Sheppard Act (RSA) vendor.
Facts of Plaintiff’s Argument
Empirical evidence was submitted by Plaintiff to support her claims.  There are laws, regulations, codes, program budgetary information that provided support for each allegation of administrative malfeasance.  Malfeasance was alleged in the case with Bid #435 the Main Post Office because documentation provided by Plaintiff shows no formal study was completed before its two profitable floors were consolidated.  Profitable units are disallowed in matters of consolidation.  The Bid #435 evaluation panel used an outdated inappropriate criterion to evaluate bidders, and simultaneously minimized educational and professional experiences type matrixes.  Plaintiff brought significant focus on Donnie Anderson.  He was/is the winner of Bid #435.  Donnie Anderson was given another facility through a special agreement with the administration, known as the Statesville Correctional Center, within a day of being approved for Bid #435.  A third case was referred to by Plaintiff, the case of the Markham Courthouse.  Donnie Anderson was able to operate that facility almost a full year after winning Bid #435 and being approved to operate the Statesville Correctional Center.  Plaintiff provides legitimate evidence about administrative conduct that was minimally unethical, and upon inspection provides evidence that administration paved the road for Donnie Anderson to achieve a high
level of enumerative revenues and simultaneously stifled Plaintiff’s right to have just an ordinary facility that functioned beyond the limitations created by COVID-19 pandemic.  Plaintiff alleges that the RSA and Illinois Blind Vendor Act both were violated, especially since administrators failed to resolve her financial shortfall caused by closing parts of her vending operations.  Plaintiff provided effective evidence that evaluations are virtually carbon copies of one another and served as testimony that instruments designed to evaluate vendors can jeopardize the efficacy of evaluations and the bidding process.  Evidence of malfeasance was presented compared administrative conduct to state rules for employee conduct.  Most striking element of Plaintiff’s argument was documents that shed light on the major issue of the case – the issue of Donnie Anderson’s unequivocal success throughout her argument.

Standard of Review
The arbitration panel must conduct a hearing and render a decision.  The party seeking relief bears the burden of proof by a preponderance of evidence.
Argument
1.	Miss Conway cannot bring a breach of contract or negligence claim because it was not part of the original request for arbitration.
2.	The bidding process was conducted properly, and it cannot be proven otherwise.  The applicant with the highest score was awarded the site.  Ms. Conway’s score was the lowest and below the threshold that would allow the site to be awarded to her regardless of how others scored.  Nothing in the law or rules guarantees that the application, bidding, and selection process will benefit all applicants.  There is no evidence that the blame for Ms. Conway’s low score lies with the selection committee rather than with Ms. Conway.  Applicants are not awarded bids because they are struggling financially.  Rather, 14 specific factors are considered by the selection committee.  There is no rule that requires a formal study prior to combining 2 profitable facilities.  Nothing in the Randolph-Sheppard Act or relevant Illinois law prohibits awarding a “windfall” which was never specifically defined.  There is no rule or law that requires applicants to divulge that they serve on the ICBV and vendors on this committee are eligible to participate in the application process.  Ms. Conway was not alerted that Mr. Anderson was awarded Site 435 within the two days required in the rules but this does not materially affect the application process.  The Department has the authority to do all things
necessary and proper to carry out the program and the service agreement in question was completely legal.  Based on the 11th amendment, damages are not an appropriate remedy even if the arbitration panel determined that the application
process was conducted improperly.  This is not a suit against the State of Illinois but rather, a complaint against the Business Enterprise Program under the department.
Conclusion
The application process was properly conducted, and the claim is not valid because damages cannot be legally awarded.
DISCUSSION:
On July 5, 2022, following the Administrative Law Judge’s decision, Complainant Eddie Conway filed for arbitration pursuant to Sections 5(b) and 6 of the Randolph-Sheppard Act with the Department of Education.  The complainant specified the reasons for the appeal.  The facts alleged and alleged violations were considered, and decision was made that an ad hoc arbitration panel be convened.  In regard thereto, the Secretary of the Department of Education, hereinafter referred to as Secretary, authorized the convening of the instant panel “. . . to hear and render a decision on the issues raised in the complaint.  The central issues to be addressed are whether the BEP violated the Act and implementing regulations by awarding Site 435 to a vendor who did not disclose his affiliation with the ICBV on his application, created a “windfall location” by moving from two vendors to one for Site 435, and by not following the bidding process established in the BEP’s Administrative Code.”
There were many exhibits offered and positions and arguments made throughout this proceeding, but what must be made abundantly clear is that the arbitrators, hereinafter referred to as the panel, are limited to the issues for which the panel was convened.  That is the scope of the panel’s jurisdiction.  At times the line of demarcation is blurred, but the panel must be mindful and limit their decision to the issues raised in the complaint and defined by the Secretary in her letter impaneling the undersigned arbitrators.  Also, as stated earlier, there were no witnesses offered at the arbitration hearing.  The parties agreed to enter into evidence the entire voluminous record compiled in the Complainant’s grievance hearing before the Department of Human Services, Business Enterprise Program for the Blind as well as their own exhibits.
Objections to Exhibits
The Department objected to Complainant’s Exhibits 1 – 9 based on relevancy or conflict of interest. The panel admits the Exhibits into evidence and will address the issues raised by the Department in their decision if needed.
Omission Issue
From the beginning, the sole issue raised by Complainant was that the person who was awarded Site 435, Donald Anderson, failed to include in his bid application, as required, that he was a member serving on the ICBV.  It is Complainant’s position that Anderson in his role at ICBV worked closely with the Bureau of Blind Services (BBS).  On the selection Committee, at the time, was the Acting Administrator of BBS, Paul Drake.  Complainant argues that Anderson’s affiliation with ICBV (meetings, etc.) influenced the Committee in awarding Anderson Site 435.
The Panel agrees with Complainant that Anderson was required to and should have disclosed his affiliation with ICBV on his application.  The issue, however, is whether the lack of disclosure was sufficient to affect the bid winner’s (Anderson) selection.
The Panel agrees that the situation on its face - Anderson’s affiliation, selection, and later transactions - raises reasonable questions and concerns about its impact on his selection.  However, to conclude from that, alone, that Anderson’s selection was based on his affiliation is mere speculation and conjecture.  There must be sufficient supporting evidence to turn speculation into fact.  The Majority has thoroughly reviewed the record and finds no such evidence.   The only evidence on the issue is the testimony of Drake and Juan Ortiz, the Vocational Rehabilitation Counselor, who was also a member of the committee.  Drake testified that he had little contact with ICBV.  Ortiz testified he could not recall any knowledge of Anderson’s affiliation or any discussion about applicants being members of ICBV.  The Majority finds Ortiz’s testimony to be credible.  While one may reasonably argue it is self-serving, there simply is no evidence to counter his testimony.   With respect to Drake, Complainant asserts that he is not a credible witness because he gave false testimony in a prior proceeding before Hearing Office Crowder.  Specifically, he stated, when asked, that Anderson had given up his other facilities (referring to the Statesville and Markham facilities).  In the opinion of the Majority, even assuming Drake to be incredible and find that Anderson’s affiliation with ICBV influenced his selection, the outcome on the omission issue remains the same.  Importantly, Drake was only one of four on the selection committee.  So, even without considering Drakes scores, the three others on the committee selected Anderson.  They all ranked Anderson number one.  They all acted independently.  There is no evidence that the committee members met and collaborated before grading the applicants.
Based on the above, Complainant has not established by the preponderance of the evidence that Anderson’s affiliation with ICBV influenced the selection Committee to award Site 435 to Anderson.  The omission itself is not sufficient to find a violation.  However, for transparency reasons, Anderson should have included in his application his membership in ICBV.
Bidding Process
The bidding process, as established at the prior hearing before the Department of Human Services, BEPB, is as follows.  The selection Committee is comprised of two blind vendors (that have been trained in the selection process), a rehabilitation counselor, and a supervisor, (1st day Tr. 55).  The Committee reviews the applications, sales and business data of each applicant provided by the accounting firm, and performance evaluations provided in advance of the interviews.  The Committee then conducts interviews with the applicants.  The questions asked are pre-determined.  Each member asks two questions.  Generally speaking, brief answers are not as informative as more lengthy answers in grading the applicant.  Complainant’s answers were brief. Anderson’s and some of the others, were lengthier. Once answered, the interviewer cannot ask follow-up questions, (1st day Tr. 58).  After each interview, the interviewer grades the applicant based on the interview, bid application and sales data and performance evaluations.  Based on the same, a grade is given to each category on the application, (1st day Tr. 68).
The Majority finds that in the instant case, there is no evidence that the normal bid process was not followed in the bid of site 435.  The complainant’s primary position is that the application process was archaic.  It cites as an example; Complainant was rated a 3 for Personal Hygiene and Anderson a score of 5.  It is argued that using a scoring mechanism that assesses “Personal Hygiene” is a classic example that the application process for Site 435 is profoundly obsolete and caused harm to her overall performance score.  The Majority agrees that at first glance the Complainant’s complaint seems to be reasonable.  However, that category was explained to cover both the individual and the facility, (2nd day Tr. 24).  This makes sense since the individual is in contact with the public and the facility is frequented by the public.  What wasn’t explained is how the Committee was able to make a grade as a result of the interview, or at least of the facility.  In the final analysis, however, while this is a category that may need to be changed, it cannot be concluded that it affected the outcome of the bidding process or that the entire bid process is archaic.
Also, in the bid process, it is alleged that Complainant’s performance evaluations were minimized throughout her lengthy employment and those scores appear to impact her Site 435 evaluations.  The Majority, however, finds no evidence to support the claim.  To begin with, there is no evidence that she was treated any differently than the other applicants.  Only the last performance evaluation was used for all applicants. It is true that Complainant’s previous evaluations, (Complainant Exh. 9), were the same as the current one used - they were all were “meet expectations.”  It is argued it shows that her evaluations were never updated.  This may be one conclusion, but it may be this was an accurate evaluation of her performance.
Lastly, Complainant alleges a violation of IDHS 9.2.3 occurred when periodic review failed to include remedies or prescriptive language associated with the impact of COVID-19 that led to her low summary score.  The Majority finds that this, as well as Complainant’s performance evaluation complaint, are really not a bid process issue.  The Committee’s job was to deal with what they had.  The issues of performance evaluations and 9.2.3 deficiencies were out of their control and not within their purview to address.  Those are matters the Complainant could have addressed when they arose.
Based on the above, the Majority does not find that the bid process itself or the application and scoring by the Committee negatively impacted Complainant’s scores as alleged.
Windfall Location
It is the Complainant’s position that the consolidation of two floors of the Chicago Main Post Office was a tremendous element of administrative malfeasance when it created Bid 435.  Complainant’s main contention is that 1) the consolidation was done without a formal study, and 2) it created a windfall for Anderson, the successful bid winner.
As to the lack of formal study, the Majority finds that there is no requirement for such a study.  Complainant cites IDHS 7.3.3 in support of its position of a required formal study.  However, said section is abundantly clear and only addresses and applies to unprofitable facilities.  Here, both of the units that were combined were profitable (Complainant Exh. 19).  Therefore, there was no violation of IDHS 7.3.3.
Also, Complainant argues that the Department by creating Bid 435 created a windfall location for Anderson.  In the opinion of the Majority, it is difficult to define what a windfall is from the record.  For instance, there is no record evidence of what the average income of the vendors is for comparison purposes which would be helpful.  Complainant in her brief, however, claims the average is $51,726.  The Panel majority will assume this to be an accurate figure.
Bid 435 combined the first and third floors of the Chicago Main Post Office. The first-floor income for 2020-2021 was projected at approximately $21,500.  The third floor at approximately $75,500, (Complainant Exh. 19).  The Majority agrees that adding $21,500 to the third-floor income was significant.  But on the other hand - based on Complainant’s calculation of the average vendor income - the floor one income is approximately one-half of the average.  Under the circumstances, it appears to be a reasonable business decision by BEPB to combine the two instead of bidding them separately.  In any event, while Anderson ended up with a substantial increase in his earnings, the Majority cannot conclude it was a windfall under the circumstances.  The addition of Floor1 was the addition of a unit earning one-half of the average.
There were other transactions (Statesville and the Markham facilities) that Anderson was involved in with the Department as alleged by Complainant; one that involved a service agreement.  These were facilities that Anderson was allowed to continue that contributed to the windfall.  The Department’s position is that under the Act the Department has the authority to “do all things necessary and proper to carry out its mission,” including entering into agreements.
With respect to the above issue, the Majority notes that 1) this issue was not listed as one of the reasons in Complainant’s appeal to arbitration, and 2) this issue does not change the outcome of the main issue in this case which is whether Anderson’s selection by the Department violated the Act.  As discussed, and found earlier by the Majority, it did not.
Based on the above facts and discussion thereon, the Panel majority renders the following
Conclusion
The preponderance of the evidence establishes that 1) the BEP did not violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act or the Illinois Blind Services Act by awarding site 435 to a vendor who did not disclose his affiliation with ICBV, 2) that BEP followed the bidding process established in the BEP’s Administrative Code, and 3) that BEP did not create a windfall location by moving from two vendors to one for site 435.
