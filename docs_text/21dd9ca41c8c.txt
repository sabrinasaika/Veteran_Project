SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (Section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to 
Section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by Illinois Division of 
Rehabilitation Services (DRS). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the process by 
which RSA carried out its activities from August 6, 2024, through August 8, 2024, was defined 
by information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, documents, and 
data submitted by DRS, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, and technical 
assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
• Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities;   
• Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

2 
achieve continuous improvement in assisting individuals with disabilities to achieve 
quality employment outcomes; 
• Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities, 
including students and youth with disabilities; 
• Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported 
Employment programs and recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the 
VR agency’s financial management of fiscal resources and strategies for maximizing the 
use of Federal grant funds;  
• Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and 
procedures, including the development or revision of policies and internal controls 
supporting effective and efficient program operations, service delivery, and data-based 
decision making; and  
• Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic 
actions to improve and maximize program and fiscal performance and the use of Federal 
funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops 
strategic actions to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds 
to achieve quality employment outcomes.  
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE
  
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by DRS on the Case Service Report (RSA-911) 
and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 2021, 
2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and requested 
documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR services, 
quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as discussions 
with DRS of potential factors influencing program performance informed the analysis and helped 
to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the performance and 
management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by DRS, information from related sources, 
and discussions with DRS during the review, RSA identified various factors, which may be 
contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s performance leading to 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of Federal 
funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to DRS’ 
requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by DRS as it develops 
strategies and practices to ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to 
maximize the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with 
disabilities in achieving quality employment outcomes. 
 
Inaccurate Reporting and Statewide Availability of Pre-Employment Transition Services 
 
DRS reported providing 330,153 pre-employment transition services to 23,981 students with 
disabilities in PY 2020, which increased to 449,019 services to 28,006 students in PY 2022. DRS 
served over three quarters of reported students each year during the review period. DRS had

4 
determined most students with disabilities (22,873 in PY 2020, 26,972 in PY 2021, and 26,243 in 
PY 2022) eligible for VR services, with the small remaining numbers representing potentially 
eligible students. Notably, approximately 77 percent of eligible individuals served by DRS are 
under age 25. 
 
Ensuring pre-employment transition services are available statewide to all students with 
disabilities regardless of whether the student applied or has been determined eligible for VR 
services promotes equitable access to services and complies with Federal requirements. DRS 
delivers pre-employment transition services primarily through Secondary Transitional 
Experience Program (STEP) contracts with local school districts, which have been in place since 
the 1960s. Participating centers for independent living (CILs) provide pre-employment transition 
services to potentially eligible students through Fast Track contracts. DRS reported that not all 
CILs provide pre-employment transition services through Fast Track contracts, and not all school 
systems have STEP contracts. DRS acknowledged service gaps in some regions of the State and 
provided RSA with a spreadsheet detailing service availability. According to the spreadsheet, 
Fast Track services are available in 60 of the 102 counties in Illinois and STEP contracted 
services are available in 94 of the 102 counties. However, in Iroquois, Marion, and Scott 
counties both Fast Track and Step services are unavailable. Ensuring the availability and access 
to pre-employment transition services across the State of Illinois to students with disabilities who 
are eligible or potentially eligible will help to support meaningful postsecondary transition and 
ultimately successful employment. 
 
According to DRS, in rural areas of the State, or in regions without these contracts, VR 
counselors may deliver pre-employment transition services. However, when RSA requested an 
example case involving a potentially eligible student receiving pre-employment transition 
services provided by VR counselors, no such case could be identified in the case management 
system (CMS). During the period of review, DRS did not report the provision of any in-house 
pre-employment transition services on the RSA-911, only those services purchased through 
vendors. Additionally, DRS reported that there is no mechanism in the agency’s CMS to track 
and report staff time providing pre-employment transition services. Therefore, DRS is unable to 
demonstrate that it is making pre-employment transition services available statewide, which is 
discussed in Finding 2.2.    
 
DRS vendors have access to the agency’s CMS and enter case notes, which automatically 
generate billings that are subsequently paid by the VR counselor (See Findings 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3 
for additional details related to pre-employment transition services authorization and billing 
processes). RSA inquired about the agency’s mechanisms to ensure contractors deliver and 
students engage in reported pre-employment transition services to which DRS responded that, in 
most cases, it relies on trust in contractors to fulfill service delivery obligations. DRS also 
mentioned that it is not familiar with the development of contractor curricula or programming 
and does not provide input in these areas.  
 
DRS indicated that most students express interest in work opportunities. RSA inquired about the 
high number of reported on-the-job trainings (OJTs) in Illinois (5,385 reported in PY 2020, 
6,004 in PY 2021, and 4,406 in PY 2022). DRS reported that work-based learning experiences

5 
were misclassified as OJTs, as referenced in the STEP contracts, and this resulted in inaccurate 
reporting in the CMS.

invoice requirements. 
• RSA shared that, in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(7)(b), DRS must establish 
written processes that include a mechanism for reviewing procedures to determine 
effectiveness. These processes must specifically detail how staff time is allocated to meet 
the reservation requirement between authorized and required services, in accordance with 
2 C.F.R. § 200.430 and 431. In addition, these written processes must specify where staff 
time charged to the reserve is reported within the RSA-17. The associated costs must also

documentation for support of the RSA-17 (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(3)).

regarding the continued use of random time sample studies as the internal control method 
for tracking agency personnel time. This approach ensures that only expenditures for 
agency personnel time dedicated to providing pre-employment transition services to 
students with disabilities are accurately reported in the CMS. When asked about the 
validity of this methodology for assigning actual staff time to the appropriate work center, 
DRS responded that it has historically relied on this tracking system. DRS indicated that 
random time sample studies align with state standards for personnel activity reporting and 
that it has received approval from the cognizant agency responsible for reviewing and

Insufficient VR Program Policies and Procedures  
 
RSA found that in DRS’ policies and procedures, in effect during the three-year review period, 
some administrative rules have not been updated since 2006 (e.g., administrative code 557.30, 
Application for Vocational Rehabilitation.) Additionally, RSA found discrepancies between the 
customer contact procedures (e.g., the number of participant contacts VR counselors are required 
to make) outlined in the VR Casework Procedure Manual and the VR Directives. Inconsistencies 
such as these may lead to confusion among staff regarding which guidance to follow. Addressing 
policy and procedural discrepancies could improve both the consistency of service delivery and 
the accuracy of program data. For example, setting clear expectations for participant contacts can 
lead to better engagement of participants. Additionally, RSA identified insufficiencies, and in 
some cases, non-compliance, in written policies and procedures covering the nature and scope of 
each of the VR services, in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.50.  
Finally, DRS’ VR Policy Directive, VR-008 Community College Initiative permits 
DRS to support the costs of attendance at public community colleges for VR 
participants without applying the agency’s financial need analysis and its cost 
participation policies. However, the policy applies financial need and cost participation 
policies for participants attending four-year degree programs and any postsecondary 
training programs. Though this policy is permissible, DRS must ensure that an 
individual’s cost participation in postsecondary and vocational training of any type 
takes into consideration any disability-related expenses paid by the individual and is not 
so high as to deny an individual necessary postsecondary education and vocational 
training (34 C.F.R. 361.54). Importantly, DRS should guard against VR counselors 
applying the policy in such a way that steers individuals toward pursuing employment 
goals that require only community college education or in other ways that discourage an 
individual from pursuing employment goals of their choice that require more advanced 
levels of education (e.g., four-year college and graduate-level education), as well as 
other types of vocational training because of a lack of financial support from DRS.

Case Service Record Deficiencies: WIOA Performance Indicators and Pre-Employment 
Transition Services 
 
From PY 2022, RSA reviewed five cases wherein DRS reported the participant earned an MSG 
and five cases wherein DRS reported the participant earned a credential. This included two cases 
where participants were enrolled in secondary education, two cases where participants were 
enrolled in postsecondary education, and one case involving both secondary and postsecondary 
education. Additionally, RSA reviewed ten cases where DRS provided pre-employment 
transition services to students with disabilities. The purpose of the case service record review 
was to ensure MSG, credential attainment, and pre-employment transition services were 
accurately reported and supported by the required documentation. RSA found the following 
issues during its review— 
 
• Insufficient supporting documentation to verify MSG and credential attainment; 
• Inconsistent reporting of MSG dates;

9 
• No written internal control processes for data collection and validation of MSG and 
credential attainment; 
• Incorrect coding of pre-employment transition services provider types; and 
• Incorrect expenditure reporting of pre-employment transition services funds. 
 
In some instances, DRS lacked the necessary supporting documentation for MSG, and where 
documentation was provided, the dates recorded in the files did not match those reported to RSA.  
DRS provided transcripts as evidence of participants earning an MSG; however, RSA found that 
some participants had failed their classes and should not have been counted as earning an MSG. 
In one case record, RSA identified that the high school diploma was missing from the file and 
credential attainment was based solely on self-reported information in the case notes and post-
exit employment status through the Unemployment Insurance (UI) data system interface in its 
CMS. Additionally, RSA noted that the agency erroneously reported credential attainment 
because the individual was not employed in any quarter for over a year after exiting the VR 
program. In a case involving postsecondary education, DRS incorrectly reported to RSA that the 
participant had earned a bachelor’s degree, even though the individual had not graduated from 
the university. There was also a case with inconsistent credential attainment dates between the 
DRS case file and what DRS reported on the RSA-911.  
 
In the review of pre-employment transition services cases, RSA found inconsistencies in how 
DRS reported provider types. In some instances, DRS labeled the same provider as public and 
private. This issue could stem from a CMS mapping error between the data and IT teams, 
indicating the need for more robust internal controls. As mentioned previously, collaboration is 
needed with the Illinois Department of Innovation and Technology, the entity responsible for 
maintaining the mapping and coding of DRS’ CMS, to develop a policy, procedures, and internal 
controls on outlining how they collaborate and maintain data accuracy to fix coding issues or 
gaps that prevent the proper collection and recording of VR services. Additionally, RSA found 
that DRS reported several purchased services with a $0 expenditure. Although the case 
management field showed the expenditure as paid, DRS explained that it could only enter the 
dollar amount once the provider cleared the payment. DRS must develop clear policies and 
procedures to ensure accurate reporting of pre-employment transition services funds on the RSA-
911. A more detailed discussion on the incorrect reporting of pre-employment transition services 
is provided in Finding 3.2.  
 
Missing supporting documentation and inaccurate reporting negatively affect data accuracy and 
thus, agency performance related to the WIOA performance indicators and pre-employment 
transition services.   
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA provided technical assistance on key internal controls needed to meet WIOA 
indicators. RSA also emphasized the importance of establishing clear policies and 
procedures to ensure that training and education programs meet the definitions of MSG 
and credential attainment and explained that supporting documentation must be included 
in case records. These controls must check the supporting documentation and prevent 
reporting for participants not enrolled in recognized programs. RSA also advised DRS

10 
that internal controls should ensure all exit and post-exit data are appropriately tracked, 
using data validation procedures and the required documentation. Internal controls should 
detect any outliers or incorrect data. For example, when a VR program reports someone 
employed at exit or post-exit, the controls should ensure that wages are reported on the 
RSA-911 when necessary. 
• RSA clarified the documentation required when reporting MSGs that may have been 
earned during on-the-job training, customized training, and apprenticeships. By 
documenting these additional MSG, DRS will be better positioned to show progress and 
meet Federal reporting requirements.  
• RSA encouraged the agency to review the Case Service Report (RSA-911)  RSA-PD-19-
03 training, which offers essential guidance on how to document training and education 
programs in line with credential and MSG definitions.  
• RSA advised DRS of the value of providing routine staff training on documenting and 
reporting MSG and credential attainment. Keeping up with best practices, new 
regulations, and updated assessment tools helps staff track progress accurately, provide 
targeted support, and ensure participants achieve better employment outcomes. 
• RSA clarified that unofficial high school transcripts may be used to document MSG; 
however, transcripts with an ‘F’ or failure cannot be counted as an MSG if a participant 
fails an entire semester. In reviewing documentation, DRS must verify that the transcript 
or report card indicates interim progress that meets the applicable academic standard to 
count as an MSG. For example, if a student fails one course but passes several others, 
they may still have the academic credits necessary to make interim progress. 
Additionally, unofficial high school transcripts may be used to document credential 
attainment, including a graduation date.

Inaccurate VR Service Reporting 
 
During the review period, DRS did not report certain data elements under career services. For 
example, DRS did not report assessment in all program years, which DRS explained as being 
due to the CMS not capturing this option. In PYs 2020 and 2021, DRS reported no job placement 
assistance, supported employment, or customized employment services. DRS stated that these 
services were either unavailable or did not have corresponding boxes for staff to select the 
service in its CMS. As a result, these data elements were not reported on the RSA-911. 
Additionally, RSA noted that DRS was missing a sufficient quality assurance process to ensure 
data validity during the period of review. 
 
RSA also observed that, across all program years reviewed, 13.6 percent of participants were 
categorized as receiving “other services.” DRS clarified that this data element captures books, 
supplies, tools, job-related equipment, and uniforms, and applies this data element to record 
interpreter services. RSA noted significant issues with how data were recorded across some VR

11 
services. For instance, DRS did not correctly report career services and job placement assistance, 
leading to no reporting across all program years reviewed. Addressing these discrepancies will 
give the agency a more accurate understanding of its performance.

• RSA advised DRS to report the appropriate type of exit. RSA clarified that the agency 
must ensure that VR counselors and other personnel are knowledgeable about all exit 
types and reasons for exit options available in the RSA-911.  
• RSA explained the role of the statistical adjustment model in performance assessment 
and emphasized the importance of accurate data reporting, including barriers to 
employment, enrollment data, outcome data, and service provision data, to RSA for a

• RSA advised DRS to develop written policies and procedures that outline DRS’ quality 
assurance process to verify the accuracy, validity, and reliability of data.   
 
Recommendations 
 
• DRS should collaborate with the Illinois Department of Innovation and Technology to 
ensure its CMS accurately captures all RSA-911 data elements. DRS should also conduct 
a full data mapping review to ensure all required RSA-911 data elements are accurately 
matched and integrated into its system. 
• DRS should develop and implement a formal definition for the ‘all other reasons’ exit 
code. Establishing a clear and consistent definition will help ensure accurate reporting,

• DRS should provide regular training for VR counselors and staff to ensure they input data 
correctly and select the appropriate options in the CMS. Additionally, DRS should 
conduct regular quality assurance reviews to identify and correct any discrepancies.  
 
Participant Attrition 
 
The number of participants who exited at various stages of the VR process included 9,534 
participants in PY 2020, 12,305 participants in PY 2021, and 11,672 participants in PY 2022. 
The percentage of participants exiting the VR program after eligibility determination before an 
IPE fluctuated over the three-year period from 6.9 percent in PY 2020 to 5.9 percent in PY 2021 
and 6.0 percent in PY 2022. Data also show the percentage of participants exiting after an IPE 
without an employment outcome increased from 41.6 percent in PY 2020 to 44.9 percent in PY 
2021 and 42.2 percent in PY 2022. The reasons for exit across PYs 2020, 2021, and 2022 
included: “unable to locate” (26.0 percent, 27.9 percent, and 28.6 percent, respectively); “no 
longer interested in receiving services or further services” (21.6 percent, 22.3 percent, and 21.7 
percent, respectively); and all other reasons (9.1 percent, 8.7 percent, and 9.0 percent, 
respectively). 
 
These trends highlight areas where DRS’ performance can improve. Many participants are 
exiting the VR program without achieving employment outcomes, raising concerns about the 
agency’s ability to engage and retain participants effectively throughout the VR process.

12 
Additionally, high exit rates for reasons such as ‘unable to locate’ and ‘no longer interested in 
receiving services’ suggest opportunities to strengthen case management practices, participant 
engagement strategies, and service delivery. Addressing these issues will help reduce premature 
exits and improve participant outcomes while ultimately enhancing DRS’ overall performance.  
 
DRS staff attributed premature exits to the agency’s increased focus on youth with disabilities, 
resulting in less engagement with other participants, particularly adults, and noted that DRS’ 
financial needs policy discourages continued participation in training services. For instance, the 
agency’s policy allowed the financial needs assessment to be waived for participants pursuing a 
community college degree; however, those choosing to continue toward a bachelor’s degree 
were unable to continue services because they could not meet their financial participation 
requirements. DRS’ quality assurance team plans to review closed case files to identify common 
themes to inform targeted training to VR counselors. 
 
Technical Assistance: 
 
• RSA informed DRS that studying attrition rates can provide valuable insights into 
mitigating participant attrition and improving the overall effectiveness of the VR 
program. Studying attrition can help to identify which groups of participants (e.g., 
students with disabilities) are most at risk of exiting the VR program. DRS can target 
those participants for extra support or address systemic issues through policy and 
procedural revisions.
 Additionally, DRS can support VR counselors and other frontline 
staff by removing, revising, or restructuring burdensome policies, practices, or alignment

Finding 2.1 Insufficient Reporting of Pre-Employment Transition Services 
 
Issue: Does DRS report accurate data to RSA regarding students with disabilities receiving pre-
employment transition services in accordance with Sections 13, 14, 101(a)(10) and 113 of the 
Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.40(a). 
 
Requirement: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. 2 C.F.R. § 200.303 requires that VR agencies 
develop an internal controls process to provide a reasonable assurance regarding the achievement 
of objectives in the effectiveness and efficiency of operations, and reliability of reporting for 
internal and external use; and that this process is established and implemented as a measure of 
checks and balances to ensure proper expenditures of funds, including the evaluation and 
monitoring of compliance with statutes, regulations and the terms and conditions of Federal 
awards. 
 
Analysis: RSA found that DRS has not developed comprehensive policies, procedures, and 
internal controls to facilitate the accurate tracking and reporting of required pre-employment 
transition services and coordination activities. RSA could not determine how the agency verifies 
whether students receive appropriate services from STEP and Fast Track contractors, as there 
appears to be an ineffective mechanism to ensure service provision before agency payment. DRS 
vendors have access to the agency’s CMS and enter case notes, which automatically generate 
billings that are subsequently paid by the VR counselor. DRS indicated no other mechanisms to 
ensure contractors deliver and students engage in reported pre-employment transition services. 
DRS indicated that it generally trusts contractors to fulfill service delivery obligations.  
 
Additionally, RSA noted the misclassification of work-based learning experiences as on-the-job 
training (OJT) in the STEP and Fast Track contracts and RSA-911 reporting. RSA inquired 
about the high number of OJTs (5,385 OJTs reported in PY 2020, 6,004 in PY 2021, and 4,406 
in PY 2022). DRS reported that work-based learning experiences were misclassified as OJTs.

15 
The STEP contracts referenced OJTs and, according to staff, contributed to the miscoding of this 
data element in the agency’s CMS.  
 
DRS also did not report any VR agency staff provided pre-employment transition services on the 
RSA-911, only services purchased through vendors. The agency reported that its CMS lacks a 
mechanism to track and report direct staff time spent on pre-employment transitions services. 
Further, during the case record review, RSA identified inaccuracies in the reporting of RSA-911 
data, including vendor type and expenditure of funds. RSA informed DRS that robust processes 
and internal controls are necessary to ensure accurate reporting and to identify data anomalies 
related to the RSA-911, including those concerning the provision of pre-employment transition 
services. 
 
Conclusion: In accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.12, DRS does not implement internal controls 
and policies and procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of the VR 
program, including those necessary to carry out all functions for which the VR agency is

ensure that it is submitting accurate and verifiable reports in the form and level of detail required 
for students receiving pre-employment transition services. Based on the review of DRS’ existing 
policies, procedures, and internal controls related to pre-employment transition services, RSA

C.F.R. § 200.303.

Finding 3.1 Obligations and Expenditures Not Properly Assigned to Correct Period of 
Performance 
Issues: Does DRS meet obligation and expenditure requirements in 2 C.F.R. § 200.71 and 34 
C.F.R. § 76.707. Does DRS assign obligations and expenditures to the correct Federal award in 
accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.12, 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.77, 200.302, 200.303(a), 200.309, and 34 
C.F.R. § 76.702. 
Requirement: As a recipient of Federal VR and Supported Employment program funds, DRS 
must have procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of its VR and Supported 
Employment programs and enable DRS to carry out all required functions, including financial 
reporting (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). In accordance with the Uniform Guidance in 2 C.F.R.  
§ 200.302(a), a State’s financial management systems, including records documenting 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be 
sufficient to permit the preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and 
conditions; and the tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such 
funds have been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions 
of the Federal award. The Uniform Guidance requires the financial management system of each 
non-Federal entity to provide for the identification, in its accounts, of all Federal awards received 
and expended and the Federal programs under which they were received (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)). 
In addition, Education Department General Administrative Regulations (EDGAR) at 34 C.F.R.  
§ 76.702 require States to use fiscal control and fund accounting procedures that ensure proper 
disbursement of and accounting for Federal funds. 
 
Each grant award has a defined “period of performance,” which is the time during which the 
non-Federal entity may incur new obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal 
award (2 C.F.R. § 200.77). A non-Federal entity may only charge to the Federal award allowable 
costs incurred during the period of performance (2 C.F.R. § 200.309, see also EDGAR 34 C.F.R. 
§§ 76.703 and 76.709). Recipients must implement internal controls necessary to ensure 
obligations and expenditures for a Federal award are assigned, tracked, recorded, and reported 
within the applicable period of performance for that Federal award, thereby ensuring the 
recipients are managing the award in compliance with Federal requirements (2 C.F.R.  
§ 200.303(a)). The proper assignment of Federal and non-Federal funds to the correct period of 
performance is necessary for DRS to correctly account for VR and Supported Employment 
program funds so that RSA can be assured that the agency has satisfied requirements for, among

27 
other things, match (34 C.F.R. § 361.60), MOE (34 C.F.R. § 361.62), and the reservation and 
expenditure of VR funds for the provision of pre-employment transition services (34 C.F.R.  
§ 361.65(a)(3)).  
A financial obligation means “orders placed for property and services, contracts and subawards 
made, and similar transactions during a given period that require payment by the non-Federal 
entity during the same or a future period." (2 C.F.R. § 200.71). For expenditures to be allowable 
under the Federal award, agencies must demonstrate that the obligation occurred within the 
period of performance of the Federal award. EDGAR regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 76.707 explain 
when a State incurs an obligation for various kinds of services and property. Expenditures must 
be for payment of actual obligations. Obligations must be charged to a Federal award and must 
occur within the appropriate period of performance. Therefore, in order to properly account for 
and liquidate expenditures, recipients must be able to assign an obligation to a Federal award 
based upon the date the obligation was made (34 C.F.R. §§ 76.703 and 76.709). 
Analysis: During the review, RSA and DRS discussed the agency’s ability to track and report 
obligations in its systems. In preparation for the review effort, RSA transmitted a fiscal 
document request to learn more about the agency’s policies and procedures for the topic areas 
identified in the MTAG. The document entitled “Section IV Financial Management of the State 
VR and Supported Employment Programs Period of Performance” contains some period of 
performance information, but it is incomplete, not organized, and does not identify who is 
responsible for specific processes and procedures. RSA asked DRS to clearly identify and 
explain how each expenditure is assigned and DRS staff confirmed that their systems do not 
allow them to assign expenditures to the correct period of performance. A further review of the 
responses to the questions, the policies, and the financial data the agency submitted for the 
period of performance topic area confirmed DRS does not obligate and expend expenditures in 
compliance with the period of performance requirements. 
DRS uses multiple systems to manage the grant funding. The systems include a legacy CMS 
which is separate from the accounting system used by DRS. DRS’ accounting system, Systems 
Applications and Products (SAP) is different from the accounting system used by the DSA. SAP 
does have a nightly feed into the DSA’s accounting system. The approved authorizations are 
housed in the Client Payment Systems (CPS) which go into the “Hub”. DRS cannot confirm 
whether this is the old Central Accounting Reporting System (CARS). The “Hub” validates the 
expenditures for the DSA then the information goes to SAP and then is forwarded to the DSA’s 
comptrollers accounting system. There is no connection between the CPS and the virtual CMS. 
The SAP version DRS uses does not maintain necessary contract history or capture accurate 
dates of obligation. While the CMS can record the dates of obligation based on the date of 
authorization, RSA observed the CPS frequently assigning obligations based on the dates of 
services provision. RSA is very concerned about this lack of data integrity. The fiscal staff in 
DRS downloads monthly data about the contracts, but this does not account for any day-to-day 
changes. The internal controls in place to capture the history appear to be missing (e.g., process 
for downloading data and who is responsible). 
While DRS states it has the capacity to track how and when funds are obligated and expended, 
DRS expressed uncertainty surrounding whether their computerized accounting system has the

28 
ability to properly identify the appropriate period of performance. RSA asked how DRS verified 
the data consistency between the CPS and the CMS to ensure DRS accurately assigns 
expenditures to the correct period of performance. DRS confirmed their system does not allow it 
to track period of performance requirements nor does it have a written process or procedure for 
how the systems worked together in compliance with the period of performance requirements. 
In January 2024, RSA received an email from DRS inquiring about retroactively approving 
approximately 25 cases in which verbal approvals were used to approve the provision of 
services. RSA reviewed the request and determined that DRS would be unable to use Federal 
share to pay for the services since the services were obligated outside the periods of performance 
of the award to which DRS wanted to assign the expenditures (2 C.F.R. § 200.1 “Period of 
Performance”). RSA also heard during a meeting with DRS staff on August 6, 2024, that 
supervisors use verbal understandings as a key component to authorize payment of vendor 
invoices. RSA advised that verbal authorizations do not meet the Federal requirements. RSA 
discussed with DRS the need for formalized written policies and procedures for authorizations. 
As a recipient of Federal funds, DRS must ensure proper stewardship of those funds to protect 
the Federal interest and ensure funds are used in accordance with Federal and State requirements. 
Verbal authorizations do not provide the same measure of protection of Federal funds as written 
authorizations. With written authorization there is a clear record of which services are being 
provided, the frequency, intensity and duration of the service, etc. Acceptance of the written 
authorization by the provider constitutes acceptance of the terms and conditions for providing the 
service in accordance with Federal requirements. Therefore, the use of verbal authorizations 
should be limited to emergency situations and the verbal authorization should be followed-up 
with a written authorization as soon as possible. 
RSA discussed with DRS the need for formalized written policies and procedures for period of 
performance and Federal reporting to ensure that obligations are assigned and tracked in 
accordance with EDGAR and Uniform Guidance requirements and reported accurately on 
Federal financial reports. RSA discussed the requirement of accurate Federal reporting of 
obligations, expenditures, and unobligated Federal and non-Federal funds, as this information is 
necessary for RSA to determine the extent to which VR agencies meet non-Federal share 
requirements of match and MOE, as well as determining whether awards qualify for a carryover 
year and extension to the period of performance. Without accurate information submitted on 
RSA-17 and SF-425 reports, RSA is unable to determine with certainty whether these 
requirements have been met.

procedures to assign, track, and report obligations and expenditures in systems for all cost 
categories identified in EDGAR 34 C.F.R. § 76.707, to ensure they are for allowable costs under 
the agency’s approved State Plan during the period of performance of the award; and 2) All non-
Federal and Federal obligations and expenditures are accurately assigned to specific fund 
sources, and tracked, paid and reported on the appropriate Federal Financial Reports for the 
appropriate awards.

Finding 3.2 Insufficient Internal Controls for Contracting and Data Collection  
Issue: Did DRS have sufficient contract monitoring and internal controls to establish that the 
funds expended were used for allowable purposes in accordance with Federal requirements. 
Requirement: DRS’ financial management system must be sufficient to permit, among other 
things, the tracing of funds to establish that the funds have been used for allowable purposes in 
accordance with Federal requirements (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a)). Under 2 C.F.R. § 200.303(a), 
States are required to “Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award 
that provides reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award.” Federal grant recipients must monitor grant-supported activities pursuant to 2 C.F.R.  
§ 200.329(a), which identifies monitoring responsibilities of the recipient for oversight, including 
compliance with all requirements covering each program, function, or activity. 
DRS assures in its Sate Plan that the methods of administration it uses will provide for proper 
and efficient administration of the VR program. These methods must include procedures to 
ensure accurate data collection and financial accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). Except where 
otherwise authorized by statute, costs are allowable with Federal program funds only if they are 
necessary and reasonable for the performance of the Federal award and allocable to that award in 
accordance with Federal cost principles at 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403 through 200.405. 
Analysis: DRS administers contracts to facilitate the provision of certain VR purchased services 
for individuals with disabilities. DRS’ contracts include Third-Party Cooperative Arrangements 
(TPCAs). RSA observed that the contracts and related internal controls DRS uses for service

31 
provision did not include the details necessary to ensure contractors report relevant cost and 
service delivery data to DRS in a manner that assures reliability of the reported data. Similarly, 
DRS’ contract monitoring and invoice payment internal controls are insufficient to meet

Based on RSA’s review, DRS does not maintain and operationalize adequate written internal 
controls regarding factors DRS staff are to consider when determining whether contractor

RSA found the following deficiencies in DRS processes involving monitoring and internal

Questionable Documentation Supporting Payments to Contractors  
DRS used VR funds to pay vendor invoices based only on vendor self-reported data. Based on 
information gathered from DRS staff during on-site interviews, DRS frontline supervisors and 
managers widely referred to the self-reported data vendors assign to invoices as “case notes.” 
Several DRS managers, including those with responsibility for approving final payment of 
invoices, explained that final payment approval was primarily “based on review of the case 
notes” that vendors self-reported. Notably, DRS staff also indicated front line staff regularly 
made payments based on unwritten “verbal” approval from their supervisors. This practice 
means little to no documentation is available to validate that the Federal funds paid were for an 
allowable service. DRS must ensure that supporting documentation includes the authorized 
service was provided in a manner consistent with DRS’ service guidelines, the IPE, and contract 
specifications. While contractors can submit the supporting documentation with the invoice, 
DRS’ must periodically monitor the documentation to ensure the validity and reliability of the 
invoices submitted. If DRS supervisors approve an invoice verbally in an emergency 
circumstance, there must be procedures to ensure the documentation required to support the 
payment is provided as soon as possible. Without such documentation, DRS is unable to 
establish that the funds expended were used for the provision of VR services. 
Consistent with the agency’s organizational chart, DRS assigned agency staff as “coordinators” 
to clusters of schools to provide coordination and support services to multiple sites. However, 
interviews with DRS coordinators who held the title of coordinator during the period under 
review provided little to no evidence that the staff engaged in formal, documented, and 
consistent monitoring or oversight of contract supported activities, including vendor self-
reported data. This information was consistent with RSA’s assessment of related documentation 
DRS provided. As noted above, DRS relied on “case notes” as the sole source of documentation 
in support of its decisions to approve payments to vendors.  
DRS’ reliance on vendor self-reported data as the basis for approving the use of VR funds to pay 
related invoices demonstrates an absence of regular, consistent monitoring by DRS of its vendor 
supported activities, thereby suggesting significant risk to the Federal interest, elevating RSA’s

Inaccurate and Missing Contract Language  
 
DRS’ contracts to provide pre-employment transition services through TPCAs were originally

Between the amendments to the Rehabilitation Act made by WIOA in 2014 and the start of the

Rehabilitation Act requires States to reserve and expend 15 percent of the Federal award 
allotment for the provision of pre-employment transition services. DRS attempted to quickly 
modify the long-existing service contracts to enable DRS to make progress toward meeting the 
pre-employment transition services requirement. 
 
Much of the language DRS includes in its current TPCA contracts is confusing and/or 
inaccurate. The language was carried over from the long-ended service contracts and remained

noted in Finding 2.1 above, STEP contracts referenced OJTs and, according to staff, contributed 
to the miscoding of this data element in the agency’s CMS. Additionally, the contracts identified 
no curriculum and did not specify how contractors were to implement service provision. 
 
DRS must update contract language and bring the statewide system into compliance with the 
Rehabilitation Act as amended by WIOA, and current VR and Uniform Guidance requirements. 
This necessitates a rewriting of TPCA contracts, revising relevant VR agency internal controls,

Inaccurate Reporting of RSA-911 Pre-Employment Transition Service Data 
DRS’ internal control processes must account for the collection of data elements required 
through the RSA-911. While vendors self-report data regarding their provision of pre-
employment transition services, their reporting is subject to little oversight or verification by 
DRS. In addition, RSA discovered DRS processes for recording and reporting DRS agency staff 
time used for the provision of pre-employment transition services also fell short of meeting VR 
program requirements. Of particular concern is DRS’ use of a Random Moment Time Study 
(RMTS) to obtain required pre-employment transition service data for staff providing pre-
employment transition services. 
 
Section 101(a)(10)(E), which permits the RSA Commissioner to collect certain data as either a 
complete count or through sampling, does not permit the sampling of VR service costs or the 
number of students with disabilities who received pre-employment transition services. The 
Rehabilitation Act, in section 101(a)(10), requires these data to be reported through a complete 
count (i.e., positively). Section 101(a)(10)(D)(i) requires VR agencies to report the cost incurred 
in providing VR services, including pre-employment transition services, both in terms of those 
services provided by VR agency staff and those services purchased from providers through a 
complete count. Further, section 101(a)(10)(C)(ii)(V) requires VR agencies to report the number 
of individuals served under the VR program, including the number of students with disabilities 
receiving pre-employment transition services, through a complete count. Therefore, DRS may 
not estimate the costs of services charged to the 15 percent pre-employment transition services 
reserve and DRS may not estimate which students with disabilities received these services. 
Through the positive reporting of these cost and service provision data, rather than using 
estimates, DRS will not only meet the statutory requirements, but it will be positioned to make 
more accurate data-informed decisions regarding its fiscal forecasting, the providers it uses to 
deliver pre-employment transition services, and the outcomes students with disabilities achieve. 
Finally, the Uniform Guidance requires VR agencies to have financial management systems that 
trace funds to a level of expenditure adequate to establish that such funds have been used 
according to the requirements of the Federal award and solely for authorized purposes (2 C.F.R. 
§ 200.302). Without a process that directly tracks the level of effort spent on providing or 
arranging pre-employment transition services, DRS is unable to ensure that all costs charged to 
the reserve are allocable and allowable based on the percentage approach resulting from the 
RMTS. DRS must ensure its internal control processes account for the proper reporting of RSA-
911 pre-employment transition service data, even when provided directly by staff.

transition service data.

Finding 3.3 Questioned Match from Third-Party Cooperative Arrangements 
Issue: Did DRS facilitate Third-Party Cooperative Arrangements (TPCAs) sufficient to generate 
allowable non-Federal share for the VR program in accordance with Federal requirements, 
including 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.28 and 76.50. 
Requirement: Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a), DSUs may enter into TPCAs to provide or 
contract for the provision of VR services with another State agency or local public agency that 
provides non-Federal share for VR agency use. To generate valid non-Federal share for the VR 
program, TPCAs must comply with the requirements of 34 C.F.R. § 361.28: 
(a) The designated State unit may enter into a third-party cooperative arrangement for 
providing or contracting for the provision of vocational rehabilitation services with 
another State agency or a local public agency that is providing part or all of the non-
Federal share in accordance with paragraph (c) of this section, if the designated State unit 
ensures that—  
(1) The services provided by the cooperating agency are not the customary or typical 
services provided by that agency but are new services that have a VR focus or existing 
services that have been modified, adapted, expanded, or reconfigured to have a VR focus;  
(2) The services provided by the cooperating agency are only available to applicants for, 
or recipients of, services from the DSU;  
(3) Program expenditures and staff providing services under the cooperative arrangement 
are under the administrative supervision of the DSU; and  
(4) All requirements of the vocational rehabilitation services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State plan, including a State’s order of selection, will apply to all services 
provided under the cooperative arrangement.  
 
(b) If a third-party cooperative agreement does not comply with the statewideness 
requirement in §361.25, the DSU must obtain a waiver of statewideness, in accordance 
with §361.26.  
 
(c) The cooperating agency's contribution toward the non-Federal share required under 
the arrangement, as set forth in paragraph (a) of this section, may be made through:  
(1) Cash transfers to the DSU;  
(2) Certified personnel expenditures for the time cooperating agency staff spent providing 
direct VR services pursuant to a TPCA that meets the requirements of this section. 
Certified personnel expenditures may include the allocable portion of staff salary and 
fringe benefits based upon the amount of time cooperating agency staff directly spent 
providing services under the arrangement; and  
(3) other direct expenditures incurred by the cooperating agency for the sole purpose of 
providing services under this section pursuant to a TPCA that—

36 
(i) Meets the requirements of this section;  
(ii) Are verifiable as being incurred under the TPCA; and  
(iii) Do not meet the definition of third-party in-kind contributions under 2 C.F.R. § 
200.96.  
 
Analysis: More than half of the total non-Federal share DRS reported as having met VR 
matching requirements during the scope of RSA’s review appears to lack sufficient evidence to 
substantiate qualification as valid allowable match under the VR award. Federal VR funds are 
made available to the VR agency only in proportion to the amount of non-Federal share the 
agency expends in a given FFY award’s year of appropriation according to the 21.3/78.7 ratio of 
non-Federal to Federal expenditures.  
 
RSA has concern over the allowability of the non-Federal share DRS accumulated through its 
TPCAs for pre-employment transition services, which it reported in its RSA-17 financial reports 
as meeting VR program requirements for furnishing required non-Federal share under such 
arrangements. This is separate from, but related to, RSA’s concern regarding DRS’ inaccurate 
reporting of pre-employment transition services expenditures and contract oversight described 
under Finding 3.2. RSA questions the allowability of a significant source of match DRS 
identified in its RSA-17 reports as meeting VR matching requirements sufficient to justify its 
drawdown and expenditure of Federal VR award funds.  
 
Uncertified TPCA Personnel Expenditures 
DRS did not certify personnel expenditures, submitted by TPCA agencies that it subsequently 
reported as meeting VR matching requirements despite VR program rules requiring DRS to 
certify such expenditures through administrative oversight of TPCAs. During on-site interviews 
with DRS staff responsible for overseeing TPCAs, DRS indicated it did not meet VR 
requirements for certifying match provided through TPCAs by certifying personnel expenditures. 
TPCAs did not include steps whereby the cooperating entity submitted documentation to DRS 
sufficient for VR agency staff to certify expenditures vendors claimed to satisfy their required 
share of personnel expenditures DRS reported as meeting Federally required VR match. Instead, 
DRS required vendors to periodically submit a form to self-certify the expenditures the vendors 
self-identified as having generated allowable match for the VR program. However, the form 
DRS required vendors to use in attestation of these expenditures for use as VR match fell short 
of meeting requirements for VR agencies to provide administrative supervision over cooperating 
agency’s reporting of personnel time generated under the arrangement.  
DRS also did not have procedures consistent with VR program requirements necessary for DRS 
to certify the claimed effort cooperating entities self-reported. DRS staff confirmed that none of 
the TPCAs Illinois implemented during the review period included language regarding 
requirements for cooperating entities to furnish documentation of staff effort toward VR service 
delivery to DRS for DRS to certify. Yet DRS routinely reported to the Federal government, via 
RSA-17s, the entire amount of expenditures TPCA partners self-reported without VR agency 
staff reviewing or certifying the expenditures as they are required to do when using TPCAs to 
generate allowable match for the VR program.

Finding 3.4 Insufficient Internal Controls 
 
Issues: Whether DRS maintains effective internal controls over the Federal award to provide 
reasonable assurance that it is managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award. Whether DRS satisfied prior approval 
requirements in 2 C.F.R. § 200.407. 
 
Requirements: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. These methods of administration (i.e., the agency’s 
internal controls) must include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial 
accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). “Internal controls” means a process, implemented by a non-
Federal entity, designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives 
in the following categories: 
 
• Effectiveness and efficiency of operations; 
• Reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and 
• Compliance with applicable laws and regulations (2 C.F.R. § 200.61). 
 
In addition, the Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.62(a)(3) defines “internal control over 
compliance requirements for Federal awards” as a process implemented by a grantee that 
provides reasonable assurance that, among other things, that transactions are accurately recorded 
and accounted for to demonstrate compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms 
and conditions of the Federal award. In accordance with the Uniform Guidance, 2 C.F.R. § 
200.303, among other things, a non-Federal entity must— 
 
• Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award; 
• Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards; 
• Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statutes, regulations and 
the terms and conditions of Federal awards; and

39 
• Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings. 
 
Additionally, 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a) requires that a State’s financial management systems, 
including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and 
conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the— 
 
• Preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and conditions; and 
• Tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have 
been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of 
the Federal award. 
 
Furthermore, provisions at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(4) require that the financial management 
system of each non-Federal entity must ensure effective control over, and accountability for, all 
funds, property, and other assets. The non-Federal entity must adequately safeguard all assets 
and assure that they are used solely for authorized purposes. In its guidance The Role of Internal 
Control, Documenting Internal Control, and Determining Allowability & Use of Funds, the U.S. 
Department of Education (Department) made clear to grantees that internal controls represent 
those processes by which an organization assures operational objectives are achieved efficiently, 
effectively, and with reliable, compliant reporting. Therefore, an internal control deficiency 
would exist when the design or operation of a control does not allow management or employees, 
in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent or correct processes that 
might lead to noncompliance with Federal and State requirements. 
Analysis: 
 
Insufficient Internal Controls 
 
RSA’s review of several written process memos provided by DRS, as examples of internal 
controls, found deficiencies and omissions. The agency did not provide all fiscal policies and 
procedures in RSA’s document request. The available policies did not provide reasonable 
assurance that the agency has adequate internal controls, including policies and procedures for 
program income, match, and MOE. 
 
During the review, including discussions with DRS management and review of the agency’s 
policy manuals, RSA found that the agency did not have sufficient policies, procedures, and 
internal controls to ensure compliance with applicable Federal requirements. RSA found that 
written processes either did not exist or did not include the details necessary to provide a 
reasonable assurance that the agency is managing the award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
requirements, and the terms and conditions of the award. 
 
DRS was not able to provide a written policy or procedure for its internal systems (CMS, Hub, 
CPS, an SAP) and how they interrelate to ensure VR funds are used in accordance with Federal 
regulations. DRS did not have a policy or procedure regarding reallotment funds. RSA reviewed 
a number of payments in DRS document request and found a few invoices were marked as 
obligated on the date of payment which is not in compliance with EDGAR at 34 C.F.R.

40 
§§ 76.703 and 76.709. For example, a bill was paid for a college student at the end of the term 
and the obligation date was the paid date, not the date it was approved by DRS. DRS did not 
have a written internal control policy about the liquidation period and how to ensure that 
payments which fell within this time period would be paid in accordance with the Federal 
requirements. For example, there wasn’t any internal control which covered how DRS and the 
DSA would ensure all eligible payments would be approved and paid in their accounting system, 
including the liquidation period of 120 days. DRS admitted that funds were returned to RSA due 
to some payments not being approved by the DSA accounting system during this period. 
 
For some of the internal control processes, DRS has taken steps to address concerns; however, 
the processes were not adequately documented. Consequently, DRS was unable to demonstrate 
that adequate safeguards and internal controls were documented, implemented, and in 
compliance with Federal requirements. When considering staff turnover in key positions, the 
lack of documented internal control processes may result in loss of continuity necessary to 
identify and correct non-compliance. 
 
Prior Approval Requirements Not Met 
 
The Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.407, includes a list of specific circumstances for which 
prior approval from the Federal awarding agency, in advance of the occurrence, is either required 
for allowability or recommended to avoid subsequent disallowance or dispute based on the 
unreasonableness or non-allocability. For example, 2 C.F.R. § 200.439(b)(1) states that capital 
expenditures for general purpose equipment, buildings, and land are unallowable as direct 
charges, except with the prior written approval of the Federal awarding or pass-through entity. 
The Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.62(a)(3) also requires the agency have internal control 
over compliance requirements for Federal awards to demonstrate compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award. On November 2, 2015, 
the Department of Education adopted the final regulations found in 2 C.F.R. part 200 (Federal 
Register notice 80 § FR 67261). The Department issued notifications to grantees regarding the 
new requirements and made training and technical assistance documents available to grantees to 
assist in implementation of the new requirements. To ensure that RSA grantees were aware of 
the applicability of the prior approval requirements, RSA included a special clause on the FFY 
2016 Grant Award Notifications that stated, in pertinent part: [T]he prior approval requirements 
listed in the Uniform Administrative Requirements, Costs Principles, and Audit Requirements 
for Federal Awards (Uniform Guidance) (2 C.F.R. part 200) are applicable to this award… 
Grantees are responsible for ensuring that prior approval, when required, is obtained prior to 
incurring the expenditure. Grantees should pay particular attention to the prior approval 
requirements listed in the Cost Principles (2 C.F.R. 200 subpart E). In addition, information 
regarding the requirements in 2 C.F.R. part 200 was communicated to grantees via RSA’s 
listserv on September 23, 2015. 
 
RSA requested the VR agency’s written policies, procedures, or processes for ensuring the 
agency was meeting the prior approval requirements when applicable. DRS did not have 
comprehensive policies for prior approval.

41 
The agency was not able to provide reasonable assurance that it complied with the Uniform 
Guidance in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.407: 
 
• The staff member who was responsible to prepare and submit the prior approvals left 
three years ago and since then, no prior approval requests have been submitted; and  
• The agency did not request prior approval for all costs that require prior approval. 
 
Conclusion: RSA’s analysis found that DRS has not established and maintained written policies 
and internal controls (2 C.F.R. §§ 200.61 and 200.62). DRS does not have sufficient internal 
controls to ensure compliance with the prior approval requirements pursuant to the Uniform  
Guidance (2 C.F.R. § 200.407). 
 
Corrective Action 3.4: RSA requires that DRS—  
 
3.4.1  Within one quarter following issuance of the monitoring report, develop, implement 
and/or update internal control policies to address issues identified in the finding; and 
3.4.2  Within one quarter following issuance of the monitoring report, develop and implement 
policies and procedures, as well as a written internal control process, including a 
monitoring component, to ensure ongoing compliance with the prior approval 
requirements and the Dear Colleague Letter (DCL) 24-05, issued by OSERS on 
September 30, 2024. Once the CAP is developed, RSA will work with the VR agency to 
determine if updated processes result in meeting Federal requirements and ongoing 
compliance. 
 
VR Agency Response:  The Illinois VR agency agrees with the finding.  
  
3.4.1 The Illinois VR agency will update internal control policies. 
3.4.2 After approval of the CAP, the Illinois VR agency will develop and implement policies, 
procedures, and an internal control process that is compliant with Federal requirements.
