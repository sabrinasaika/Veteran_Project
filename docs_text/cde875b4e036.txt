SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and performance 
indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the performance 
accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the Commissioner 
must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances made in the State 
Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the Rehabilitation Act. In 
conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget and financial 
management data (section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to section 
107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, including pre-
employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR agencies 
provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of RSA’s 
monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and OCTAE conduct 
with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by New Jersey Division of 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services (DVRS). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the 
process by which RSA carried out its activities from January 29, 2024, through February 2, 2024, 
was defined by information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, 
documents, and data submitted by DVRS, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, and 
technical assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
• Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including how
the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous improvement
in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition services, leading to
quality competitive integrated employment and supported employment outcomes for
individuals with disabilities;

• Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE VOCATIONAL 
REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED EMPLOYMENT 
SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment programs’ 
performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most significant 
disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive integrated 
employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies and internal 
controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of maximizing the 
use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where appropriate, RSA provided 
technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective actions. RSA also provided 
recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops a strategic action plan to improve 
performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds to achieve quality employment 
outcomes.  
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE  
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs 
incorporated a review of data reported by DVRS on the Case Service Report (RSA-911) and the 
WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 2021, and 2022, 
as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and requested documents. A 
review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR services, quality of 
employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as discussions with DVRS 
of potential factors influencing program performance informed the analysis and helped to identify 
technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the performance and management of 
the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from related 
sources, and discussions with DVRS during the review, RSA identified various factors that may be 
contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s performance leading to employment 
outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of Federal funds. In addition 
to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to DVRS’ requests, RSA also 
developed recommendations for consideration by the VR agency as it develops strategic actions to 
ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to maximize the use and expenditure 
of available Federal funds to assist individuals with disabilities in achieving quality employment 
outcomes. 
 
Staffing Capacity 
 
Staffing capacity impacts the effectiveness, efficiency, and overall performance of the VR program. 
Adequate staffing increases the likelihood that the VR agency can deliver needed services promptly

5 
 
and more satisfactorily to the individuals it serves, reduce staff burden, decrease staff turnover, and 
increase staff retention and satisfaction. At the time of the on-site review, DVRS reported 37 
vacancies (25 percent) in its 151 VR counselor positions. The average number of cases carried by 
VR counselors was approximately 126 individuals (including potentially eligible students with 
disabilities), which varied based on office location, vacancies, and service demands. DVRS reported 
that offices in south New Jersey and those in rural areas of the State generally experience a higher 
number of VR counselor vacancies compared to other VR offices in the State.  
 
DVRS attributed its VR counselor vacancies to a combination of factors including several 
resignations during the COVID-19 pandemic, staff retirements, the availability of positions in less 
desirably located offices in rural areas, and perceived barriers to career advancement. DVRS 
explained that qualified VR counselors have limited opportunities for career growth and 
advancement because of the State’s requirement that the applicant pass a New Jersey Civil Service 
Commission exam, described by DVRS as an exam that does not appropriately assess candidates’ 
ability to perform the duties within the VR field. In addition to the need to fill VR counselor 
positions, DVRS expressed a need for additional support staff to handle administrative tasks to free 
up VR counselors’ time to focus on their more specialized professional roles, ultimately improving 
their effectiveness and productivity in serving VR program participants.  
 
VR counselor vacancies require existing staff to manage larger caseloads resulting in fewer 
opportunities for engagement, potential delays in the provision of services, and inadequate follow-
up and support, all of which may contribute to individuals prematurely exiting the VR program. For 
example, in PY 2022, the reasons for exit for more than 50 percent of individuals who exited the 
VR program without an employment outcome were reported as “unable to locate” and “no longer 
interested in services.” Lack of engagement is correlated with high rates of premature exits and 
unsuccessful outcomes. DVRS has attempted to manage its VR counselor deficit by assigning a 
supervising rehabilitation counselor or manager to cover vacant caseloads until VR counselor 
positions can be filled. DVRS reported having 16 supervising rehabilitation counselors who have 
been covering 68 vacant caseloads, as well as 13 managers who have stepped in to assist with 
overseeing 31 vacant caseloads.  
 
Due to the projected need to replace a large number of management, supervisory, and VR counselor 
positions over the next five years, DVRS incorporated a substitution clause in the original Civil 
Service requirements to allow for a bachelor’s degree with the requisite experience. However, 
DVRS has been in the process of working with the New Jersey Civil Service Commission to change 
the educational requirement back to a master’s degree as it had been in the past. DVRS has also 
developed and implemented a recruitment strategy to minimize the length of time it takes to fill 
vacancies. As VR counselors leave the agency or are promoted into higher positions within the 
agency, DVRS posts positions 60 days prior to anticipated vacancies, which limits the length of 
time positions remain vacant. Additionally, the agency believes that current salaries, employment 
benefits, staff training, development opportunities, and telework options are factors that have 
assisted in promoting staff retention in recent years.  
 
DVRS continues to explore viable mechanisms to recruit staff for the agency working in 
conjunction with the Division of Human Capital Strategies within the New Jersey Department of

6 
 
Labor and Workforce Development (NJLWD). DVRS advertises positions on an online platform 
and has developed targeted recruitment for VR counselors by expanding outreach to master’s 
degree programs beyond New Jersey to surrounding states. As DVRS continues to recruit additional 
full-time positions, the agency is also exploring the use of temporary employees to fill in current 
staffing gaps. DVRS offers an unpaid intern program; however, based upon the recommendation of 
its SRC, DVRS will be exploring the use of paid internship opportunities in its field offices for 
master’s program students.

• In order to help State VR agencies address the challenges related to the recruitment and 
retention of staff, RSA shared that the Vocational Rehabilitation Technical Assistance 
Center for Quality Management (VRTAC-QM) has created an assessment tool to help 
agency leadership perform an in-depth analysis of their current operating environments in

• RSA shared information from RSA Technical Assistance Circular (TAC) 24-01, Promoting

Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends that DVRS analyze data related to caseload size, number of referrals 
received, applications submitted, number of individualized plans for employment created, 
and training and employment outcomes at individual, district, regional and statewide levels. 
This analysis can inform the number of positions designated to each geographic area and the 
design and delivery of services. 
• RSA recommends that DVRS support and provide opportunities for ongoing staff training, 
particularly for counselors interested in obtaining and maintaining their certification as 
rehabilitation counselors. 
• Because DVRS’ positions are approved through the agency’s designated State agency 
(DSA), the NJLWD, Division of Human Capital Strategies it is recommended that the 
agency work with the DSA to address support staff shortages.  
• To maintain continuity and to minimize disruptions in organizational leadership it is 
recommended that DVRS develop strategic planning processes and tools to ensure a smooth 
transition when key roles become vacant due to retirements, promotions, and unforeseen 
circumstances, particularly for management positions.  
• RSA recommends that DVRS use resources provided by the Vocational Rehabilitation 
Technical Assistance Center for Quality Employment (VRTAC-QE) to explore strategies to 
increase retention of State VR counselors, as well as the major barriers confronting the VR 
program in recruiting and retaining qualified VR counselors.

Referral and Application Process Exceeds Statutory and Regulatory Requirements, Creating 
Undue Burden 
 
During the period under review, DVRS showed a 37 percent increase in the number of individuals 
applying for the VR program (5,198 applicants in PY 2020 to 7,140 applicants in PY 2022). DVRS 
attributed the growth in applicants to increased outreach and marketing efforts and a recently 
implemented online referral form meant to improve accessibility to the VR program. RSA reviewed 
DVRS’ new online referral form, which contains 35 questions and 14 required responses before the 
form can be submitted electronically. A majority of the responses requested on the form exceed 
what is necessary or required for a referral. Additionally, the referral form cannot be submitted 
online if, for example, a telephone number, disability information and a veteran status has not been 
indicated. Because referrals to the VR program can be made by multiple sources such as community 
organizations, employers, family members, healthcare providers and educational professionals, all 
information required on the referral form may not be known to the referral source. A streamlined or 
simplified referral form and process could enhance agency efficiency and reduce unnecessary 
barriers in the referral process for individuals self-referring or others making referrals. Timely 
engagement of individuals during referral and application are essential early in the VR process and 
is best achieved if VR agencies streamline procedures and remove any unnecessary barriers and 
requirements. 
 
DVRS shared that in addition to its online process it also accepts referrals by phone, email, walk-
ins, from schools, family and friends, local agencies, and local workforce development offices. 
Although DVRS uses the same 35 questions/data points for referrals made in person or by phone, 
the lack of information, unlike in the online referral form, does not preclude or delay the agency’s 
ability to accept the referral. DVRS reported it has since reduced its referral form to 25 data 
questions. 
 
DVRS requires its staff to contact individuals within 14 days of receiving the referrals to schedule 
an “Survey Interview” appointment, during which the VR counselor explains DVRS’ process, 
services and eligibility requirements, and provides the DVRS Consumer Handbook. The individual 
discusses their specific needs and is made aware of their rights and responsibilities as outlined in the 
handbook. If an individual decides during the survey interview to pursue VR services, the 
individual will complete, sign and date the application form. An individual must apply for VR 
services either in person by appointment at a local DVRS office, by printing or scanning the 
Application Form when they meet counselors for virtual appointment, or at a local one-stop as a 
walk-in without an appointment. DVRS does not offer the option to apply for VR services online, 
by phone, at an employer location, including an entity holding a certificate issued under Section 
14(c) of the Fair Labor Standards Act), or a mutually convenient location in the community such as 
a library or an individual’s home. Limiting the various avenues to apply for VR services can impose 
unnecessary barriers and delay the application process or unintentionally discourage some 
individuals from applying for services.

Technical Assistance  
 
• RSA reviewed DVRS’ extensive online referral form and application process with the 
agency and clarified that the Rehabilitation Act and the VR program regulations (34 C.F.R. 
§ 361.41(a)) require standards for the prompt and equitable handling of referrals of 
individuals for VR services, including referrals of individuals made through the one-stop 
delivery system under Section 121 of WIOA, and that the agency must include timelines for 
making good faith efforts to inform individuals of application requirements and to gather 
information necessary to begin an assessment for determining eligibility and priority for 
services (34 C.F.R. § 361.41(a)).  
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends that DVRS revise the online referral form to only include essential 
information needed to contact the individual being referred. Ease and timely engagement of 
individuals during referral and application are essential at this early stage in the VR process 
and is best achieved if VR agencies streamline procedures and remove any unnecessary 
barriers and requirements.  
• RSA recommends DVRS revise its policies and procedures governing the application 
process to be consistent with 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2) for what constitutes the submission of 
an application and not add additional burden. Specifically, an individual is considered to 
have applied for VR services: once an individual has completed and signed an application 
form, has completed a common intake form at a one-stop center, or has otherwise requested 
services from the VR agency; has provided the agency sufficient information to initiate an 
assessment to determine eligibility; and is available to complete the assessment process, 
when necessary.  
• RSA recommends that DVRS develop methods and approaches to encourage, inform, and 
inspire the development of strategies for optimizing VR program visibility, easing 
application and eligibility processes, and recognizing and incentivizing VR program 
successes and achievements. 
• RSA recommends that DVRS develop additional and more comprehensive strategies for 
coordinating referrals across the WIOA programs. Both the WIOA Youth and VR programs 
may take advantage of the strengths of each other’s program and make cross-agency 
referrals. For example, VR agencies can refer youth with disabilities, particularly those who 
are out of school, to the WIOA Youth program for training and work experience services. 
Likewise, WIOA Youth programs can refer students or out-of-school youth with disabilities 
to the VR program for pre-employment transition services, as applicable, transition services, 
and VR services. 
 
 
Attrition  
 
Although DVRS reported increasing the number of applicants and eligible individuals in the VR 
program, a large percentage of individuals are exiting prematurely or without an employment 
outcome. In PY 2022, 14.96 percent of individuals exited the VR program as an applicant, prior to 
eligibility determination or trial work experience. During the same period, 15 percent of individuals

9 
 
exited the VR program after an eligibility determination, but prior to an individualized plan for 
employment (IPE), and the percentage of individuals who exited the program after an IPE without 
an employment outcome was 37 percent. When reviewing the reported reasons for exit during the 
period under review, an average of 28 percent of individuals were reported as exiting the program 
because they were unable to be located or contacted, 24 percent exited because they were no longer 
interested in receiving VR services, and 11 percent exited for all other reasons, accounting for more 
than 60 percent of all exits.  
 
DVRS conducts a financial needs assessment of each individual prior to or at the IPE conference to 
establish how much financial participation, if any, the individual will provide toward the cost of 
services. While onsite, RSA discussed with DVRS the impact that a financial participation 
requirement may have on the agency’s attrition due to its burdensome process. Prior to the IPE 
conference, eligible individuals must provide supporting documentation that includes Social 
Security number, liquid assets of family, gross family income, extraordinary medical expenses of 
each family member, and disability related expenses of the individual and family unit, among other 
items. RSA reviewed portions of its financial policy with DVRS for clarification; however, DVRS 
was unable to explain the application of its financial policy. RSA suggested that the agency assess 
whether its financial participation policy is contributing to client attrition through a combination of 
data analysis, client feedback, and targeted evaluation to identify trends and look for correlations 
between financial participation requirements and points at which individuals disengage from VR 
services.  
 
Although DVRS demonstrated timely eligibility determinations (98.5 percent in PY 2022) and 
timely IPE development (98.6 percent in PY 2022), the attrition rate and reasons for exit may 
indicate a lack of engagement as well as inadequate or delayed services needed by participants to 
achieve employment goals. Continuous engagement contributes to the quality of the VR counselor-
client relationship, improves communication, fosters participation in VR services, and increases the 
likelihood of successful employment outcomes. High rates of attrition can impact the effectiveness 
of the VR program and impact DVRS’ ability to expend funds and also pose challenges in 
accurately assessing the program’s overall success and outcomes. DVRS reported initiating 
discussions prior to the on-site portion of the review to coordinate training with consultants from the 
VRTAC-QM directed to field staff, specifically VR counselors, who determine eligibility and 
initiate services necessary to achieve a successful employment outcome.  
 
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA and DVRS discussed the need to review agency guidelines to promote and sustain 
engagement. DVRS’ Case Services Policy Manual, Chapter “IPE Development” indicates 
there must be a minimum contact made every 60 days for participants in an IPE; however, 
there is no guidance for the engagement of those individuals prior to the development of an 
IPE. RSA recommended that DVRS establish a frequency of contact/communication for 
individuals prior to the development of an IPE to reduce premature exits from the VR 
program and include the criteria in its quality assurance (QA) case reviews when assessing 
VR counselor performance.

10 
 
• RSA provided technical assistance related to the accurate reporting of reasons for exit. 
DVRS is inaccurately reporting individuals who exit the VR program using “all other 
reasons” when other code values more accurately reflect exit reasons. 
• RSA provided technical assistance related to RSA-TAC-24-01, Promoting Meaningful and 
Sustained Engagement of Individuals with Disabilities in the State Vocational Rehabilitation 
Services Program, which provides information to support State VR agencies in their efforts 
to make changes that will promote meaningful and sustained engagement with individuals 
with disabilities throughout the VR process. 
• RSA reviewed DVRS’ financial participation policy with the agency, and although there is 
no Federal requirement that the financial need of individuals with disabilities be considered 
in the provision of VR services (34 C.F.R. §361.54(a)), the New Jersey Administrative Code 
(NJAC) § 12:45-1.14 requires a financial needs assessment prior to the implementation of 
VR services. DVRS is considering eliminating its financial needs requirements for 
participants in the provision of VR services. RSA provided technical assistance to DVRS on 
conducting a financial analysis of the types of services and amounts paid by participants and 
consider the long-term sustainability of the decision to eliminate financial participation. 
DVRS should evaluate whether it can continue to provide quality services without relying 
on participants’ contributions in the future and ensure that removing financial participation 
does not inadvertently create other barriers to accessing services or create the need to 
implement an order of selection. DVRS should seek the input of the SRC, the Client 
Assistance Program, and other stakeholders when making decisions about revising the 
statutory requirement to eliminate financial participation. DVRS may wish to consider 
whether there is flexibility to adjust the financial participation levels or apply financial 
participation towards specific VR services or equipment rather than eliminating it entirely. 
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends once DVRS determines appropriate level of communication, the agency 
should develop procedures and conduct training for its VR counselors that prescribe the 
frequency of contact/communication for individuals prior to the development of an IPE to 
reduce premature exits from the VR program and include the criteria in its quality assurance 
(QA) case reviews when assessing VR counselor performance. 
• When assessing its performance, DVRS should consider evaluating the reasons for attrition 
of participants before and after receiving services to determine why this is occurring and 
identify strategies such as frequent counselor contact and conducting surveys to assess 
participant satisfaction. RSA recommends that DVRS move forward with requesting 
technical assistance from the VRTAC-QM in identifying and addressing factors contributing 
to attrition, and methods to improve engagement and timely delivery of VR services that 
fully meet the needs of participants to achieve their intended employment outcomes.   
• Should DVRS move forward with the elimination of the financial needs assessment, it is 
recommended that DVRS establish mechanisms for monitoring and evaluating the impact of 
removing the requirement over time, including financial performance and stakeholder 
satisfaction. RSA supports DVRS’ effort to remove or, at minimum, reduce the impact of its 
financial needs policy by decreasing the portion its participants would need to contribute 
towards certain VR services, such as education and training services that would help prepare

participants for higher quality employment. RSA recommends that DVRS begin reducing 
the amount participants would need to contribute for specific VR services while exploring 
its ability to further reduce or eliminate participants’ contribution as it continues to conduct 
a thorough financial analysis to understand the agency’s current financial situation and the 
potential consequences of removing the requirement. 
• RSA suggested DVRS use existing information to identify the extent that its financial needs 
policies have reduced the amount of costs paid towards college and university program, 
technical or other vocational training programs, medical costs, etc. to determine if the 
elimination or reduction of the specific costs for different service types would be

Postsecondary Readiness Assessment 
 
To evaluate an individual’s readiness to complete an educational program, DVRS requires a core 
academic skills assessment and/or a vocational assessment be completed, and the results must 
support the student’s ability to succeed in postsecondary training prior to providing financial 
sponsorship. DVRS’ policy indicates that if a review of school achievement, academic background 
or placement testing indicates postsecondary education is not a realistic goal, the individual will be 
required to attend a trial semester/grading period at the individual’s expense. DVRS financial 
assistance for a subsequent semester/grading period will depend on the progress achieved.   
 
Using assessments and trial semesters to determine whether DVRS will fund postsecondary 
education for an individual who has already been admitted into a school program, thus 
demonstrating that the individual meets the institution's standards for academic and vocational 
potential, represents an unnecessary burden for the individual and agency expenditure. VR agency 
policies requiring core or vocational assessments as a pre-requisite for supporting participants in 
postsecondary education, particularly if existing academic performance information and/or 
acceptance into a postsecondary program or institution exists, may result in an undue delay in 
services. Additionally, requiring an individual to engage in a trial semester at the individual’s 
expense also places an undue burden, particularly if the supports needed by the individual to 
succeed are not provided by the VR agency. Furthermore, through this policy, DVRS may be 
restricting the participation of individuals with intellectual disabilities to engage in Comprehensive 
Transition and Postsecondary (CTP) Programs or educational programs designed to address the 
needs of neurodiverse learners. 
 
Technical Assistance 
 
• RSA provided technical assistance on policies that restrict or place limitations on the type, 
degree, and costs of support provided to individuals attending both in-State and out-of-State 
postsecondary Institutions of higher education (IHE), indicating that it can deter 
opportunities for individuals with disabilities to maximize their employment and careers. In 
accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.50(a), VR agencies must develop and maintain written 
policies covering the nature and scope of each of the VR services specified in 34 C.F.R. § 
361.48(b) and the criteria under which each service is provided. While not establishing any 
arbitrary limits on the nature and scope of VR services to be provided to the individual, the

12 
 
policies must ensure that the provision of services is based on the rehabilitation needs of 
each individual as identified in that individual's IPE and be consistent with the individual's 
informed choice.
 
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends that DVRS re-evaluate the circumstances under which an individual is 
required to complete a core academic skills assessment and a vocational assessment to 
evaluate an individual’s readiness to complete an educational program. While these 
assessments can provide insights, they should not be the determining factor of an 
individual’s readiness for postsecondary education, particularly if an individual has been 
accepted into a postsecondary program or could benefit from a CTP or a program serving 
neurodiverse students in pursuit of a vocational goal.  
• DVRS should eliminate the use of trial semesters when determining the appropriateness for 
an individual to participate in training or education if the service has been determined 
necessary to achieve an agreed upon employment goal and the individual has been accepted 
into a school or training program.  
 
Provision of Services and Effect on Employment Outcomes 
The RSA review team examined data reported by DVRS to determine how the VR agency is 
maximizing the provision of VR services leading to quality employment and self-sufficiency for 
participants with disabilities. In PY 2022, 5,747 individuals exited the VR program. Of these, 1,899 
individuals exited after an IPE in competitive integrated employment, including supported 
employment. When comparing the number of individuals who exited the VR program with an 
employment outcome after receiving services to the total number of individuals who exited after 
receiving services, DVRS had an employment rate of 47.2 percent in PY 2022, a slight 
improvement from PY 2021 at 46.1 percent.   
  
Prior to the on-site review, DVRS’ Client Assistance Program raised concerns regarding complaints 
that individual’s employment goals were not mutually determined through collaboration between 
the individual and their VR counselor and represented limited available options during the 
development of the IPE. DVRS disagreed with this assertion and maintained individuals are always 
afforded informed choice throughout the VR process. Nevertheless, according to Case Service 
Report (RSA-911) data, the top ten most frequent employment outcomes using the Standard 
Occupational Classification (SOC) titles for the four quarters in PY 2022 primarily consisted of 
entry-level types of employment, such as production workers, laborers, and customer service 
representatives, stock and order fillers, and fast-food workers, accounting for 46 to 48 percent of all 
employment outcomes. DVRS primarily attributed these outcomes to mostly serving individuals 
with the most significant disabilities and individuals pursuing supported employment.  
  
Of the participants in PY 2022 exiting the VR program in competitive integrated employment, 
including supported employment, 422 participants (22.2 percent) received private health insurance 
through their employer and an additional 25.8 percent received private insurance through other 
means at exit. The median hourly wage increased from $12.75 in PY 2020 to $15.00 in PY 2022,

13 
 
surpassing New Jersey’s minimum wage of $14.00 (effective January 2023); however, the median 
hours worked per week remained constant at 30 hours per week from PY 2020 to PY 2022.   
  
In PY 2022, of the 13,535 individuals provided VR services, only five individuals were reported to 
have received apprenticeship training, down from seven individuals for PY 2020, and no individuals 
were reported to have received customized training or work-based learning experiences (for adults). 
In addition, from PY 2020 to PY 2022, the number of customized employment services declined 
from six individuals to one individual, and on-the-job training opportunities decreased from 210 
individuals (1.5 percent) to 159 individuals (1.2 percent). DVRS staff acknowledged the importance 
of developing training opportunities directly with employers to provide unique training for specific 
skilled and semi-skilled positions that may not be offered through standard training and education 
programs and often lead to direct employment upon successful completion of training. To maximize 
training and services, DVRS has developed contracts with approximately 65 community 
rehabilitation providers (CRPs) to expand service coverage and deliver a variety of employment 
services to individuals with disabilities who require support to obtain and maintain employment. 
DVRS has approximately 56 CRPs that are actively providing job placement services, supported 
employment services, pre-employment transition services and long term follow along (LTFA) 
services. DVRS had identified the need to increase the number of CRPs who can deliver specialized 
services, such as Community Based Work Evaluations, Internship Development and Supports, and 
Customized Employment (CE).   
  
While DVRS recognizes the benefits of postsecondary education and training, the agency 
experienced a steady decline in the number and percentage of participants receiving bachelor’s 
degree training, junior or community college, and occupational or vocational training programs 
from PY 2020 to PY 2022. Most notably, when comparing PY 2020 to PY 2022, the number of 
participants reported in junior or community college decreased by half from 516 participants (3.7

Inaccurate Reporting and Insufficient Provision of Pre-Employment Transition Services 
Pre-employment transition services must be made available statewide to students with disabilities. 
When low numbers of students are being served coupled with inaccurate reporting and tracking of 
those who are being served either through DVRS staff or providers, the VR agency’s performance 
in meeting the 15 percent reserve requirement and the provision of pre-employment transition 
services results in poor performance and the need for corrective actions.  
DVRS provides pre-employment transition services through a combination of its staff and 
community providers to both eligible and potentially eligible students with disabilities aged 14 
through 21 across New Jersey’s 21 counties. The total number of students with disabilities reported 
by DVRS more than doubled from PY 2020 to PY 2022 (2,221 to 4,825 students). However, 
according to the U.S. Department of Education OSERS PY 2022 data, the number of students who 
receive services through IDEA-Part B in New Jersey was approximately 80,000 students aged 14 to 
21 years. Thus, DVRS reported serving only about two percent of the students eligible to receive 
pre-employment transition services. The number of students with disabilities who received pre-
employment transition services doubled from PY 2020 to PY 2022, from 589 students to 1,176. 
However, the percentage of students with disabilities reported who received pre-employment 
transition services slightly decreased during the same period, from 26.5 percent to 24.4 percent. The 
majority of students who received pre-employment transition services were potentially eligible for 
services with 38 percent of students with disabilities applying for VR services. 
DVRS primarily provides pre-employment transition services through its fee-for-service agreements 
using both milestone payments and hourly rates for different providers. DVRS identified 
approximately 35 CRPs that provide all five required activities across the State and continues 
efforts to expand the number of providers for pre-employment transition services, particularly in 
rural parts of the State to ensure access for all students eligible and potentially eligible for services. 
To meet the required 15 percent reservation of Federal VR funds for pre-employment transition 
services, DVRS has increased its efforts and resources to expand services and now has two part-
time program managers to identify students with disabilities who could benefit from services.  
DVRS also reported providing the five required pre-employment transition services through its VR 
counselors. During its interviews with VR counselors RSA learned that most of the counselors do 
not provide all five required activities; rather, they provide a few, such as counseling on

16 
 
postsecondary education opportunities and job exploration counseling. They rely on area providers 
to offer workplace readiness, self-advocacy and work-based learning activities. However, other VR 
counselors indicated providing all five activities directly to the students. DVRS requires its staff to 
identify the time allocated for the provision of required and coordination pre-employment transition 
activities through the agency’s electronic timekeeping system though DVRS did not report any 
staff-provided pre-employment transition services during the time of the review. DVRS 
acknowledged VR counselors were told to include case notes when providing services to students 
and did not recognize the need to develop service authorizations for the RSA-911 reporting 
purposes. DVRS has failed to accurately report pre-employment transition services provided to 
students with disabilities by both its staff and contracted providers. In general, VR counselors 
reported having little time to provide pre-employment transition services and they rely primarily on 
service providers, including Centers for Independent Living, to provide these services and would 
like to see additional providers approved to increase options for the students.  
DVRS reported making changes in PY 2022 to increase available resources and prioritized its 
efforts to increase the VR counselor’s focus to identify students with disabilities and expand the

reporting 6.4 percent and 10.0 percent, respectively, despite the New Jersey Commission for the

Effects of Inadequate Internal Controls on Performance 
VR agencies must implement policies and procedures that ensure the proper and efficient 
administration of the VR program, including those necessary to carry out all functions for which the 
State is responsible under the program. These methods must include procedures to ensure accurate 
data collection and financial accountability. It is incumbent on VR agencies to establish policies and 
procedures that ensure the accurate collection, retention, and timely reporting of all data. The 
requirements in 34 C.F.R. § 361.47 and 34 C.F.R. § 361.56, taken together, also require VR 
agencies to maintain supporting documentation in an individual’s case file, particularly regarding 
eligibility determinations, development of the IPE, VR services provided, and case closure. 
Furthermore, on December 19, 2018, the U.S. Department of Labor and the U.S. Department of 
Education (Departments) issued joint guidance titled, “Guidance for Validating Jointly Required 
Performance Data Submitted under WIOA” (RSA-TAC-19-01). This guidance provides States with 
a general framework for data validation. Specifically, the Departments developed the joint guidance 
pursuant to Section 116(d)(5) of WIOA, which requires the Departments to establish data validation 
guidelines to ensure the information contained in program reports is valid and reliable. Lastly, VR 
agencies must remember that compliance with RSA’s edit checks is but one component of the VR 
agency’s internal controls system necessary to ensure the integrity of RSA-911 data. VR agencies 
should not imply the correctness or completeness of data submissions from compliance with the 
RSA’s edit checks alone. 
Inaccurate Service Reporting 
The DVRS case management system requires updates and modification to allow for accurate and 
complete recording of VR services, in accordance with statutory requirements. The user interface 
should allow counselors and support staff to specify and record the full range of VR services, 
whether the service is provided through a contract, purchased via a fee-for-service arrangement, 
obtained through a comparable benefit, or if VR staff themselves provided the service. Furthermore, 
maintenance is required on the system’s back-end reporting mechanisms to accurately sort and 
characterize system values and nomenclature into those defined by the RSA-911. DVRS would 
benefit from enhancing agency roles and functions aimed at identifying and correcting these issues 
in an ongoing manner. 
When reviewing Table 4, DVRS leadership acknowledged that service reporting as reflected on the 
RSA-911 was quite different than what was expected. Numerous reporting categories were entirely

18 
 
blank or much lower than anticipated. Blank service categories over the three-year review period 
included commonly provided VR services, such as job search assistance, job placement assistance, 
short-term job supports, information and referral services, extended services for youth, and work-
based learning experiences. Service categories minimally reported included transportation, 
maintenance, diagnosis and treatment of impairments, and interpreter services. DVRS also 
acknowledge that other services reported were, in fact, reported incorrectly, including bachelor’s 
degree, junior or community college and occupational or vocational training services, due to the 
agency not reporting services provided through comparable benefits. In addition, DVRS failed to 
capture VR and pre-employment transition services provided through its staff or through its 
contracts. DVRS leadership acknowledges the only pre-employment transition services reliably 
reported are those purchased through a fee-for-service arrangement. DVRS also acknowledged 
inaccurate reporting of supported employment services. With about 40 percent of all participants 
having supported employment indicated on the IPE, the agency reported between 10 and 11 percent 
of its participants received supported employment services during the review period, per RSA-911 
reporting. Agency leadership expressed some confidence in the percentage of supported 
employment plans in the caseload and suggested that some supported employment activities are not 
being reported accurately when funded through another provider. DVRS further indicated that the 
reported data includes any type of job search, job development, and short-term job support services 
because the agency considers these services to be supported employment services.   
In addition to failing to report the services mentioned above, DVRS acknowledged that it recently 
found that some reported services may have been mischaracterized and that it incorrectly reported 
other VR service data elements on the RSA-911. For example, when an eligible individual requires 
transportation or maintenance to participate in an assessment or a training service, the service (e.g., 
transportation) may have been characterized as an assessment or training service. Another example 
recently investigated by the agency is the classification of paid internships as registered 
apprenticeships, as opposed to work-based learning experiences. These mischaracterizations and 
grouping of services further obscure DVRS’s actual performance. Without an accurate 
understanding of the services it provides, DVRS cannot determine what services have been 
beneficial for participants in terms of achieving employment and the actual cost of each service 
type. 
A strong system of internal controls would include the capacity to catch and correct these issues 
before inaccurate reporting persists. To correct systemic errors and omissions in the reporting of 
services provided, DVRS will need to address known issues, such as the reporting of staff provided 
services and comparable benefits, and strengthen ongoing processes for program evaluation, 
enabling the agency to identify, prioritize, and correct these issues as they occur.  
Insufficient Data Validation and Quality Assurance 
DVRS lacks adequate internal control roles and functions that would validate key elements of 
casework that are reported through the RSA-911. As a result, DVRS cannot provide reasonable 
assurances that the data reported through the RSA-911 are accurate and supported. While DVRS 
has some elements of internal controls in place, it was apparent during the review that the agency 
lacks an effective system of managerial checks on compliance and the validation of reported data.

19 
 
RSA determined functions related to program evaluation and quality assurance were under-staffed, 
under-resourced, and not well defined, creating lapses in the internal control processes.  
DVRS has a quality assurance manager in place and has developed a quality assurance tool within 
its case management system. However, this tool has only been used once since its development in 
2019 and has not been documented within agency policy and procedures, nor has it been 
operationalized within job roles or responsibilities. Furthermore, DVRS does not engage in topic-
focused/ad hoc case reviews of any kind. For example, DVRS has not yet conducted a review of 
agency documentation provided in support of education enrollment when determining measurable 
skill gains (MSG) earned or credentials attained. While DVRS relies upon supervisors to review 
casework on an ongoing basis, there appeared to be no centralized evaluation criteria regarding 
casework expectations. Supervisory oversight regarding casework did not focus on expectations for 
production, timeliness, the scrutiny of authorizations, and the review of IPEs. Once supervisory 
reviews are completed, the results of the reviews are not aggregated or analyzed for patterns that 
would inform agency training development. 
During the onsite review of case records, RSA reviewed ten case service records randomly chosen 
based on the attainment of one or more MSG during PY 2022. Of the ten service records reviewed, 
only three of 10 of the service records contained adequate supporting documentation. DVRS 
management informed RSA it developed training to address the agency’s low performance in MSG. 
While it does appear that this training had a positive effect (the agency improved from 11.5 percent 
to 56.1 percent in its MSG Rate) it appears that the changes applied to coding cases affirmatively 
but did not necessarily verify the presence of supporting documentation. Several other cases 
reported to RSA with credential attainments were also problematic and lacked documentation of 
diplomas or certificates. DVRS was informed during the review that educational enrollment data 
reported by the agency present as an outlier among agencies. This is apparent when comparing 
RSA’s quarterly dashboard to end of year outcomes for credential attainment. For most agencies, 
quarterly progress on the credential attainment measure will closely match end of year calculations 
when the program year is complete. This is not the case for DVRS and suggests that the agency may 
be reporting data elements 78, 84, 400, or perhaps others, in a manner that is inconsistent with 
requirements, or perhaps changed often throughout the life of the case. With WIOA performance 
assessments entering their seventh year, agencies must have the capacity to accurately report data 
that determine performance after reviewing the veracity of their data and ensuring that the data are 
accompanied by supporting documentation when necessary. The agency should advise staff when 
data are not accurate and how to avoid future errors.

Finding 2.1 Deficiencies in the Management of the Federal Award 
 
Issue: Did DVRS maintain effective internal controls over the Federal award to provide a 
reasonable assurance that it was managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.303 and 
the requirements at 34 C.F.R. § 361.40(a). 
 
Requirement: Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, VR agencies are required to develop internal control 
processes to provide reasonable assurances regarding the effectiveness and efficiency of operations, 
reliability of reporting for internal and external use, and implemented as a measure of checks and 
balances to ensure proper expenditure of funds, including the evaluation, and monitoring of 
compliance with statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of Federal awards. Furthermore, 
a State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or Combined State Plan, 
that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and efficient administration of 
the VR program. Additionally, 2 C.F.R. § 200.303 requires, among other things, a non-Federal 
entity to— 
  
• Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award. These internal controls should follow guidance in Standards for Internal Control in 
the Federal Government issued by the Comptroller General of the United States and the 
Internal Control-Integrated Framework, issued by the Committee of Sponsoring 
Organizations of the Treadway Commission;  
• Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards;  
• Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statutes, regulations, and the 
terms and conditions of Federal awards; and  
• Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings.

22 
 
 
An internal control deficiency exists when the design or operation of a control does not allow 
management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent 
or correct processes that might lead to noncompliance with Federal and State requirements.  
 
Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.47(a), VR agencies must maintain for each applicant and eligible 
individual a record of services that includes, to the extent pertinent, documentation including, but 
not limited to, the individual’s application for VR services, the individual’s IPE, and information 
related to closing the service record of an individual who achieves an employment outcome. The 
record of service must allow for accurate and timely data reports pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.40 and 
meet the performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. 
 
Analysis: As detailed above in the discussion of factors influencing performance, RSA’s 
examination of DVRS’ internal controls for data collection and reporting found many areas to be 
incomplete or lacking altogether. For example, DVRS’s continued failure to implement a structured 
case review process limits the agency’s ability to detect instances of non-compliance, particularly 
with respect to the verification of supporting documentation. The lack of verification efforts was 
evident when RSA reviewed cases reported to have achieved measurable skill gains and/or 
credentials. Furthermore, through the review, RSA made DVRS aware of several areas of service 
reporting that were either absent altogether or missing entire categories of served individuals. A 
functioning system of internal controls would be capable of detecting, and acting upon, non-
compliance with supporting documentation and missing accounts of agency activity. While DVRS 
maintains some components of an internal control system for data collection and reporting, lacking 
features, such as ongoing quality assurance and administrative oversight through program 
evaluation, are clearly contributing to factual inaccuracies and omissions in Federal reporting.  
     
Conclusion: RSA determined that, at the time of the review, DVRS had not established and 
maintained effective internal control over its Federal award that provided reasonable assurance that 
the non-Federal entity was managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award. RSA determined that DVRS’ 
internal controls did not ensure the reporting requirements specified in 34 C.F.R. § 361.40, which 
requires the VR agency to submit reports, including reports required under sections 13, 14, and 
101(a)(10) of the Rehabilitation Act, in the form and level of detail and at the time required by the 
Secretary regarding applicants for and eligible individuals receiving services in a manner that 
provides a complete count (other than the information obtained through sampling consistent with 
Section 101(a)(10)(E) of the Rehabilitation Act) of the applicants and eligible individuals to permit 
the greatest possible cross-classification of data; and protect the confidentiality of the identity of 
each individual. In addition, the designated State agency must comply with any requirements 
necessary to ensure the accuracy and verification of those reports, pursuant to the requirements 
described at RSA PD-19-03. Because DVRS is not accurately collecting or reporting data related to 
measurable skill gains and credentials, and under-reporting counseling and guidance, information 
and referral, and services delivered through comparable benefits, DVRS has not met the 
requirements set forth in 34 C.F.R. § 361.40 and is not in compliance with the requirements at 2 
C.F.R. § 200.303.

SECTION 3: FOCUS AREA – FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area RSA assessed the financial management and fiscal accountability of the VR 
and Supported Employment programs to ensure that: funds were being used only for intended 
purposes; there were sound internal controls and reliable reporting systems; that available resources 
were maximized for program needs; and funds supported the achievement of competitive integrated 
employment for individuals with disabilities, including those with the most significant disabilities, 
and the needs of students with disabilities for pre-employment transition services.  
B. SCOPE OF FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT REVIEW AND PERFORMANCE   
    ANALYSIS 
The effective and efficient fiscal administration of the VR and Supported Employment programs is 
essential to the ability of a VR agency to maximize the funds available for attainment of 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities. RSA reviewed components of DVRS’ 
financial management system and assessed the fiscal accountability of the programs to—  
• Ensure funds are obligated and expended in accordance with State and Federal requirements 
and used only for allowable purposes;  
• Ensure programs have sound internal controls and reliable reporting systems;  
• Identify needed improvements in fiscal policy, processes, and procedures to maximize the 
use of Federal funds to benefit VR participants and students with disabilities; and  
• Ensure a relative benefit to the VR system is proportional to the expenditure (e.g., contracts, 
one-stops, indirect cost rates, cost allocation plans, etc.). 
RSA reviewed the use and expenditure of Federal funds to assist DVRS in minimizing the return of 
funds for reallotment and/or the return of unused funds at the end of the period of performance for 
the award. Therefore, the scope of this focus area is primarily on the financial management and 
operations that impact the use of or return of Federal funds. 
During FFYs 2018 through 2023, DVRS’ VR formula award increased from $48,645,642 to 
$56,013,181, representing a 15.1 percent increase in Federal VR funds. Over the same period, 
DVRS provided a steady increase of non-Federal funds to match the increase in the Federal award. 
However, the amount of matched Federal VR award funds that DVRS expended, expressed as a 
percentage of the VR formula award, varied over the period. DVRS expended 93.1 percent of its 
FFY 2019 VR formula award but did not spend $3,414,362 of matched VR award funds that were

Period of Performance  
Period of performance is the time during which the non-Federal entity (grantee) may incur new 
obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal award (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). To 
accurately account for Federal and non-Federal funds, the VR agency must ensure that allowable 
non-Federal and Federal obligations and expenditures are assigned to the correct Federal fiscal year 
award. RSA uses the financial information reported by the grantee to determine each VR agency’s 
compliance with fiscal requirements (e.g., carryover, reservation of funds, matching, maintenance 
of effort, etc.).  
 
The RSA review team assessed DVRS’ performance in meeting the period of performance 
requirements related to the proper assignment of obligations and expenditures to the correct grant 
awards and fund sources.  
 
DVRS and NJLWD described the systems used in managing period of performance. DVRS’ CMS 
includes fund source coding related to Federal awards and cost objectives, including VR (01), the 
pre-employment transition services reserve (13), and the Supported Employment awards, Supported 
Employment-A (SE-A) award (06), and Supported Employment-B (SE-B) award (07). New Jersey 
staff confirmed non-Federal funds are not typically used for CMS obligations. In the field, VR 
counselors use authorization input forms to identify data, including a fund source, that the data entry 
operator (DEO) enters into the CMS. An authorization and invoice (A&I) document (authorization) 
is generated in PDF form. The binding written commitment identifying the date of obligation occurs 
when the VR counselor signs the printed authorization.  
 
The CMS defaults the authorization fund source selected by the VR counselor to the current FFY of 
appropriation and includes an authorization end date of September 30 of the same year. The 
authorization doubles as the invoice, signed by the CRPs and vendors, which is sent back to field

26 
 
offices for payment processing review. When the authorization is returned to the VR counselor at 
the completion of the service, the counselor will ensure the file contains sufficient written 
documentation that the service has been provided. Documentation may include reports, contracts, 
receipts, etc. The VR counselor then signs the document to certify approval for payment. The DEO 
uses the information to prepare a final payoff selection. NJLWD finance and accounting (F&A) 
staff retrieve imported batch payments ready for processing from the financial module. Final payoff 
is sent by the New Jersey Department of Treasury (NJ Treasury) and the payment is created in the 
New Jersey Comprehensive Financial System (NJ CFS). The payment data are recorded in both NJ 
CFS and the CMS. With an authorization end date of September 30, or due to cancellations of 
authorizations, DVRS indicated in rare instances an authorization may be cancelled but services 
begun. However, a new authorization may be generated in a subsequent period of performance with 
funds not available when the original authorization and services occurred. RSA stressed that the 
obligation, services, and fund source availability must always coincide with the same period of 
performance. Consistent with the findings described below, DVRS must review its authorization 
management process to ensure period of performance requirements are met in all circumstances.  
 
During the on-site review, RSA learned of several limitations in the various New Jersey systems 
that create challenges in managing the VR and other Federal programs. While the NJ Treasury 
provides budget authority for DVRS/NJLWD to initially use advanced State funds to pay invoices, 
etc., the budget authority is based on each grant award notification (GAN) the State receives. 
During FFYs with multiple continuing resolutions (CRs), since budget authority is limited to each 
GAN issued by RSA, NJLWD’s F&A staff must create a Federal Module (FM) document in NJ 
CFS for each GAN and increase budget authority each time until the final GAN is received. The 
FM is reviewed and approved by NJ Treasury before the budget authority to use the State funds are 
available in NJ CFS.  
 
Neither State funds nor carryover fund sources are used consistently within the CMS, and these 
funds are typically spent down via chargebacks at the NJLWD (DSA) F&A office level. Due to the 
assignment of fund source in the CMS to the FFY of appropriation, the fund source in the NJ CFS 
general ledger also identifies the fund source as the FFY of appropriation award. While NJLWD 
F&A staff can “apply” other funds such as carryover year funds or non-Federal funds through a 
User Agency Payment Voucher (UA) document, to move funds around at high levels, it will not 
change the default accounting fund source for transactions in the general ledger. This affects New 
Jersey’s ability to effectively manage carryover, non-Federal share and the Federal awards with 
respect to aligning the accounting records with the Federal financial date reported to RSA.  
 
RSA further learned NJLWD is not permitted to employ accounting adjustments such as journal 
vouchers, which are only permitted by NJ Treasury. Therefore, the transactions will always reflect 
FFY of appropriation Federal funds. Additionally, since NJ Treasury provides budget authority to 
use State funds advanced to the agency to pay for initial transactions, despite the coding in NJ CFS, 
at some point F&A must draw down Federal award funds from G6 to reimburse itself. Historically, 
New Jersey has not drawn down funds in an efficient and timely manner, resulting in a disconnect 
between Federal expenditures reported on SF-425 and RSA-17 reports for the VR program and the

27 
 
amount of Federal funds drawn down from G6. 
RSA clarified that period of performance requirements must ensure alignment of the obligation date 
with the same period services are provided and the availability of the fund source. During the 
review, New Jersey asked if it could access funds based on a budget. RSA described the difference 
between discretionary awards with budgets and formula awards like the VR and SE awards, which 
do not have budgets. RSA reviewed the DVRS internal controls manual and recommended the 
agency clarify and expand its practices, as well as the detailed actions it was taking at the time of 
the on-stie review, to account for period of performance activities. Due to the restriction in making 
accounting adjustments in NJ CFS, RSA recommends the agency explore expanding the availability

The RSA review team assessed DVRS’ performance in meeting the matching requirements for the

appropriations dedicated to the VR program, or other State funds the agency is authorized to use.

Supported Employment Program Match 
Supported Employment program regulations require that the VR agency expend 50 percent of its 
total Supported Employment program allotment for the provision of supported employment 
services, including extended services, to youth with the most significant disabilities. The Supported 
Employment program funds required to be reserved and expended for services to youth with the 
most significant disabilities are awarded through the SE-B grant award. The Federal share for 
expenditures from the VR agency’s SE-B grant award is 90 percent. The statutorily required 10 
percent match requirement applies to the costs of carrying out the provision of supported 
employment services, including extended services, to youth with the most significant disabilities. 
This means that the 10 percent is applied to total expenditures, including both the Federal and non-
Federal shares, incurred for this purpose, and that the non-Federal share must also be spent on the 
provision of supported employment services, including extended services, to youth with the most 
significant disabilities. 
The RSA review team assessed the matching requirements for the Supported Employment program, 
including an assessment of whether the matching level was met, as well as whether the sources of 
the match were consistent with Federal requirements.

Pre-Employment Transition Services Reservation and Expenditure of Funds 
Section 101(a)(25) of the Rehabilitation Act requires State VR agencies to assure in their Unified or 
Combined State Plan, with respect to students with disabilities, that the State has developed and 
implemented strategies to—   
• Address the needs identified in the assessments described in Section 101(a)(15) of the 
Rehabilitation Act; and 
• Achieve the goals and priorities identified by the State in accordance with Section 
101(a)(15) of the Rehabilitation Act to improve and expand VR services for students with 
disabilities on a statewide basis; and provide pre-employment transition services. 
“Pre-employment transition services,” as defined at Section 7(30) of the Rehabilitation Act, “means 
services provided in accordance with Section 113.” Section 113(a) of the Rehabilitation Act 
requires the State to ensure it will use the funds reserved under Section 110(d)(1) of the 
Rehabilitation Act to provide, or arrange for the provision of, pre-employment transition services to 
students with disabilities. Section 110(d)(1) of the Rehabilitation Act establishes the 15 percent 
minimum reservation requirement for the State from its VR allotment. Together, Sections 7(30), 
101(a)(25), 110(d)(1), and 113 of the Rehabilitation Act create the statutory basis for the State Plan 
provisions governing pre-employment transition services.  
Early career exploration through pre-employment transition services increases the likelihood of 
students with disabilities achieving high-quality competitive integrated employment. RSA explored 
the use of funds for the provision of pre-employment transition services and the extent to which 
DVRS is meeting the State required reservation and expenditure of at least 15 percent of its 
matched Federal VR grant funds. 
Since the enactment of WIOA in FFY 2014, DVRS and the New Jersey Commission for the Blind 
and Visually Impaired, collectively as the State, have not expended sufficient Federal reserve funds 
on pre-employment transition services to meet the 15 percent reservation requirement. In recent 
FFYs, DVRS expended a low of 4.1 percent of matched FFY 2021 VR award funds on pre-
employment transition services. However, the highest percentage of matched Federal award funds 
spent on pre-employment transition services occurred with the FFY 2022 award, from which DVRS

30 
 
expended 8.4 percent toward the reserve. 
DVRS spends VR award funds on pre-employment transition services on personnel costs of DVRS 
staff, but primarily expends funds through approximately 20 vendors approved for not-to-exceed 
contracts to provide summer internships. While the total amount let across all contracts would far 
exceed the 15 percent reserve target (e.g., approximately $7,000,000 required for the FFY 2022 VR 
award), the actual amount that is expended under the contracts is not sufficient to meet the reserve. 
In recent years, DVRS has continued to increase the overall capacity of such contracts, which span 
a State fiscal year (SFY) term of July 1 through June 30, from a total of about $5,000,000 for 2021-
2022, to $9,697,424 for 2022-2023, and recently in 2023-2024 increasing the total capacity of the

Finding 3.1 Insufficient Policies and Procedures and Non-Compliance with Period of

Issue: Does DVRS meet obligation and expenditure requirements in 2 C.F.R. § 200.1 and 34 C.F.R. 
§ 76.707. Does DVRS assign and liquidate obligations such that expenditures align with the correct

Requirement: As a recipient of Federal VR and Supported Employment program funds, DVRS 
must have procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of its VR and Supported 
Employment programs and enable DVRS to carry out all required functions, including financial 
reporting (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). In accordance with the Uniform Guidance in 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a), 
a State’s financial management systems, including records documenting compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the 
preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and conditions; and the 
tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have been used 
according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award. 
The Uniform Guidance requires the financial management system of each non-Federal entity to 
provide for the identification, in its accounts, of all Federal awards received and expended and the 
Federal programs under which they were received (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)). In addition, Education 
Department General Administrative Regulations (EDGAR) at 34 C.F.R. § 76.702 require States to 
use fiscal control and fund accounting procedures that ensure proper disbursement of and 
accounting for Federal funds.  
Each grant award has a defined “period of performance” that is the time during which the non-
Federal entity may incur new obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal award 
(2 C.F.R. § 200.1). A non-Federal entity may only charge to the Federal award allowable costs 
incurred during the period of performance (2 C.F.R. § 200.309, see also 34 C.F.R. §§ 76.703 and 
76.709). Grantees must implement internal controls necessary to ensure obligations and 
expenditures for a Federal award are assigned, tracked, recorded, and reported within the applicable 
period of performance for that Federal award, thereby ensuring the grantees are managing the award 
in compliance with Federal requirements (2 C.F.R. § 200.303(a)). The proper assignment of Federal 
and non-Federal funds to the correct period of performance is necessary for DVRS to correctly 
account for VR and Supported Employment program funds so that RSA can be assured the agency 
has satisfied requirements for, among other things, match (34 C.F.R. § 361.60), MOE (34 C.F.R. § 
361.62), and the reservation and expenditure of VR funds for the provision of pre-employment 
transition services (34 C.F.R. § 361.65(a)(3)).  
A financial obligation means “orders placed for property and services, contracts and subawards 
made, and similar transactions during a given period that require payment by the non-Federal entity 
during the same or a future period" (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). For expenditures to be allowable under the 
Federal award, agencies must demonstrate that the obligation occurred within the period of 
performance of the Federal award. EDGAR regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 76.707 explain when a State 
incurs an obligation for various kinds of services and property. Expenditures must be for payment 
of actual obligations. Obligations must be charged to a fund source (e.g., Federal award funds, State 
appropriations, etc.) and must occur within the appropriate period of performance. Therefore, to

36 
 
properly account for and liquidate expenditures, grantees must be able to assign an obligation to 
fund sources including the specific Federal award based upon the date the obligation was made (34 
C.F.R. §§ 76.703 and 76.709).

Finding 3.2 Federal Financial Reporting Deficiencies 
 
Issue: Does DVRS maintain effective internal control over the Federal award to provide reasonable

regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, including controls related to financial

Requirements: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. These methods of administration (i.e., the agency’s 
internal controls) must include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial 
accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12).  
 
“Internal controls” means a process, implemented by a non-Federal entity, designed to provide 
reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in the following categories: 
  
• Effectiveness and efficiency of operations;  
• Reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and  
• Compliance with applicable laws and regulations (2 C.F.R. § 200.1).  
 
In accordance with the Uniform Guidance, 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, among other things, a non-Federal 
entity must—  
 
• Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award. These internal controls should follow guidance in Standards for Internal Control in 
the Federal Government issued by the Comptroller General of the United States and the 
Internal Control Integrated Framework, issued by the Committee of Sponsoring 
Organizations of the Treadway Commission;  
• Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards;

41 
 
• Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statutes, regulations, and the 
terms and conditions of Federal awards; and  
• Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings.  
 
In its guidance, The Role of Internal Control, Documenting Internal Control, and Determining 
Allowability & Use of Funds, the Department made clear to grantees that internal controls represent 
those processes by which an organization assures operational objectives are achieved efficiently, 
effectively, and with reliable, compliant reporting. Therefore, an internal control deficiency would 
exist when the design or operation of a control does not allow management or employees, in the 
normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent or correct processes that might 
lead to noncompliance with Federal and State requirements.

Finding 3.3 Unallowable Costs  
 
Issue: Whether DVRS met the Federal requirements including internal controls for determining 
allowability and allocability of costs in pre-employment internship contracts (2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403 
through 200.405).  
 
Requirements: Regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 361.3 limit authorized activities under the VR program 
to VR services and administrative costs under the State Plan. Except where otherwise authorized by 
statute or implementing regulations, costs are allowable with Federal program funds only if they are 
necessary and reasonable for the performance of the Federal award and allocable to that award in 
accordance with Federal cost principles at 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403 through 200.405 (2 C.F.R. § 
200.403(a)). In accordance with Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.405(a), a cost is allocable to a 
Federal award or cost objective if the goods or services involved are chargeable to that cost 
objective in accordance with the relative benefit received. 
 
Analysis: During the review, RSA reviewed a New Jersey Notice of Contract Opportunity for Paid 
Internship Contracts for FFY 2023, designed to provide pre-employment transition services to 
students with disabilities. RSA’s review of Section C. Available Funding identified the following 
statement:  
 
“Following successful completion of workplace readiness training, job exploration counseling and 
instruction on self-advocacy during the first three weeks, the student intern will receive a $100.00 
stipend per completed week. During the following weeks in which the student is actively 
participating in a 150-hour work-based learning experience paid internship, student intern will 
receive $18 per hour. Lastly, during the final three weeks of workplace readiness training wrap-up 
the student intern will receive a $100.00 stipend for successful participation and completion of the 
week. The stipend will be paid through payroll of the agency.”  
 
A review of the scope of work, duration of services provided, and amount of the stipend further 
demonstrates students with disabilities may participate in up to six weeks of services in which the 
paid stipend is applicable, including the—   
 
• First three weeks of workplace readiness training, job exploration counseling and instruction 
on self-advocacy; and  
• Final three weeks of workplace readiness training wrap up.  
 
Therefore, this amounts to a potential total payment of stipends up to $600 per student. The NCO 
requires a minimum of 60 students per year, with a maximum of 80 students. This means each 
contract could result in $36,000 - $48,000 of training stipends per FFY. RSA understands there are 
up to 20 contracts in place, increasing the potential FFY stipend total range between $720,000 - 
$960,000 per year. Each contract covers a one-year period with the option for annual renewal.

45 
 
DVRS may provide VR services, including maintenance and transportation, to assist applicants or 
recipients of VR services on an individual basis to participate in those services. Participants must be 
determined eligible for the VR program and the approved IPE should include the necessary 
services, such as maintenance and transportation, that would support the individual’s participation 
in all VR services necessary to achieve the employment goal.  
 
However, costs to participate in VR services must be based on the needs of the individual. In the 
case of an IPE for an eligible individual that is a student or youth with a disability, the IPE must 
include the specific transition services and supports needed to achieve the individual’s employment 
outcome or projected post-school employment outcome (34 C.F.R. § 361.46(a)(2)(ii)). Additionally, 
the term stipend typically refers to a fixed regular sum paid as a salary or allowance, often as an 
alternative to a salary or wage. While a stipend may be appropriate for a work-based learning 
experience, it is not reasonable, necessary, or allocable to the VR program when used as an 
incentive for the participation and completion of the other required pre-employment transition 
services.  
 
To the extent that DVRS paid students stipends, gift cards, or any form of honorarium as an 
incentive for the participant to complete VR services, including pre-employment transition services, 
the amount paid would represent questioned VR costs. DVRS has a responsibility to ensure that 
costs paid are allowable, reasonable, and necessary for the provision of VR services, including pre-
employment transition services.  
 
Conclusion: Incentive payments or honorariums, including the use of gift cards, made with Federal 
funds are not considered allowable, necessary, reasonable, or allocable and should not be used. 
Uniform Guidance further clarifies that costs must meet the following general criteria in order to be 
allowable under Federal awards. DVRS did not ensure the costs were allowable with Federal 
program funds in accordance with Federal cost principles at 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.403 through 200.405 
(2 C.F.R. § 200.403(a)). 
 
Corrective Actions 3.3 RSA requires that DVRS —

participation and completion of pre-employment transition services; 
 
3.3.2  Develop and implement internal controls necessary to ensure the accuracy of contract 
language, including fees or costs paid for services, do not include unallowable costs; and 
 
3.3.3  Terminate or revise existing pre-employment transition service internship contracts to 
remove unallowable costs and ensure the proper payment for services is provided.

Finding 3.4 Supported Employment Program Matching, Drawdown and Reporting 
Requirements Not Met 
 
Issue: Whether DVRS meets the matching, drawdown and reporting requirements of the Supported 
Employment program cited at 34 C.F.R. § 363.23(a)(2)(i). 
 
Requirements: In accordance with Section 603 of the Rehabilitation Act, the VR agency must 
reserve and expend 50 percent of its Supported Employment allotment for the provision of 
supported employment services to youth with the most significant disabilities. While RSA has 
divided the Supported Employment allotment into separate grants each constituting 50 percent of 
the allotment, i.e. Supported Employment-A and Supported Employment-B (SE-A and SE-B), the 
VR agency’s Supported Employment allotment is considered the total of both those grants, and the 
VR agency’s obligation to reserve and expend half of its grant on youth with the most significant 
disabilities continues regardless of whether the size of the total allotment increases or decreases. A 
VR agency must expend its SE-B grant on youth with the most significant disabilities and match at 
least 10 percent of the total combined Federal and non-Federal expenditures associated with the SE-
B grant. 
 
Because the SE-B component of the Supported Employment program has a matching requirement, 
VR agencies must obligate and/or expend the non-Federal share to be used as match for the SE-B 
award by the end of the year of appropriation. To carry over unobligated SE-A and/or SE-B Federal 
funds to a subsequent Federal fiscal year, grantees must obligate the non-Federal share to be used as 
match for the SE-B award by the end of the year of appropriation. A VR agency may carry over 
unobligated SE-A funds proportionate to the amount of SE-B funds matched in the year of 
appropriation. 
 
VR agencies may not expend more in SE-A Federal funds than are matched and expended in SE-B 
Federal funds by the end of the period of performance. This means that at the end of the period of 
performance for the SE-A and SE-B awards, a grantee must ensure the amount of Federal SE-A 
funds obligated and expended is equal to the amount of Federal SE-B funds matched by the end of 
the year of appropriation and expended at the end of the SE-B award’s period of performance. If a

47 
 
grantee includes obligations as non-Federal share on its SE-B fourth quarter SF-425 (Federal 
Financial Report) and those obligations are not subsequently liquidated, the amount of SE-A and 
SE-B funds available for obligation and liquidation is reduced accordingly. 
 
As a recipient of Federal Supported Employment funds, DVRS must have internal controls and 
procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of its Supported Employment 
program and enable DVRS to carry out all required functions, including meeting the non-Federal 
share requirement (34 C.F.R. § 361.12 and 2 C.F.R. §§ 302 and 303). 
 
Analysis: As part of the monitoring effort, RSA reviewed SF-425 financial reports submitted by 
DVRS for the monitoring period for the Supported Employment program. RSA’s review indicated 
that both the matching, drawdown, proportionate spending, and reporting requirements were unmet 
for the FFY 2022 SE-B and SE-A awards. 
 
For H187B220044, DVRS must first have met its matching requirement to spend any Federal award 
funds. While the fourth quarter SF-425 report identifies $19,960.00 in non-Federal share, the Final 
SF-425 report indicates $0 reported as match. RSA determines match for the SE-B award based on 
a comparison of the fourth quarter and final non-Federal share, taking the lower of the two. Based 
on the reports, $0 is the match for H187B220044. The amount of Federal expenditures reported on 
the Final SF-425 report is $64,442.00. However, the amount of Federal grant funds drawn down 
from the Department of Education’s Grants Management System (GMS) is only $1,798.00. This 
Federal expenditure discrepancy demonstrates a lack of internal controls to ensure Federal reports 
are accurate. Additionally, the $0 reported as non-Federal share on the Final SF-425 report means 
the $1,798 drawn down and expended from the GMS was not matched as required.  
 
As stated in the requirements, DVRS may not expend more in SE-A Federal funds than are matched 
and expended in SE-B Federal funds by the end of the period of performance. A review of the Final 
SF-425 report for H187A220044 demonstrates $62,333.00 reported as Federal expenditures. 
However, the amount of Federal grant funds drawn down from the GMS is only $23,170.51. This 
Federal expenditure discrepancy demonstrates a lack of internal controls to ensure Federal reports 
are accurate. Additionally, since $0 was the final match determination for the SE-B award, this 
means the $23,170.51 drawn down and expended from the GMS for the SE-A award was not 
allowable.  
 
Because DVRS reported no non-Federal SE-B expenditures in FFY 2022, DVRS was not permitted 
to draw down and expend any SE-B or SE-A funds. Despite this, DVRS drew down and expended 
$1,798.00 in SE-B funds and $23,170.51 in SE-A funds, considered unallowable expenditures for 
H187B220044 and H187A220044, respectively. 
 
Given the period of performance issues identified earlier in this report, RSA has additional concerns 
regarding DVRS’ ability to accurately track the proper supported employment expenditures to the 
program. Therefore, RSA questions the validity of the costs DVRS is charging to the Supported 
Employment program. DVRS must ensure all Supported Employment expenditures, including non-
Federal expenditures incurred for the provision of services to youth with the most significant

48 
 
disabilities, are accurately reported, and that a monitoring protocol is implemented to ensure 
ongoing agency compliance with Supported Employment matching and reporting requirements.  
 
Conclusion: DVRS is not ensuring the accuracy of expenditures charged to the Supported  
Employment program. The SF-425 reports submitted by DVRS for award H187B220044 do not 
reconcile with the draw down information identified in the GMS. Thus, RSA is unable to rely on the 
accuracy of the financial data reported. Additionally, DVRS failed to ensure the drawdown 
requirements related to meeting the non-Federal share and proportionality requirements were met 
prior to drawing down and expending Supported Employment award funds.  
 
Corrective Actions 3.4 RSA requires that DVRS— 
 
3.4.1  Within two quarters after the issuance of the final monitoring report, develop and implement 
policies and procedures, including a monitoring component, that ensure only allowable non-

3.4.2  Within two quarters after the issuance of the final monitoring report, develop and implement

synthesized, mapped and accurately reported in the SF-425 data elements for each report.
