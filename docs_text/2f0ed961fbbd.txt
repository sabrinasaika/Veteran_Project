IV. CONCLUSIONSOFLAW 
1. Congress passed the R-S Act in 1936 to provide blind and visually impaired 
persons with increased employment opportunities by allowing them to operate vending 
machines and cafeterias on federal property. 20 U.S.C. § 107(b). Under the R-S Act, blind 
vendors register with a State Licensing Agency ("SLA"), which in turn applies for contracts 
on federal property to operate vending machines or cafeterias. AB such, the SLA determines 
the blind persons assigned to awarded contracts. The Colorado Department of Labor and 
6

Employment, Division of Vocational Rehabilitation and Business Enterprise Program is the 
SLAfor the State of Colorado. The R-S Act expressly provides SLAs statutory priority for the 
continued operation of vending facilities and cafeterias on federal property. 20 U.S.C. § 
107d(e.). 
2. The Competition in Contracting Act ( CICA), the Federal Acquisition Regulation 
(FAR) and the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation (DF AR) apply "except in cases of other 
procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute." 10 U.S.C. § 2304(a)(l)4, 41 U.S.C. § 
330f. The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. AutomatedComm. Syst. V. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 
570, 577-78 (2001); NISH 11. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003); MSH 11. Cohen, 247 F.3d 
197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the FAR and DFARs regulations do not apply to R-S Act 
procurements, insofar as they conflict with the R-S Act or its implementing regulations. 
3. The SLA is not required to file a pre-award bid protest, because the law does not 
require the doing of a futile act. Whether filed in the Government Accountability Office, the 
United States Court ofClaims, or other venue, such a protest would be dismissed as not ripe for 
adjudication. 
4. The R-S Act's priority must be implemented by placing an SLA's bid in the 
competitive range where the bid may be made acceptable through discussions.

which an SLA places a bid, the agency must evaluate the bid pwsuant to the R-S Act; it is

D. The R-S Act Cannot be Voided by Failure ofa Federal Agencies to 
Establish Competitive Range when Evaluating an SI.A's Proposal 
Colorado is correct that Schriever's failure to establish a competitive range 
and place Colorado in the competitive range where meaningful discussions could have 
resulted in Colorado1s bid being made acceptable violated the R-S Act. Schriever 
incorrectly interprets 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e). Schriever1s interpretation places undue 
authority in the hands ofa contracting officer when evaluating an SI.A's bid. The R-S 
Act directs all Federal agencies to establish, wherever feasible, on or more vending 
facilities on all Federal property. 20 U.S.C. § 107(b)(2). The only exception is where 
such vending facility would "adversely affect the interests ofthe United States.,, Id. 
Only the Secretary of Education has the authority to determine whether the 
placement of a vending facility7 would adversely affect the interests ofthe United 
States. Id. 
The R-S Act's implementing regulation at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) requires that 
contracting officers establish a competitive range when evaluating contracts that

pertain to the operation of a cafeteria. A contracting officer does not have the 
authority to eviscerate the R-S Act by simply foregoing the establishment of a 
competitive range. Hall Federal agencies decided to forego the establishment of a

V. CONCLUSIONS OF LAW 
1. Congress passed the RSA in 1936 to provide blind persons with increased 
employment opportunities through the operation of vending facilities on federal property. 20 
U.S.C. § 107(b). The RSA itself has not been amended or revised since 1974 and the applicable 
Department of Education (DOE) regulations, 34 C.F.R. §§ 395. I -395.38 have not been amended 
or revised since their implementation in 1977. Under the RSA, blind vendors do not apply 
directly for government contracts. 20 U.S.C. § 107a(a)(5). The RSA requires blind vendors to 
register with a State Licensing Agency, which in tum applies for contracts involving the 
operation of vending facilities. 34 C.F.R. § 395.33. The State Licensing Agencies bid for 
particular federal contracts, and then assign blind vendors to operate the facility. See 34 C.F.R. 
§§ 395.2-395.17. As such, the State Licensing Agency determines the number of blind persons 
assigned to awarded contracts. The Colorado Department of Labor and Employment, Division of 
Vocational Rehabilitation and Business Enterprise Program, is the State Licensing Agency for 
the State of Colorado.

2. The RSA also establishes an arbitration process designed to resolve disputes 
amongst parties involved in bidding under the Act. When a State Licensing Agency believes a 
federal agency has failed to comply with the RSA, the State Licensing Agency may file a 
complaint for arbitration with the Secretary of Education. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-l(b). Section 107d-
2(a) of the RSA provides that the arbitration panel "shall ... give notice, conduct a hearing, and 
render its decision which shall be subject to appeal and review as a final agency action" by the 
SLA. "A person suffering legal wrong because of agency action, or adversely affected or 
aggrieved by agency action within the meaning of a relevant statute, is entitled to judicial review 
thereof." 5 U.S.C. § 702. The APA limits judicial review to "persons" suffering a legal wrong 
because of agency action. As such, the agency has no right to judicial review under the AP A 
because it is not a "person" with respect to arbitration panel decisions. 
3. Section 107d-2(b)(2) describes the process to convene an arbitration panel in 
response to a complaint filed by a State Licensing Agency. This section also provides that the 
authority of the panel is limited to determining whether the acts or practices of the agency are in 
violation of the RSA or its implementing regulations. If the panel so finds, the head of the 
agency shall cause such acts or practices to be terminated promptly and shall take such other 
action as may be necessary to carry out the decision of the panel. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(b) 
(emphasis added). The panel does not have authority to enact remedial measures; such panel

B. The Air Force did not violate the RSA and its implementing regulations 
by not placing Colorado in a competitive range. 
The ultimate issue before this Panel is whether the Air Force violated the RSA or its 
implementing regulations by not establishing a competitive range as part of its evaluation 
process. The SLA failed to meet its burden under 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a) and 5 U.S.C. § 556(d) 
because the SLA fails to identify any express provision in the RSA or its implementing 
regulations that expressly requires a procuring agency to establish a competitive range for such 
procurements. With respect to the RSA, the term "competitive range" is not mentioned at all in 
the Act; it is only mentioned, but not defined, in the implementing regulations. See 34 C.F .R. § 
395.33{b). For RSA procurements, Federal agencies routinely follow the guidance applicable to 
26

"competitive range" specified in the FAR§§ 15.305 - 15.306. The term "priority" is not defined 
in the RSA or implementing regulations. See 20 U.S.C. § I07e; 34 C.F.R. § 395.1. 
Section 395.33(b) provides that when a federal agency contemplates a cafeteria contract, 
the agency shall invite the SLA to respond to the agency solicitation. Such solicitations shall 
contain criteria under which all responses will be judged. See 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). This section 
further provides that if the proposal submitted by the SLA is judged to be within a competitive 
range and ranked among those proposals which have a reasonable chance of being selected for 
final award, the agency shall consult with the Secretary as required by section 395.33(a). It 
should be noted that this section contains the word "shall" in three distinct parts mandating 
specific action by the agency. However, the sentence pertaining to the competitive range does 
not contain such mandatory language. The drafters of the implementing regulation had the 
opportunity to mandate that the agency establish a competitive range for solicitations when a 
cafeteria contract is contemplated, but no such language is present. Based upon a fair reading of 
the express terms of section 395.33(b) and the absence of the word "shall," the establishment of a 
"competitive range" by a federal contracting officer is not required by the RSA or its 
implementing regulations. As Judge Brimmer determined during the preliminary injunction 
hearing on September 18, 2020, the decision to establish a competitive range or not is left to the 
discretion of the contracting officer under the provisions of the Federal Acquisition Regulation. 
See FAR§ 15.306(a)(3); Respondent Exhibit N, p. 163, lines 5-11 ["... the agency may either 
decide to conduct 'discussions', which are essentially negotiations, with the offerors, or it may

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has expressly held that if a party fails 
to object to an ambiguity or patent error in a solicitation prior to the solicitation closing time, it 
waives the ability to raise the objection subsequent to the closing date. Blue & Gold Fleet, L.P. v. 
United States, 492 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2007). The Court explained that it would be inefficient 
and costly to allow dissatisfied parties to protest the terms of the solicitation after offerors and 
the agency had expended considerable time and effort submitting or evaluating proposals in 
response to a defective solicitation. Id. at 1315. Rather than challenge the plain terms of the 
solicitation prior to submitting its offer, the SLA opted to bid on the contract. But "'[v]endors 
cannot sit on their rights to challenge what they believe is an unfair solicitation, roll the dice and 
see if they receive award [sic] and then, if unsuccessful, claim the solicitation was infirm."' Id. 
The waiver rule has been applied to cases involving the RSA. In North Carolina Division 
ofServices for the Blindv. United States, the Court held where there is a "deficiency or problem 
in a solicitation ...the proper procedure for the offeror to follow is not to wait to see if it is the 
successful offeror before deciding whether to challenge the procurement, but rather to raise the 
objection in a timely fashion." North Carolina Division ofServices for the Blind v. United States, 
53 Fed. CL 147, 165 (Fed. Cl. 2002). In Moore's Cafeteria Services v. United States, the Court 
applied the holding in Blue & Gold Fleet and held that the "plaintiff [Moore's] had the

VII. CONCLUSION 
The Air Force was not required by law or regulation to establish a competitive range or 
conduct discussions for this procurement. As Judge Brimmer noted in his decision, the 
contracting officer's decision to not establish a competitive range and enter into discussions was 
not an abuse of discretion. The published Solicitation notified all potential o:fferors, to include 
the SLA, that there would likely be no discussions and as such, all offerors were on notice to 
submit their best and final offers. After evaluation of the submitted proposals by a Source 
Selection Evaluation Board, the contract was awarded to NRDC. The SLA was not awarded the 
contract due to its low score on past performance and exceptionally high price. The Air Force's 
procedures in preparing and issuing the Solicitation and evaluation of the proposals complied 
with the FAR, the RSA, and the RSA implementing regulations. As there was no competitive 
range established, the provisions of34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) to consult the Secretary of Education 
if the SLA's proposal was in the competitive range did not apply. 
There is no "feasibility standard" for contract awards in the RSA. Rather, federal 
agencies should seek to have an RSA vending facility located on federal property, "wherever 
feasible" depending on ample space and patronage. Petitioner's creation of a new evaluation 
standard for cafeteria contracts, the "feasibility standard," is misplaced.

procurements. Further, Judge Brimmer determined that the definition of the term "competitive 
range" applicable to the Schriever Space Force FFS contract is defined by FAR Section 15.306 
in effect at the time of the solicitation's issuance. Finally, Judge Brimmer determined that the 
Handbook was not entitled to any Auer deference. 
Despite a federal district court judge ruling on these three issues, the panel decision 
asserts contrary views to all three issues: (1) the FAR does NOT apply to R-S Act procurements, 
(2) the FAR definition of competitive range does NOT apply, and (3) the rescinded DOE 
Handbook is entitled to Auer deference.
