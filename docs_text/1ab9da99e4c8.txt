I. 	INTRODUCTION
A. 	JURISDICTION
This arbitration panel has jurisdiction pursuant to the Randolph-Sheppard Act, 20 U.S.C.
§§ 107 through 107f (R-S Act), which authorizes the Secretary of the Department of Education (DoE) to convene a panel to arbitrate a dispute between a State Licensing Agency[] and a federal department and to issue a final and binding decision. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-1(b).
Petitioner Arizona Department of Economic Security (ADES), the designated SLA pursuant to the R-S Act, designated Susan Rockwood Gashel as arbitrator, and the United States Department of the Army (Army) designated J. Mackey Ives as arbitrator. Betty Rankin Widgeon was jointly designated by arbitrators Gashel and Ives as Panel Chair. The hearing took place on March 19 and 20, 2024, in Phoenix, Arizona. ADES was represented by Andrew Freeman and Neel Lalchandani. Army was represented by Gerald R. Krimbill and Samuel E. Gregory.
From 2004 to the present, ADES has provided full food services at Fort Huachuca for multiple dining facilities subject to a series of contracts. On March 25, 2022, the Army issued Solicitation W9124J-21-R-0027 (Solicitation), requesting proposals for a new five-year full food services contract at Fort Huachuca. The Solicitation identified three criteria by which proposals would be evaluated: (1) technical capability, (2) past performance, and (3) price. The technical capability factor consisted of two subfactors: (i) key personnel and qualification and (ii) staffing plan. On May 12, 2022, ADES timely submitted a proposal in response to the Solicitation. By letter dated January 20, 2023, the Army notified ADES that its proposal in response to the Solicitation was excluded from the competitive range. The Army rated ADES’s proposal as acceptable for subfactor 1 of the technical capability factor (key personnel and qualification) but rated the proposal as unacceptable for subfactor 2 (staffing plan). The Army also rated ADES’s past performance as acceptable. Also, on January 20, 2023, ADES requested a pre-award debriefing pursuant to Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) 15.503 and FAR 505(a)(1), which the Army provided via letter dated January 26, 2023; on March 6, 2023, ADES requested reconsideration of the Army’s exclusion of it from the competitive range, and via letter dated April 3, 2023, the Army denied the requested reconsideration.
B. 	BACKGROUND
ADES filed this arbitration in May 2023. On June 2, 2023, ADES sued the Secretary of the Army and filed its motion for a preliminary injunction to prevent the Army from awarding the food services contract at Fort Huachuca to a different provider while this arbitration was pending. By Order dated September 11, 2023, the United States District Court for the District of Arizona granted ADES’s motion for a preliminary injunction requiring the Army to maintain ADES as the food services vendor at Fort Huachuca until there is a final decision in the pending arbitration before the DOE or until further order of the Court.
On October 3, 2023, the DOE authorized the convening of an arbitration panel in this matter, stating:
[t]he central issues to be addressed are whether the Army violated the Randolph-Sheppard Act and implementing regulations by finding the SLA’s bid technically unacceptable and without meaningful discussions, applying the solicitation criteria unfairly, and ultimately excluding the SLA from the competitive range.
C. 	ISSUES
The issues set out in the Complaint authorized to be addressed by this Panel are:
Did the Department of the Army violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by judging ADES’s bid to be technically unacceptable?
Did the Department of the Army violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by excluding ADES’s bid from the competitive range even though, to the extent there were any deficiencies with the proposal, the proposal could have been made acceptable through meaningful discussions?
If the panel finds that the Army should have included ADES’s proposal in the competitive range, did the Department of the Army violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by failing to consult with the
Secretary of Education to have the Secretary of Education determine
/whether ADES can operate the full food service contract at Fort Huachuca at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees?
Did the Department of the Army violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by failing to consistently apply the criteria stated in the Solicitation to all bidders?
Did the Department of the Army violate the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by relying on factors other than the criteria stated in the Solicitation when evaluating the proposals and setting the competitive range?
DoE’s October 3, 2023 letter advised the Panel that question 3 may be considered if the Panel finds one or more of the alleged violations of the R-S Act and implementing regulations.

On October 11, 2023, ADES submitted its discovery request to the Army. On November 10, 2023, ADES submitted its Motion for Entry of a Protective Order, seeking to safeguard competition-sensitive or otherwise protectable confidential information likely to be disclosed orally or in writing during the course of the arbitration. Given that the Army has no supportable grounds for refusing to comply with the Panel’s discovery order, and given that the Panel unquestionably has the right to order discovery in order to “assure the production of the most credible evidence available,” as pointed out by the Army, there is no legal impediment to the issuance of adverse inferences. II. 	STANDARD OF REVIEW
This Panel is directed by the DoE to, in accordance with the provisions of subchapter II of chapter 5 of Title 5, give notice, conduct a hearing, and render its decision. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a). That subchapter provides, at 5 U.S.C.§ 556(d), that the proponent of an order has the burden of proof and an order may issue “in accordance with the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence.” Id. Substantial evidence “means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 292, 300 (1938).
III. 	FINDINGS OF FACT
A. 	ADES’s Proposal
Mr. Scott Weber (“Weber”) is the blind vendor assigned to Ft. Huachuca; he has operated the ADES contract since December 2013.
Weber operates three facilities: two cafeterias and a remote facility site at Black Tower. The largest cafeteria is Weinstein, which serves soldiers participating in Advanced Individual Training. The smaller cafeteria is Thunderbird, which serves officers.
ADES and its licensed blind vendors have consistently provided high-quality full food services, including cooking, staffing, menu planning, food storage and serving, and cleaning, among other tasks, to the soldiers and personnel at Fort Huachuca. This is reflected in the Army’s Contractor Performance Assessment Reports (CPARs).
ADES’s staffing schedule is based on historical data, its use of cross-utilization
(or cross-training), its managers being working managers, and the fact that Black Tower is only open during the lunch shift; the employees there could work at the other two dining facilities as needed.
Weber has long used cross training to improve the efficiency of the food services operation and comply with the required union contract provision that all employees have to be full-time. Hence, those working at Black Tower for lunch are assigned to Weinstein and Thunderbird for either dinner or breakfast. This was referenced in the technical proposal.
B. 	The Army’s Response
When the Army evaluated ADES’s proposal, it concluded that the cashier position was “significantly” or “grossly” understaffed, that the Food Sanitation Specialist and Dining Facility Attendant positions were “somewhat” understaffed and that two staffing scenarios at Thunderbird were not included. Those scenarios involved shifting to 100% takeout (which had only happened during the pandemic) and a two-week holiday break (exodus) when Weinstein closes and only brunch and supper are served at Thunderhead (100% takeout and exodus scenarios)
On March 6, 2023, ADES offered the following response:
it had indeed provided staffing for the cashier position, under the titles admin/cashier and office clerk, pointing out that the Army had ignored ADES’s actual experience in running the Fort Huachuca cafeterias, as reflected in the CPARs provided as exhibits at the hearing;
the Army’s evaluation did not account for employee cross-training; and
the Army ignored that ADES did provide pricing for 100% takeout and exodus scenarios. ADES further offered to immediately provide the staffing matrix for these scenarios.
The Army responded, acknowledging that “hours are ample for managing the headcount and admin functions.” This left as the only omission the lack of a staffing matrix for the 100% takeout and exodus scenarios.
The Army added a new rationale for rejecting ADES’s proposal, that ADES did not have adequate controls over the $25 change fund.
The Army did not review ADES’s March 6 letter as part of the reevaluation, and thus, did not take advantage of the opportunity to obtain the 100% take-out and exodus scenarios.
The Solicitation called for the Army to evaluate price before establishing a competitive range; yet the Army did not evaluate the offerors’ pricing, indeed, so long as an offeror’s bid was not understaffed, it did not matter that a bid was significantly overstaffed.
Mr. Manuel Grajeda participated in the development of the IGCE and later received a conditional offer of employment from a bidder, ORSA, that was later placed in the competitive range.
Mr. Kendall Brown was a former inspector of ADES’s performance at Ft. Huachuca and later Senior Vice President of Food Services at ORSA.
IV. 	CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
A. 	Legal Background
Congress passed the R-S Act "for the purposes of providing blind persons with remunerative employment, enlarging the economic opportunities of the blind, and stimulating the blind to greater efforts in striving to make themselves self-supporting." 20 U.S.C. § 107(a). The Secretary of DoE is tasked with ensuring the purpose of the R-S Act is carried out by prescribing regulations and enforcing the statute and regulations. 20 U.S.C. §§ 107a(a), 107(b), 107d-1, 107d-3(e).
With respect to cafeterias, Congress directed the Secretary to prescribe regulations “to establish a priority for the operation of cafeterias on Federal property by blind licensees when [the Secretary] Determines … that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided to employees.” That standard governs when a federal agency reviews an SLA’s proposal.
The R-S Act and implementing regulations control over the FAR. The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA) and the FAR apply “except in case of other procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute.” 10 U.S.C. § 3201(a), The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst
v. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001), NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003), NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the CICA and FAR do not apply to R-S Act procurements insofar as they conflict with the regulations implementing the R-S Act.
The regulation at issue in this arbitration (Cafeteria Regulation) is at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33, which states the following:
Priority in the operation of cafeterias by blind vendors on Federal property shall be afforded when the Secretary determines, on an individual basis, and after consultation with the appropriate property managing department, agency, or instrumentality, that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees, whether by contract or otherwise. Such operation shall be expected to provide maximum employment opportunities to blind vendors to the greatest extent possible.
In order to establish the ability of blind vendors to operate a cafeteria in such a manner as to provide food service at comparable cost and of comparable high quality as that available from other providers of cafeteria services, the appropriate State licensing agency shall be invited to respond to solicitations for offers when a cafeteria contract is contemplated by the appropriate property managing department, agency, or instrumentality. Such solicitations for offers shall establish criteria under which all responses will be judged. Such criteria may include sanitation practices, personnel, staffing, menu pricing and portion sizes, menu variety, budget and accounting practices. If the proposal received from the State licensing agency is judged to be within a competitive range and has been ranked among those proposals which have a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, the property managing department, agency, or instrumentality shall consult with the Secretary as required under paragraph (a) of this section. If the State licensing agency is dissatisfied with an action taken relative to its proposal, it may file a complaint with the Secretary under the provisions of § 395.37
.…
Notwithstanding the requirements of paragraphs (a) and (b) of this section, Federal property managing departments, agencies, and instrumentalities may afford priority in the operation of cafeterias by blind vendors on Federal property through direct negotiations with State licensing agencies whenever such department, agency, or instrumentality determines, on an individual basis, that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees: Provided, however, That the provisions of paragraphs (a) and (b) of this section shall apply in the event that the negotiations authorized by this paragraph do not result in a contract.
In 1977 when the Cafeteria Regulation was adopted, the understanding of the term competitive range “meant that any proposal ‘must be considered to be within a competitive range so as to require negotiations unless it is so technically inferior or out of line with regard to price that meaningful discussions are precluded.’” To the Sec’y of the Air Force, 48 Comp. Gen. 314, 317 (Nov. 13, 1968). Further, the FAR in place at the time the Cafeteria Regulation was promulgated provided that “[w]hen there is doubt as to whether a proposal is in the competitive range, the proposal should be included . . . the competitive range includes ‘all proposals that have a reasonable chance of being selected for award.’” 48 C.F.R. § 15.609 (1995 version).
The DOE, in 1988, issued its Handbook. Chapter VII announced: Considering the clear intent of the [Randolph-Sheppard] Act, the determining factor for judging whether a proposal [of the SLA] should be within the competitive range is if the offer can be made acceptable by conducting meaningful discussions.
…
A proposal must be considered to be within the competitive range unless it is technically inferior or contains unduly high selling prices to patrons that the possibility of being made acceptable through meaningful negotiations is precluded.
While the DoE Handbook was retired in 2017, in 2021 Acting Deputy
Commissioner of DoE’s Office of Special Education and Rehabilitative Services advised:
Retirement of the outdated handbook does not preclude Federal property managers from following the substance of the Randolph-Sheppard Act regarding cafeterias, including military dining facilities (20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e)). The Department recognizes that while portions of the handbook are outdated, other sections, including Chapter VII, do provide valuable guidance to our stakeholders and may continue to be consulted.
Because DoE is charged with implementation of the R-S Act, its “policy decisions are entitled to deference.” NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197, 202 (4th Cir.
2001).
Accordingly, this Panel concludes that the Army was required to evaluate ADES’s bid so as to include it within the competitive range, given that the determining factor for judging whether an SLA proposal should be within the competitive range is whether the offer can be made acceptable by conducting meaningful discussions.
Because the Army failed to follow the Solicitation’s requirements to include pricing in the evaluation before establishing the competitive range, and because the Army failed to provide discovery so as to enable this Panel to meaningfully evaluate ADES’s claims, the Panel concludes that the Army failed to consistently apply the criteria stated in the Solicitation to all bidders, and that the Army relied on factors other than those stated in the Solicitation when evaluating the proposals and setting the competitive range.
V. 	ANALYSIS AND DECISION
The Randolph-Sheppard Act prescribes processes and procedures for blind vendors that do not require everyone to be treated “the same” in the solicitation process. The Department of the Army violated the Randolph-Sheppard Act and regulations by excluding ADES’s bid from the competitive range even though, to the extent there were any deficiencies with the proposal, the panel could have been made acceptable through meaningful discussions.
The Panel has carefully considered all the issues presented. Having done so, it agrees with ADES’s perspective that Issue 2 provides an appropriate and efficient path to the resolution of this dispute. The R-S Act requires that a proposal from a State Licensing Agency should be included in a competitive range if any deficiencies it has could be made acceptable through meaningful discussion. The parties present different positions on the appropriate definition of the term “competitive range.” ADES favors the description provided in the DoE’s 1977 Cafeteria regulation, the definition that was in use at the time the R-S Act’s regulation was drafted. Chapter VII of the DoE Randolph-Sheppard Handbook makes clear that an SLA’s proposal must be included within the competitive range “if the offer can be made acceptable by conducting meaningful discussions.”
The Army argues that the current FAR definition of “competitive range” should be used because it governs other contracting interactions in which the Army is involved. It argues that the five-step solicitation process provided in FAR § 15.306 prevents the Army from being able to conduct meaningful discussions before the competitive range is set. However, ADES’s argument is not that meaningful discussions must be conducted before the competitive range is set. The R-S Act does not require that proposals that have been made acceptable through meaningful discussions be included in the competitive range; rather, it argues that the kind of proposal that could be made acceptable through meaningful discussions should be included in the competitive range.
The Department of Education’s interpretation of its own R-S regulations is entitled to deference. Given the cafeteria regulation's text, structure, purpose, and specific application, this panel finds that it controls where conflict arises.
The Army initially identified two categories of deficiencies with the ADES’s proposal:
understaffing and the absence of a proposal for two scenarios at the Thunderbird cafeteria. Testimony confirmed that both of these deficiencies are of the nature that could have been remedied or demonstrated not to have been outcome-determinative with meaningful discussions, especially given ADES’s demonstrated history of satisfactory work performance under previous contracts. For these reasons, the panel finds that the R-S Act and its implementing regulations required the Army to place ADES’s proposal in the competitive range because it could have been made acceptable through meaningful discussions, and the Army violated the R-S Act by failing to do so.
With respect to issues 1, 4, and 5, the Panel holds, based on the Army’s failure to comply, with the Panel’s order regarding production of documents, and the Panel’s subsequent granting of adverse inferences herein, that it must rule in favor of ADES. With respect to issue 3, the Panel holds that an SLA’s bid cannot be rejected without the on-site official consulting with the Secretary of Education.
VI.   	CONCLUSION
Accordingly, as required by 20 U.S.C. § 107, the head of the Army “shall cause such acts or practices to be terminated promptly and shall take such other action as may be necessary to carry out the decision of the panel.”

DISSENTING OPINION
Pursuant to the Randolph-Sheppard Act (RSA), 20 U.S.C. §§ 107 through 107f, an arbitration was convened in the above-captioned matter, as authorized by 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2.  Petitioner Arizona Department of Economic Security (ADES), the designated State Licensing Agency for Arizona, nominated Susan Gashel as its panel representative, and the United States Department of the Army (Army), Fort Huachuca, Arizona, nominated J. Mackey Ives as its panel representative. Ms. Betty Widgeon was appointed as Panel Chair by the Department of Education (DOE). The hearing took place on March 19-20, 2024 in Phoenix, Arizona. The Panel Majority issued its written Decision and Award for R/S 23-05 on June 17, 2024 finding that the Army had violated the RSA in its evaluation of Solicitation W9124J-20-R-0027 for the eventual award of a full food service (FFS) contract at Fort Huachuca. For the reasons discussed in this Opinion, the undersigned Panel Member respectfully dissents.
It should be noted that prior Orders of the Panel relating to approval of a Protective Order, Discovery, and Adverse Inferences were approved by the Panel Majority over the written Dissents of the undersigned Panel Member. This arbitration is essentially a pre-award bid protest that should have been filed with the U.S. Court of Federal Claims (COFC) under the Tucker Act, as amended by the Administrative Dispute Resolution Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-230, § 12, 110 Stat. 3870 (revising 28 U.S.C.§ 1491(b) of the General Accounting Office (GAO), both of which have published guidance relating to protective orders, discovery, and adverse inferences. A review of the RSA and its implementing regulations (34 C.F.R. §§ 395.1-395.38) and the 1978 Revised Interim Policies and Procedures that govern the processing of RSA arbitrations reveals that there is no authority or guidance relating to the issuance of protective orders, discovery, or the issuance of adverse inferences in a DOE convened RSA arbitration. Had this pre-award bid protest been filed in the U.S Court of Federal Claims or GAO, the petitioner’s claim would have been dismissed as was Cantu Service’s Inc. bid protest to the General Accounting Office (GAO). See Respondent’s Post-Hearing Brief, p.14. Petitioner’s claim that the Army unfairly evaluated ADES’s proposal per the Solicitation criteria is a classic bid protest claim. See, e.g., The State of Oklahoma and Cantu Services Inc. v. United States, No. 19-1022C, August 12, 2019, U.S. Court of Federal Claims (J. Letow). The Department of Education erroneously convened an arbitration panel in this matter and should have referred the petitioner to the COFC or GAO.  DOE convened arbitration panels do not currently have the proper legal authority with respect to discovery, protective orders, and issuance of adverse inferences. Until such authority or guidance is provided, the DOE should not convene further arbitrations regarding matters that could be adjudicated in the COFC or the GAO.
I. BACKGROUND
In their September 15, 2023 amended complaint to the Department of Education seeking arbitration pursuant to 20 U.S.C. §§ 107d-1(b) and 107d-2 of the Randolph Sheppard Act, ADES alleged, without identifying the specific provisions of the Act, that the Army violated the RSA. Petitioner’s September 15th letter further asked that the arbitration panel consider four key issues, any of which would “establish that the Army violated the RSA and its implementing regulations when it failed to award the Fort Huachuca full food service contract to ADES.”  Their complaint briefly discusses the four key issues and concludes by requesting that an arbitration panel be convened to consider six (6) questions.
In their October 3, 2023 response to ADES’ September 15, 2023 amended complaint, the Department of Education authorized the convening of an arbitration panel to hear and render a decision on the issues raised in the complaint. The “central issues to be addressed are whether the Army violated the Randolph-Sheppard Act and implementing regulations by finding the SLA’s bid technically unacceptable and without meaningful discussions, applying the solicitation criteria unfairly, and ultimately excluding the SLA from the competitive range. Questions 1, 2, 4, and 5 that the SLA raises in the conclusion of its complaint are part of determining these central issues.”  The convening letter, like the complaint, does not identify the specific provisions of the Randolph Sheppard Act that the Army purportedly violated. A quick review of the RSA provisions indicates that the only statutory provision of the RSA that the Army purportedly violated would have been placing a “limitation on the placement or operation of a vending facility,” which was not alleged or asserted by the Petitioner. See 20 U.S.C. § 107(b). Petitioner’s complaint relies upon the Army’s purported violations of the promulgated implementing regulations, specifically 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.33(a) and (b), which are addressed below.
Nonetheless, the DOE amended convening letter does confirm that the although the panel’s responsibility is to determine if the Army violated the RSA and its implementing regulations, the panel has no authority to order the agency to undertake any particular remedy. It is the Army’s responsibility to cease the violation and to take the steps necessary to carry out the arbitration panel’s decision. The panel has no authority to direct, mandate, or recommend any remedial measures to the Secretary of the Army, or the Secretary’s designee. In fact, the Secretary is under no obligation to enact any remedial measures, if he or she determines none are necessary.
II. INTRODUCTION
As the Army posits in their post hearing brief, this case is actually quite simple. The Army did not violate any express provisions of the RSA or its implementing regulations, the threshold requirement for the DOE to convene an arbitration hearing. Rather, the Army conducted a fair and equitable evaluation of nine submitted proposals, including the proposal submitted by ADES, based upon the Solicitation’s criteria. For almost twenty years, Mr. Scott Weber and his company Thunder Mountain Enterprises had themselves prepared and submitted their proposals every five years for the Fort Huachuca food service contract and been declared the awardee each time. Unfortunately, in 2022, Mr. Weber and an ADES committee of blind vendors hired an outside French company, Sodexho, to prepare ADES’s proposal in response to Solicitation W9124J-20-R-0027 (“Solicitation”). The Sodexho proposal was fraught with errors and as the evidence at the hearing showed, contained incomplete information and incoherent provisions resulting in their exclusion from the competitive range.
The Army’s post-hearing brief in paragraphs 14-19, 24, 26, and 28-29 clearly illustrates that the Solicitation advised bidders that they needed to submit their best and final proposals as there would be no discussions. Neither ADES nor any of the other nine bidders were permitted to revise their proposals once they were submitted, according to the published Solicitation. Once a proposal was initially evaluated and excluded from the competitive range, no further action was required by the Army evaluators for that proposal. The Solicitation clearly stated that if an offeror’s proposal was not placed in the competitive range, the proposal was excluded, and no discussions were permitted with that offeror. See Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, pages 10-11. Five offerors submitted proposals that did not make it into the competitive range. Id. at p.12. The Army was not required to discuss the specifics of these five rejected proposals with their offerors. It is unfortunate that Sodexho prepared an inadequate proposal on ADES behalf, but the RSA “priority” does not entitle or guarantee a licensed blind vendor perpetual award of a contract. Each proposal is evaluated on its own merits for each Solicitation. Contrary to petitioner’s assertions, the RSA priority does not require automatic award to ADES. To favor an incumbent who has held the contract for twenty consecutive years over any other bidder is absolutely prohibited by federal procurement laws and regulations.
The Source Selection Evaluation Board found that ADES’s proposal (prepared by Sodexho) left two elements at Building 52107 completely blank, and that several labor bands were understaffed at several facilities and mealtimes. See Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, paras. 32-36. As such, ADES proposal was found Unacceptable with respect to its Staffing Plan, and thus Unacceptable overall. Although Mr. Weber was aware that Sodexho’s staffing titles in the proposal differed from his, he did not review the final proposal submission to the Army. Because ADES proposal was deemed Unacceptable with respect to its Staffing Plan, it was excluded from the Competitive Range. Petitioner asserts that had they been given the opportunity after initial evaluations to discuss the matter, they could have explained the misunderstanding to the SSEB and Procurement Contracting Officer. Unfortunately, none of the offerors had the right under the express terms of the Solicitation to engage in discussions or to submit a revised proposal. See Respondents Post Hearing Brief, Tab 23, p. 2. The Army’s post-hearing brief outlining the deficiencies in ADES’s proposal is overwhelmingly convincing. See Respondent’s Post-Hearing Brief, paragraphs 18, 33a-k, and 34a-g. The Army evaluation team had no obligation or responsibility to speculate as to what certain positions or terms proffered by ADES meant or implied in their proposal. ADES failed to properly explain or complete their technical proposal sufficiently in accordance with the Solicitation requirements.
After being notified of their exclusion from the competitive range (CR), ADES requested a pre-award debriefing. On January 26, 2023, the Army provided ADES with a written pre-award debriefing concerning their exclusion from the CR in accordance with FAR 15.505. See Respondent’s Tab 23 and paragraph 37, Post Hearing Brief.  On March 6, 2023, ADES sent a letter to Mr. Vicory, the contracting officer, complaining about their exclusion from the CR. On March 8, 2023, Mr. Vicory forwarded ADES’ request to two subject matter experts (SMEs) on food services from the Army Sustainment Command (ASC) for them to conduct an “independent review” of the evaluation of ADES’s proposal. See Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, Tab 37.On March 8th, the two experts, Mr. Craig Schwarz and Mr. Wade Froehlich, informed Mr. Vicory that they had conducted their independent review and found ADES’s proposal “confusing in that the proposal does not adequately explain the functions for the personnel.” See Respondent’s Post Hearing  Brief, Tab 38 at 1.The ASC experts also noted other issues with ADES’ proposal, including inadequacies with ADES explanation of the cashier and head count functions. The two independent subject matter experts were unable to reconcile ADES confusion of “needed staffing” with ADES staffing matrix and technical volume data. After independently reviewing ADES’ proposal, the two procurement experts concluded that the unacceptable technical rating was justified. In providing ADES with this independent review, the Army had gone above and beyond their normal evaluation process. This review confirmed the SSEB’s finding of unacceptable for technical rating. See Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, paras. 43-50.
III. LEGAL ANALYSIS
The Army decision to exclude ADES’ proposal from the CR was a proper response to ADES’ failure to submit a complete and technically acceptable proposal. As the Army’s post hearing brief explains in convincing detail, ADES proposal was incomplete and incoherent, resulting in its exclusion from the CR. Contrary to petitioner’s assertion, the RSA does not provide an automatic priority such that an SLA can submit a wholly inadequate response to a competitive solicitation and be awarded a federal contract.
Rather, a priority is applied, if at all, only after the federal agency receives a competitive proposal from the SLA that is initially evaluated and determined to be in the CR of offerors with whom discussions will be held (Step 1 of the evaluation as described by Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, Tab 23, Respondent’s Pre-Award Debriefing). If the SLA proposal had been acceptable and placed in the CR during Step 1, the Army would have engaged in discussions with the SLA (ADES) and other offerors in the CR. If the proposal was excluded from the CR after initial evaluations, the proposal was excluded from the competition. ADES and four other offerors’ proposals were excluded from the CR due to the initial evaluations. After these discussions, if the Army determined that an offeror’s proposal should no longer be included in the CR, the proposal would be eliminated from consideration for award (Step 2). Those offerors remaining in the CR after discussions would submit a Final Proposal Revision (FPR) to the Army to be evaluated with no further discussions (Step 3). If the Army determines after the evaluation of the FPR’s that the FPR from the SLA is technically acceptable with the lowest evaluated reasonable price and acceptable past performance (Step 4), award will be made to the SLA subject to a determination of responsibility after consultation with the Secretary of Education. See Tab 23, Respondent’s Pre-Award Debriefing, p. 2.; 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a).  Because ADES’ proposal was evaluated as technically unacceptable for Factor One, Technical Capability Factor, during Step 1 of the evaluation process, it was excluded from the competitive range.
As the Army notes in its post-award brief, the burden of proof is upon the petitioner, not the Army, to prove that their exclusion from the CR was improper under the RSA. As noted, exclusion of any proposal from a CR is not discussed anywhere in the RSA or its implementing regulations. Rather, such violations, if any, may have been violative of CICA, the FAR, and DFAR. Purported violations of CICA, the FAR, and DFAR are not within the authority of a DOE convened panel’s review. Such matters are normally adjudicated by the U.S. Court of Federal Claims or the General Accounting Office. DOE convened arbitration panels are limited to determining whether the “acts or practices of. . .  [the Army] are in violation of . . . [the RSA or its implementing regulations],” not CICA, the FAR, or DFAR.
Section 395.33(b) of Title 34, Code of Federal Regulation provides that “if a State Licensing Agency is dissatisfied with an action taken relative to its proposal, it may file a complaint with the Secretary under the provisions of §395.37. This specific language in section 395.33(b), the main operational section of Title 34, limits the adjudication of an arbitration complaint to the evaluation of the SLA’s proposal and not the proposals of other offerors. Without any regulatory authority other than an extremely broad interpretation of the Revised Interim Policies, the Panel Majority determined that the Army violated the discovery process by not producing the proposals of the other eight (8) offerors, even though the Army cited statutory authorities that precluded it from doing so. As a result of this purported violation of discovery principles, the Panel Majority approved numerous adverse inferences proffered by the petitioner. In this case, the Army should not have been penalized because the DOE has not promulgated any federal regulations, guidance, or policy memorandum under the auspices of the RSA describing discovery obligations, protective orders, or adverse inferences. Rather, the panel members had to rely upon  the extremely ambiguous “discovery” provisions of Paragraph 9(b) of the Revised Interim Policies.
The Interim Policies were issued on February 3, 1978 and as the October 3, 2023 DOE convening letter states, the Secretary under the authority of 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2, uses the “Policies and Procedures for Convening and Conducting an Arbitration” to process arbitration complaints. No mention is made in DOE’s standard convening letter that any other regulation, statute, or policy memorandum governs the adjudication of an RSA arbitration panel. As such, panels do not have the authority to expand the scope of a DOE convened arbitration to include issuance of protective orders, order expansive discovery procedures and impose adverse inferences upon a federal agency that withholds release of procurement sensitive material under the authority of federal procurement statutes. It is incumbent upon the Department of Education to immediately revamp their woefully outdated Revised Interim Policies and provide meaningful guidance to the parties relating to discovery, protective orders, and adverse inferences. Such updated regulations are especially necessary if DOE decides to continue the practice convening of arbitration panels to adjudicate a de facto pre-award bid protest that should properly be filed with the US Court of Federal Claims or GAO. The Adverse Inferences relied upon by the petitioner and the Panel Majority in this case were ultra vires and should be dismissed.
THE ARMY DECISION TO EXCLUDE ADES’s PROPOSAL FROM THE COMPETITIVE RANGE WAS PROPER.
The Army properly complied with the requirements of the competitive selection process outlined in the RSA and its implementing regulations found at 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.1-395.38. Although the petitioner asserts that the Army violated the RSA and its implementing regulations by failing to place ADES in the competitive range, there is no provision in the RSA or the implementing regulations for the Army to automatically put ADES in the competitive range. Nor is there a provision in the RSA and its regulations to conduct discussions for any procurement. Selection for the competitive range must be earned based upon an evaluation of ADES’s proposal. There is no automatic right to place ADES’s proposal in the CR or automatic award under the RSA. In fact, the implementing regulations only required the federal agency (Army) to execute only three actions in this procurement, the third action being a contingent action. The three statutorily required actions under 34 C.F.R. 395.33(b) are: (1) the agency shall invite the State Licensing Agency (SLA) to respond to solicitations when a cafeteria contract is contemplated by the agency; (2) the solicitation shall establish criteria under which all responses will be judged, and a contingent requirement that (3) if the SLA proposal is placed in the competitive range AND is ranked among those proposals with a reasonable chance for award, the Army shall consult with the Secretary as required under 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a). The Army complied with requirements (1) and (2) but the conditions for requirement (3) were never met as ADES’s proposal was not placed in the CR and did not have a reasonable chance for award; hence, no consultation requirement under 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) was triggered.
Neither the RSA nor the implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) expressly require the Army to have discussions, establish a competitive range, award a contract, conduct pre-award debriefings, or provide any specific guidance as to how the proposals will be evaluated. These routine procurement actions conducted by a federal agency during a food service procurement are required under the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation (DFAR), and the Competition in Contracting Act (CICA). If the Army violated any laws or implementing regulations in the evaluation of ADES’ proposal, such purported violations were of specific procurement statutes (CICA) and the FAR and DFAR, not the RSA or its implementing regulations.
The Fort Huachuca solicitation was issued under the Small Business Act and the Randolph Sheppard Act. The solicitation was written in a manner in which both socioeconomic procurement statutes were given effect and eliminated any inherent conflict. Further, there was no conflict between the RSA and its implementing regulations and the FAR, DFAR, and CICA during the processing of the Fort Huachuca solicitation and pre-award evaluations. Contrary to petitioner’s assertions, the RSA and its implementing regulations expressly required the Army to only do two specific acts in the procurement – invite the SLA to respond to the food service solicitation and issue a solicitation with evaluation criteria, as noted infra. The Army performed both those express requirements; hence, the Army did not violate the RSA or its implementing regulations. While 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) mentions a competitive range, the detailed, specific guidance to establish and perform a competitive range analysis are not contained in the RSA or its regulations, but rather in the FAR and DFAR. The CICA, FAR and DFAR provide specific and detailed guidance regarding the myriad of tasks and actions required in a FFS procurement, as the RSA and its regulations are silent on the evaluation of offers, competitive range execution, award without discussions, rejection of offers, debriefings, and other contracting matters. The CICA, FAR, and DFAR govern the evaluation of offers, the competitive range, conduct of discussions, award without discussions, and a myriad of other contracting decisions about which the R-S Act and its regulations are silent. In this procurement, the RSA and CICA/FAR/DFAR did not conflict, but rather worked seamlessly together. In fact, the Army procurement was a small business set-aside which normally would have excluded RSA vendors in the past as the SLA’s are not classified as small businesses. Rather, the Army MICC developed a comprehensive solicitation to allow both small businesses and RSA vendors to compete as CICA promotes and encourages competition.
The evaluation process, establishment of a competitive range, and pre-award debriefings are actions required and outlined in CICA, the FAR, and the DFARs. To claim that the Army violated the RSA by not placing ADES in the competitive range may have been a violation of CICA or the FAR, but certainly was not a violation of the RSA or its implementing regulations. Accordingly, it is paramount that the DOE require SLA’s that submit a complaint seeking an arbitration hearing identify the specific provision of the RSA or its implementing regulations that the federal agency purportedly violated. For too long, the DOE has glossed over this significant requirement to have the SLA “set forth the specific provisions of the Act or regulations. . . which the Federal agency is alleged to have failed to comply.”  Revised Interim Policies, paragraph 3, page 2. For too long, the DOE has routinely granted SLA’s an arbitration hearing (to which non-licensed blind vendors are not eligible to receive) when the federal agency has not actually violated an express provision of the RSA or its implementing regulations. Rather, the purported violation by a federal agency during a food service procurement is likely a violation of procurement law CICA, the FAR and DFAR, if any.
The RSA does not provide an automatic priority such that an SLA can submit an incomplete proposal to a competitive solicitation and be awarded a federal contract. Rather, the SLA must first submit a proposal that if it is judged to be within a competitive range and has been ranked among those proposals which have a reasonable chance for final award, the federal agency (Army) shall consult with the Secretary of Education under 395.33(a) (emphasis added). It should be noted that the mandatory requirement to consult with the Secretary of Education only applies IF the offeror’s proposal is judged to be in the competitive range. In this case, the Army determined that ADES’ proposal was unacceptable and was not placed in the competitive range. As such, the Army did not violate this third provision of the RSA’s implementing regulations, that is, the requirement to consult with the Secretary of Education, as ADES’ proposal was not judged to be within the CR and ranked high enough to have a reasonable chanced of award.
ADES assertion that a 1988 Handbook entitled Administration of the Randolph-Sheppard Vending Program by Federal Property Managing Agencies stands for the proposition that the Army should have permitted ADES to make its proposal “acceptable through meaningful discussions” is erroneous. First, the 1988 Handbook is not an implementing regulation of the RSA.  Second, any reliance upon the 1988 Handbook for the assertion that the term “competitive range” as discussed in the 1988 Handbook applies in this case, is clear error. The  Court of Federal Claims determined in 2002 that the definition of “competitive range” as expressed at FAR 15.306 applies to a RSA competitive range determination.  See N.C. Div. of Servs. For the Blind v. U.S., 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 167 (2002),  Affirmed by N.C. Div. of Servs. for the Blind v. United States, 50 Fed. Appx. 985, 2003 U.S. App. LEXIS 7276 (2003) (hereafter “North Carolina” case). Finally, Section 107(b) provides authority for the Secretary of Education to prescribe regulations designed to assure that the RSA priority is given to licensed blind persons and where feasible, one or more vending machines are established on federal property. The prescribed regulations which implement the RSA were enacted at Title 34, Sections 395.1-395.38 after publication in the Federal Register on March 23, 1977. The petitioner proffered no evidence that the 1988 Handbook was published in the Federal Register, was subjected to public comments, or finalized as required by the federal rules making procedures outlined in the Administrative Procedure Act and 41 U.S.C. § 1707.
This panel member concurs with petitioner’s assertion in the post-hearing brief that the success of petitioner’s case ultimately hinges upon the resolution of Issue 2: what is the proper standard for determining whether a proposal from a State Licensing Agency should be placed in the “competitive range”? The Army correctly asserts that the Solicitation criteria govern the process and procedures of the procurement evaluation. “Agencies are required to evaluate proposals and make contract awards based on the criteria set forth in the solicitation.” Texas v. United States (hereafter “Texas” case), 134 Fed. Cl. 8, 23 (quoting NEQ, LLC v. United States, 88 Fed. Cl. 38, 47-48 (2009) (“It is hornbook law that agencies must evaluate proposal and make awards based on the criteria stated in the solicitation.”). In this instance, the Army’s Solicitation properly notified offerors that no discussions would be conducted for initial submissions, which is authorized by FAR Clause 52.212-1(g).  ADES, an experienced government contractor, knew when they submitted their proposal that NO discussions would be held or permitted and as such, each offeror should submit their best and final offers. The RSA does not require discussions after an initial evaluation of submitted proposals and FAR Clause 52.212-1(g) authorizes the agency to hold no discussions after initial evaluations. Five offerors proposals were excluded from the competitive range based upon an initial evaluation of their proposal. These five offerors had no entitlement to discuss, revise, amend, or explain their respective rejected proposal. Simply put, once your final examination is submitted to the teacher, your submitted answers determine your grade. No revisions, discussions, or explanations are permitted.
The Court of Federal Claims (COFC) pointed out more than twenty years ago, “[t]here is no precedent to support [ADES’] argument that the [RSA] regulations require the application of a competitive range definition that is different from that typically used in federal procurement law today.” North Carolina at 167.  As the evaluation of ADES’ incomplete and incoherent proposal was in accord with the terms of the Solicitation, the FAR, and the RSA, the decision to exclude ADES’ proposal did not violate the RSA. The “competitive range” definition that applies to this Solicitation is not the meaning of “competitive range” when the RSA regulations were adopted in 1977 or based upon a rescinded 1988 Handbook. Rather, it is clear federal law based upon the North Carolina case that the competitive range definition is the current definition proscribed by the Federal Acquisition Regulation Section15.306 which applies to all federal procurements, including RSA procurement for food service contracts. Since 2002, all federal procurements, to include ALL RSA procurements, apply the current competitive range definition prescribed in FAR 15.306.  A separate definition of ‘competitive range” based upon the 1977 definition or 1988 Handbook is not permitted in this procurement, or any other food serviced procurement. The FAR definition of “competitive range” applies to all federal procurements and to all federal agencies. All nine proposals submitted to the Army, including ADES, were all subject to the current definition of competitive range. The SSEB evaluation team was prohibited by law from using a separate definition of competitive range for ADES’ proposal. The Panel Majority’s reliance upon the 1977 definition of CR and the 1988 Handbook are clear error.
This settled legal doctrine that the FAR definition of competitive range applies in RSA procurements should be put to rest by the Department of Education once and for all. The Department of Education should include this admonition in future convening letters citing to N.C. Div. of Servs. For the Blind v. U.S., 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 167 (2002). The DOE convening letter currently advises that the federal entity is responsible for enacting remedial measures if a RSA violation has occurred. See Maryland State Dep't of Educ. v. Nash, 915 F.2d 1482, 1492 (11th Cir. 1990).The CR admonition should be added as well to all DOE convening letters.
Additionally, the North Carolina case was a post-award bid protest filed in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims in which plaintiffs (the North Carolina SLA) sought a declaratory judgment and permanent injunctive relief. The parties filed cross motions for summary judgment and the U.S. Court of Federal Claims applied the standard of review applicable to bid protests: an agency's procurement decision will be upheld unless shown to be "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law."  5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A); 28 U.S.C. § 1491(b)(4), see North Carolina at 159. The North Carolina court, contrary to the Panel Majority’s assertion, applied the proper standard of an APA review for a post-award bid protest. It is binding precedent on this panel and the Department of Education. Likewise, the standard of review for this arbitration panel is specified in subchapter II of chapter 5 of title 5, U.S. Code which is an APA review as well, not de novo. See 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2.

THE RSA PRIORITY DOES NOT GRANT ADES A RIGHT TO THE CONTRACT.
The Army issued Solicitation W9124J-20-R-0027 and invited the SLA and small business vendors to submit proposals for the potential award of a five-year contract to operate Fort Huachuca’s three dining facilities. Unlike in year’s past, Mr. Weber hired a French company, Sodexho, to prepare his proposal. The Solicitation specified that the Army would not conduct discussions as permitted by the FAR and DFARs. Contrary to petitioner’s statement that overwhelming legal authority requires the Army to place ADES’s proposal in the CR, neither the RSA statute nor its implementing regulations or any federal statutes or regulations expressly require the Army to do so. The RSA and its implementing regulations do not address the conduct of discussions during the evaluation process of proposals or specify that ADES’ proposal should be placed in the CR.
Further, Petitioner’s Conclusion of Law #14 in their post-hearing brief that “the Randolph-Sheppard Act and its regulations require that SLAs and their blind vendors be treated differently than other offerors in several respects, at all stages of the procurement process” does not apply to the evaluation stage of their proposal. (emphasis added). As required by the CICA, FAR, DFAR, all submitted proposals are evaluated in accordance with the same solicitation criteria. With respect to the SBA preference, all offerors prices are increased by 10% except for the small business. See FAR 19.1307(b).The RSA does not provide such an exception for the SLA’s proposal during the evaluation process.  The RSA implementing regulation, as noted infra, requires the agency to perform two mandatory actions under the RSA – invite the SLA to submit a proposal and establish a Solicitation with evaluation criteria. See 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). A third mandatory action, consultation between the agency and Secretary of Education, is only triggered if the SLA’s proposal is placed in the CR and has a reasonable chance of award. See Respondent’s Post Hearing Brief, Tab 41; 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a).
The Army Solicitation advised offerors to submit their best and final proposal as there would not be any discussions. Yet, ADEs now complains the errors in their proposal could have easily been rectified if there had been discussions. The other four offerors whose proposals were also excluded from the CR likewise could have remedied their proposals. However, the Army issued the Solicitation which contained this notice of no discussions for all offerors and was by law required to adhere to this criterion. The RSA does not grant the SLA immunity or exception from the specified Solicitation criteria. Neither the SLA nor any other offeror challenged the Solicitation criteria prior to submission of its proposal, so any post-evaluation challenge to the Solicitation criteria is waived.
Petitioner mistakenly believes that 20 USC § 107 “grants” a priority for federal government contracts and that some specific priority language has been prescribed for federal contract solicitations. Neither is true. Section 107 of Title 20 merely directs the Secretary of Education, in consultation with the GSA and other federal agencies that manage federal property, to publish regulations that will explain how priority may be granted in future situations on a one-by-one basis. There is no automatic priority of award or placement in the CR granted by the RSA or any of its implementing regulations.
As stated in the RSA, in awarding contracts for vending facilities, priority is given to “blind persons licensed by a State agency.” 20 U.S.C. § 107(b). The RSA implementing regulations state that “[i]n order to establish the ability of blind vendors to operate a cafeteria,” SLAs are “shall be invited to respond to solicitations for offers when a cafeteria contract is contemplated by the appropriate property managing department, agency, or instrumentality.” 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). The implementing regulations further state that “[p]riority in the operation of cafeterias by blind vendors on Federal property shall be afforded when the Secretary [of Education] determines . . . that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided by employees, whether by contract or otherwise.” 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) (emphasis added). The word “priority,” however, is not defined in the RSA or in the implementing regulations. See 20 U.S.C. § 107(e) (Definitions) and 34 C.F.R. 395.1 (Terms).
Although the term “priority” is not defined in either the RSA or its implementing regulations, Section 395.33(a) and (b) describe the operational process as to how the “priority” works. In the case of Texas et al. v. United States,134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017), Judge Horn’s opinion contains a detailed description as to how the RSA “priority” is applied for a food service contract. In response to an agency protest filed by the SLA, the Texas Workforce Commission, the Air Force took corrective action and created a new competitive range that included six offerors, including the Texas SLA for a full food service contract at an Air Force base. The Air Force notified the six offerors in the competitive range that a second technical evaluation would be conducted. The six offerors responded to the second technical evaluation and received evaluation notices, including the Texas SLA. The six offerors responded to the evaluation notices, resulting in the Air Force narrowing the competitive range to just four offerors, including the Texas SLA. The four offerors were prepared to submit Final Proposal Revisions (as is outlined in the Solicitation in this case) when the State of Texas filed a pre-award bid protest in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims.
The State of Texas argued that once it was entered into the competitive range, it was entitled to automatically received the contract award under the RSA and that the Air Force should not be allowed to conduct negotiations with other offerors in the competitive range. Protestors allege that “by failing to conduct negotiations/discussions solely with Texas and failing to make award to Texas, the Air Force continues to ignore relevant procurement statutes and regulations.” Texas at 18.The Texas SLA sought injunctive relief to “enjoin the government from implementing its revised corrective action and enjoin the government from conducting discussions with other offerors and soliciting/receiving final proposal revisions from those same offerors.” Id. As Judge Horn notes, “[s]ignificant to this bid protest, the regulations explain when a blind vendor will receive priority:
Priority in the operation of cafeterias by blind vendors on Federal property shall be afforded when the Secretary determines, on an individual basis, and after consultation with the appropriate property managing department, agency, or instrumentality, thatsuch operation can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees. . . .
Id. at 19; 34 C.F.R. §395.33(a); see also Commonwealth of Ky., Educ. Cabinet, Dep't for the Blind v. United States, 62 Fed. Cl. at 455 (emphasis added). The Court further notes that an SLA must clear two hurdles before receiving a priority contract award under the RSA. 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). “First, the managing agency must find that the SLA falls within ‘a competitive range’ and possesses ‘a reasonable chance’ of receiving the contract,” and “[s]econd, after the managing agency makes the aforementioned determinations, the agency must consult with the Secretary of Education to determine whether the SLA’s operations ‘can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality.” 34 C.F.R.§ 395.33(a). Id.  Judge Horn noted that “the issue for the court is one of timing, specifically, at what point is the priority contemplated in the Randolph Sheppard Act SA to be afforded to an SLA.” Id. at 19. (emphasis added). In reviewing numerous GAO cases relating to RSA cases, Judge Horn was not persuaded. . . that “an SLA is entitled to receive an automatic priority award immediately upon being included in the competitive range, without further evaluation by the agency of the SLA proposal submitted to the agency and the consultation with the US Department of Education required by the Randolph-Sheppard Act implementing regulations”. Id. at 21. As noted in Commonwealth of Kentucky , Education Cabinet, Department for the Blind v. United States, the Court noted that even if the SLA’s proposal does fall within the competitive range, the Secretary could still deny the SLA the priority. Commonwealth of Ky. Educ. Cabinet, Dep’t for the Blind v. United States, 62 Fed. Cl. at 455. As Judge Horn notes in the Texas case, the RSA and its implementing regulations “set forth a multi-step process, including a cost evaluation and a food quality evaluation that an agency must complete before the case is referred to the United States Secretary of Education” as part of the consultation process. Id. at 22; see 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) . The placement of an SLA’s proposal in the competitive range is “not equivalent to a finding by the agency that the SLA’s proposal is among the proposals that have a reasonable chance of award.” Id. at 22. The Secretary of Education has a specific role in whether a priority should be afforded to an SLA as outlined in 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.33(a) and (b). In this case, ADES did not pass the first step of being placed in the competitive range. Despite petitioner’s assertion of an automatic award to ADES and that the Army should place ADES in the competitive range, neither the RSA nor its implementing regulations require such action. Instead, the multi-step process prescribed by the Texas opinion must be followed.
Priority is applied, if at all, only after the federal agency that contemplates a cafeteria contract receives competitive proposals from multiple sources, and conducts an initial evaluation of the proposals in accordance with the Solicitation provisions. If the SLA’s (ADES) proposal is deemed to be in the CR, the Army would hold discussions with them and the other offerors in the CR and evaluated their revised proposals. If a proposal is not selected for the CR and the Solicitation directs that no discussions would be held, those offerors are excluded from the competition. In this case, ADES’s proposal was not selected for the CR and hence, was excluded from the competition. If ADES’s proposal had been placed in the CR with other offerors, those offerors would undergo discussion as prescribed by Solicitation W924J-20-R-0027, and given the opportunity to revise their proposals. Those revised proposals, including ADES, that have a reasonable chance of being selected for final award, would have been forwarded to the Secretary of Education for consultation in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a). +
IV. CONCLUSION
The evidence presented in the arbitration proceeding clearly demonstrates that the Army did not violate the RSA or its implementing in the evaluation of ADES proposal and exclusion of ADES from the competitive range. Under the seminal case describing the application of the RSA “priority” in a RSA procurement, Texas v. U.S., 134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017), the priority was not applicable as ADES’s proposal was excluded from the CR after initial evaluations by the Army. As noted above, the RSA and its implementing regulation only required the Army to perform two mandated tasks: (1) invite the SLA to submit a proposal and (2) issue a Solicitation with evaluation criteria. The Army complied with both these requirements. Petitioner challenges the Army’s evaluation of ADES’s proposal which was evaluated using the guidance under CICA, the FAR, and the DFAR. This arbitration panel is not authorized to adjudicate whether the Army complied with federal procurement regulations and statutes – only the U.S. Court of Federal Claims or the General Accounting Office has such authority.
Petitioner’s assertion that the “competitive range” determination that should have been used by the Army in this procurement should have been based upon the definition in effect in 1977 (the “ASPR”) and a rescinded 1988 DOE Handbook is erroneous. All federal procurements, including RSA procurements, are governed by the FAR and specifically, the “competitive range” guidance contained in FAR Section 15.306. See North Carolina Division of Services for the Blind v. United States, 53 Fed. Cl. 14 (2002).
The Army fully investigated allegations of an organizational conflict of interest that purportedly tainted the procurement process and debunked the allegation. Contrary to petitioner’s assertion, there was no OCI involving either Mr. Brown or Mr. Grajeda.
The issuance of Adverse Inferences against the Army is ultra vires as the DOE Revised Interim Policy (1978) does not expressly authorize the issuance of Adverse Inferences. If the DOE continues to allow SLAs to circumvent the COFC and GAO and file de facto bid protests in DOE arbitration panels, the DOE should promulgate regulations to provide appropriate guidance for future panels relating to protective orders, discovery, and issuance of adverse inferences.

The Federal Court of Claims and the Government Accountability Office Only Hear Cases Brought by State Licensing Agencies After Arbitration Panel Decisions
At page 2, the Dissent asserts that this case should have been filed with the United States Court of Federal Claims (COFC) or the Government Accountability Office (GAO). At page 3, the Dissent states, illogically, that the Department of Education (DoE) should not convene arbitrations regarding matters that could be adjudicated in the COFC or GAO. Yet, the COVC and the GAO would dismiss a claim regarding the R-S Act filed by a State Licensing Agency (SLA) unless brought after an arbitration panel decision. See Kentucky v. United States, 62 Fed. Cl. 445, 460 (2004), aff’d sum nom Kentucky Educ.
Cabinet Dep’t for the Blind v. United States, 424 F.3d 1222 (Fed. Cir. 2005):
this court concludes that the statutory scheme of the RSA requires exhaustion of
administrative procedures before an aggrieved SLA may raise an RSA claim in this court.
As a matter of routine, the GAO dismisses bid protests brought by SLAs. GAO B-400912.2, GAO B- 400583; B400583.2. The Court of Claims has, since 2004, rejected bid protests filed by SLAs. See, Colo. Dep’t of Human Serv. v. U.S., 74 Fed. Cl. 339 (2006), (COFC lacks jurisdiction under the Tucker Act and the Administrative Procedure Act until after an arbitration is concluded); State of Kansas v. U.S., 192 F. Supp.3d 1184, 1194-95 (D. Kan. 2016), aff’d in part sub. nom. Kansas by & through Kansas Dep’t for Children & Families v. Source America, 874.F.3d 1226 (10th Cir. 2017) (Dispute alleging an R-S Act violation must be arbitrated as COFC lacks jurisdiction until arbitration panel decides if an R-S Act violation occurred).
The Dissent’s position ignores well established precedent.
Discovery, Protective Orders, and Adverse Inferences are Authorized in an R-S Act Arbitration When Necessary to Ensure Meaningful Evaluation of Petitioner’s Claims
At pages 2, 10-12, the Dissent asserts that the Panel cannot order protective orders, discovery, or adverse inferences. That is not the case.
The 1978 Revised Interim Policies and Procedures require the Panel Chair to apply rules or principles designed to assure production of the most credible evidence available, to assure an orderly, expeditious, and fair arbitration, by receiving, ruling on, excluding, or limiting evidence, to rule on motions and other procedural items, and to regulate the course of the arbitration and the conduct of counsel. Thus, the rulings on protective orders, discovery, and adverse inferences must be entered by the Panel to ensure an orderly and fair proceeding. Otherwise, the Army would be able to profit from withholding key documents and there could be no fair arbitration.
The Dissent wrongfully claims that it withheld discovery “under the authority of the federal
procurement statutes.” The Panel disagreed, as once it issued its Protective Order, the Trade Secrets Act permits disclosure (18 U.S.C. § 1905, 18 U.S.C. § 1333), as does the Procurement Integrity Act (41 U.S.C. § 2102(a)(1), 41 U.S.C. § 2107(1).
This Panel’s decision is a final agency action pursuant to the Administrative Procedure Act. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a). This Panel conducts a de novo review of the contracting officer’s decision. De novo review is required due to the terms “petition” and “determine” rather than “appeal” in 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a). Given that it is Secretary of DoE who is required to “assure” compliance with the R-S Act’s purposes and objectives, this Panel accordingly conducts de novo review when reviewing an agency’s determinations under the R-S Act. The Panel is thus required to have access to all of the information that the Army had when it eliminated ADES’s bid from the competitive range.
Whether or Not an SLA’s Bid is in the Competitive Range, Consultation with the Secretary of Education is Required
At page 26, the Dissent claims that ADES “mistakenly believes that 20 USC § 107 ‘grants’ a priority for federal government contracts[.]”. The Dissent, at page 27, cites to State of Texas v. United States ,134 Fed. Cl. 8 (2017), for an explanation of the R-S Act’s priority. There Texas SLA filed a pre-bid protest following an R-S Act arbitration. Defendant United States sought and was granted dismissal. The Dissent, at page 28, relying on this case, states “Texas argued that once it was entered into the competitive range, it was entitled to automatically received (sic) the contract award[.]” The Dissent neglects to quote the dispositive language:
If the SLA's proposal satisfies the requirements to preempt the contract award to the best value offeror, then the Air Force would be required to consult with the United States
Secretary of Education. If, however, the SLA does not satisfy this criteria, then the contract will be awarded to the best value offeror, subject to a determination of contractor responsibility and consultation with the United States Department of Education.
Texas v. United States, 134 Fed. Cl. 25 (2017) (emphasis added). Thus, the case makes it abundantly clear that, whether or not the SLA is awarded the contract, the United States Department of Education makes the final decision as to whether “such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided to employees[.]” 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e), 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a). While the Court was not persuaded that an SLA is entitled to receive an automatic priority upon being included in the competitive range, the fact is that the Court made it abundantly clear, that, whether or not an SLA’s bid is placed in the competitive range, that the Secretary of Education makes the final determination of whether the contract should be awarded to the SLA.
In a case where the Georgia SLA sought a preliminary injunction to maintain the status quo (to continue to operate the dining facility at issue), the Court stated that: “it is clear that it is the
arbitration panel’s role to determine whether the R-S Act was violated.” Georgia v. United States, 398 F. Supp. 3d 1330, 1355. (S. D. Ga. 2019). There, the Court described the Secretary of Education’s role:
If Plaintiffs' position is correct that “competitive range” and “reasonable chance of being selected for final award” mean the same thing and that they are entitled to an award once they're found to be within that range, then Defendants likely violated the RSA since they were deemed to be within the competitive range. Alternatively, even under Defendants' interpretation of those terms as being two separate requirements, Plaintiffs are still likely to succeed on the merits because their proposal was likely reasonable, which means that, at minimum, Defendants had to consult the Secretary of Education before removing Plaintiffs' bid from consideration.
Id. at 1356.
Solicitation Evaluation Criteria in Conflict with R-S Act Cannot be Applied to ADES’s Bid
At page 5, the Dissent asserts that that the Army was not required to conduct discussions with ADES, because the terms of the Solicitation (authorized by the FAR) advised there would be no discussions. That is not the case. The Dissent ignores the fact that the Competition in Contracting Act and the Federal Acquisitions Regulations apply “except in case of other procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute.” 10 U.S.C. § 3201(a), The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst v. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001), NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003), NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the CICA and FAR do not apply to R-S Act procurements, insofar as they conflict with the regulations implementing the R-S Act. See the Majority Decision at Conclusions of Law IV.A.4, 5, 6, and 7. As explained in Georgia v. United States, 398 F. Supp. 1330 (S.D. Ga. 2019):
while the FAR may provide regulations for the procurement of government contracts
generally, Congress passed the RSA to change the normal government contracting procedures for the benefit of the blind, and as such, the RSA might require a different result than the general FAR regulations would otherwise with certain government contracts.
Id. at 1351, n. 7.
The R-S Act Does Not Provide the Army With Unfettered Discretion to Exclude an SLA’s Proposal from the Competitive Range
Starting at page 12, the Dissent asserts that the Army’s decision to exclude ADES’s proposal was proper, claiming that the R-S Act only requires that the Army invite the SLA to respond to the solicitation and to issue a solicitation with evaluation criteria. If that were the case, it would leave all federal agencies with sole discretion to evaluate an SLA’s bid. Congress amended the R-S Act to provide for the Secretary of Education to have “overall authority” given the “record of abuses and neglect” of the R-S Act by “officials of various Federal agencies.” S. Rep. No. 93-937 (1974) at 16. In particular, Congress determined that “[c]ommanders of military installations are singularly insensitive to the need to develop the program.” Id. at 10. 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(d), which authorizes direct negotiations for the award of cafeteria contracts, requires that when such negotiations do not result in a contract, that the provisions of both 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) and (b) apply. Thus, it is self-evident that an SLA’s bid cannot be rejected by a federal agency without first consulting with the Secretary of Education, who makes the final decision. The Army is simply incorrect when it states that the Secretary need only be consulted when the Army, in its sole discretion, determines that a bid is not within the competitive range.
The R-S Act cannot be so interpreted, for to do so would nullify the statute. The Army (or any federal agency), in its sole discretion, would be able to always determine that an SLA’s bid is not in the competitive range, and the decision would be essentially unreviewable. This is a “stark violation of the elementary principle of statutory interpretation” of statutory interpretation that requires “every clause and word of a statute” be given effect. King v. Burwell, 135 S. Ct. 2480, 2498 (2015).
The Definition of. Competitive Range in Place When the 1977 Regulations were Enacted is the Correct Measure of Competitive Range to Evaluate ADES’s Bid
The Army relies on N.C. Div. of Servs. for the Blind v. U.S., 53 Fed. Cl. 147, 167 (2002), for the
proposition that there is no support for the argument that requires the application of a definition of competitive range different from that used in federal procurement today. The 2002 North Carolina case is inapplicable, because it incorrectly reviewed the contracting officer’s decision under a highly deferential standard instead of the correct standard, de novo.
The correct standard, as set out in Conclusion of Law IV.A.5. of the majority decision, is the standard that was in place at the time the R-S Act regulations were promulgated: any proposal “must be considered to be within a competitive range so as to require negotiations unless it is so technically inferior or out of line with regard to price that meaningful discussions are precluded.”
ADES’s Bid Contained Easily Curable Minor Discrepancies and Omissions
The Dissent, claims, at pages 5, 9, 15, and 18, that ADES’s bid was fraught with errors, or incomplete, or incoherent. The factual record shows the contrary. See Findings of Fact, Section III.B. 1 through 5 of Panel Majority Decision. The uncontroverted testimony, along with the exhibits, establish that the discrepancies in ADES’s bid were minor and curable, if the Army had followed the R-S Act’s requirement and held meaningful discussions with ADES.
