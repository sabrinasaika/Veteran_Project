SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (Section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to 
Section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services.
 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by New York Adult Career 
and Continuing Education Services – Vocational Rehabilitation (ACCES-VR). The nature, 
scope, and focus of this review and the process by which RSA carried out its activities from 
August 13 through 15, 2024, was defined by information regarding the VR agency’s use and 
expenditure of Federal funds, documents, and data submitted by ACCES-VR, taking into 
account the goals, unique circumstances, and technical assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
 Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities;   
 Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

4 
achieve continuous improvement in assisting individuals with disabilities to achieve 
quality employment outcomes; 
 Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities, 
including students and youth with disabilities; 
 Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported 
Employment programs and recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the 
VR agency’s financial management of fiscal resources and strategies for maximizing the 
use of Federal grant funds;  
 Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and 
procedures, including the development or revision of policies and internal controls 
supporting effective and efficient program operations, service delivery, and data-based 
decision making; and  
 Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic 
action plans to improve and maximize program and fiscal performance and the use of 
Federal funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops a 
strategic action plan (SAP) to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of 
Federal funds to achieve quality employment outcomes.  
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE  
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by ACCES-VR on the Case Service Report 
(RSA-911) and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PY) 
2020, 2021, and 2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and 
requested documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR 
services, quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as 
discussions with ACCES-VR of potential factors influencing program performance informed the 
analysis and helped to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the 
performance and management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from 
related sources, and discussions with ACCES-VR during the review, RSA identified various 
factors, which may be contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s 
performance leading to employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and 
expenditure of Federal funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in 
response to ACCES-VR’s requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by 
the VR agency as it plans for continuous improvement in program performance and to maximize 
the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with disabilities in 
achieving quality employment outcomes. 
 
Participant, Staff, and Vendor Attrition 
 
The attrition of applicants, eligible individuals and participants prior to or after receiving services 
plays a key role in evaluating the performance of the VR agency. Premature or untimely exit of 
individuals with disabilities during the VR process can, among other things, impact ACCES-

6 
VR’s ability to expend funds to fully support participants with the necessary services to achieve 
their employment goals. When attrition is high, VR agencies should examine the reasons for exit 
and make the necessary adjustments to their operations, policies, and practices to sustain 
engagement and remove unnecessary barriers or contributing factors. ACCES-VR experienced a 
high rate of attrition during the period under review. The percentage of individuals who exited 
after applying for VR services and prior to the agency determining their eligibility averaged 
20.89 percent over the three-year review period. The percentage of individuals who exited after 
the determination of their eligibility but prior to the development of a signed individualized plan 
for employment (IPE) increased from 9.8 percent in PY 2020 to 12.8 percent in PY 2022. The 
percentage of participants who exited after receiving services listed on an IPE without an 
employment outcome averaged 50.1 percent over the review period.  
 
ACCES-VR made an average of 95.5 percent of eligibility determinations and developed and 
average of 93.2 percent of IPEs during the three-year review period within federally required 
time standards. In addition, the number of applicants and individuals determined eligible 
increased from PYs 2020 to PY 2022. However, the number of individuals who received services 
listed on an IPE decreased by 3,269 participants and the number of individuals who did not 
receive services after developing an IPE increased from nine individuals in PY 2020 to 1,063 in 
PY 2022. The reasons most reported for exit during the period of the review included “no longer 
interested in receiving services” (ranging from 35.7 percent in PY 2020 to 36.1 percent in PY 
2022) or “unable to locate” (ranging from 30.6 percent in PY 2020 to 33.5 percent in PY 2022). 
The attrition rates, decline in service provision, and reasons for exit may indicate issues related 
to sustained engagement with eligible individuals and participants or VR services that are not 
sufficiently meeting the needs of participants. 
ACCES-VR has addressed staff attrition through the hiring and training of staff since the start of 
the COVID-19 pandemic, which affected the agency’s ability to sustain participant engagement, 
as well as coordinate and provide timely services. To address staff vacancies, ACCES-VR hired 
110 vocational rehabilitation counselors over the 18 months prior to the on-site review. 
However, the VR agency reported a 23.1 percent VR counselor staff vacancy rate (73 of its 315 
counselor positions remain vacant) across 15 districts and 24 physical offices. At the time of the 
review, ACCES-VR shared that counselors were managing caseloads ranging from 130 to 200 
VR participants and additional staff resources were still needed to quickly engage and support 
individuals. ACCES-VR would like to develop State personnel positions/titles that are dedicated 
to sustained participant engagement in the VR process beyond the traditional civil service 
positions for clerical support, and to provide direct pre-employment transition services to 
students with disabilities. At this time, the VR agency staff capacity is limited by established 
civil service job classifications. ACCES-VR reported communicating its program needs to the 
Office of Human Resources Management within the New York State Education Department 
(NYSED) and the New York State Department of Civil Service. ACCES-VR was successful in 
establishing counselor training positions and is hopeful that it will also be able to establish 
positions to improve and sustain participant engagement and increase the availability and 
provision of pre-employment transition services for students with disabilities. 
The majority of VR and supported employment services are purchased through core 
rehabilitation services (CRS) contracts with community rehabilitation providers (CRPs). During 
the COVID-19 pandemic, ACCES-VR and its VR participants experienced delays in the

7 
coordination and delivery of VR services due to vendor capacity (e.g., service provider mergers 
and staff attrition, decreased service options, and service waitlists), procurement limitations (e.g., 
contracts are required when total services purchased from an entity exceed $50,000), and 
contracting issues (e.g., service rates must be approved by NYSED and the Office of the New 
York State Comptroller). In response to these challenges, ACCES-VR worked with vendors to 
offer remote and virtual core rehabilitation services to meet VR participant requests for such 
services. In addition, ACCES-VR terminated its FFYs 2020 through 2025 “project Pre-ETS” 
contracts in December 2023, and included pre-employment transition services for potentially 
eligible and VR-eligible students in its FFYs 2024 through 2028 CRS contracts in an effort to 
expand the availability of pre-employment transition services providers from an average of two 
vendors in each district to over 180 vendors statewide. The approval process for the new CRS 
contracts delayed service provision. The VR agency also experienced a decline in the number of 
vendors, including psychologists, willing to accept its service rates. ACCES-VR was 
unsuccessful in attempts to increase and align its contract rates with the New York State Office 
for People with Developmental Disabilities (OPWDD) as VR agency requests were denied by 
NYSED staff and the Office of the New York State Comptroller (OSC). In another effort to 
expand its network of effective service providers and services for individuals with disabilities 
across the State, ACCES-VR was able to include travel reimbursement for vendors traveling to 
and providing services in rural and remote areas of the State. ACCES-VR communicated that it 
has been a challenge to expand the number of vendors due to its inability to increase service 
rates. This is further addressed in finding 2.2. 
Technical Assistance

Impediments and Practices Affecting the Accurate Reporting and Provision of Pre-
Employment Transition Services 
 
Section 113(a) of the Rehabilitation Act requires VR agencies to provide or arrange for, in 
coordination with local educational agencies (LEA), the provision of pre-employment transition 
services to a “student with a disability” as defined in Section 7(37). Early engagement and career 
exploration services expose students to the VR program and services available to support their 
career goals. Although the number of reported students fluctuated during the three-year review 
period, the total number of students with disabilities reported and total number of students (both 
eligible and potentially eligible for the VR program) in receipt of pre-employment transition 
services decreased from PYs 2021 to 2022. The number of potentially eligible students in receipt 
of services decreased significantly from 2,081 individuals in PY 2021 to 579 individuals in PY 
2022. Only 13.2 percent of students reported by ACCES-VR received one or more pre-
employment transition services in PY 2022 (Monitoring Table 7). The amount of VR program 
funds ACCESS-VR expended on pre-employment transition services decreased from $8,281,262 
in PY 2021 to $5,914,900 in PY 2022 (RSA-911). ACCES-VR reported a similar decrease on the 
VR Program Financial Reports (RSA-17), which showed $20,347,020 in FFY 2021 and 
$18,232,449 in FFY 2022 for pre-employment transition services expenditures. 
 
ACCES-VR communicated that the decrease in students reported and the small percentage of 
students in receipt of pre-employment transition services was due, in part, to the closure of 
schools and service providers as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. ACCES-VR also 
communicated that it was not accurately capturing and reporting pre-employment transition 
services and expenditures provided by VR program staff and purchased from vendors. 
Specifically, ACCES-VR shared that it was erroneously reporting students from cases closed 
prior to PY 2017 (approximately 40,000) due to staff not verifying whether an individual was 
currently enrolled in an educational program or updating whether or not the individual was still a 
student with a disability. ACCES-VR reported that the new case management system was 
programmed to remove the “student with a disability” designation when an individual no longer 
meets the age range criteria. However, the new case management system was not accurately 
capturing secondary and postsecondary education enrollment data, which impacts ACCES-VR’s 
performance measures such as measurable skill gains and credential attainment rates. It is 
imperative that ACCES-VR have processes to verify and validate whether a student is enrolled in

9 
an educational program and information reported, as both the age range and enrollment criteria 
must be met to accurately report a student with a disability. 
 
The majority of pre-employment transition services reported in PYs 2020 through 2022 were 
provided directly by ACCES-VR staff (e.g., SVRC TAYS (15), VR counselors and VR 
counselor assistants) despite the agency having pre-ETS contracts with service providers. 
ACCES-VR staff began reporting services it provides as “actual services” and “activities 
provided case notes” that include service check boxes (available May 15, 2024) in the new case 
management system, as well as time spent providing or arranging for the provision of pre-
employment transition services through time and effort allocation system (TEAs) codes available 
on their timesheets. The new case management system allows vendors to refer potentially 
eligible students for services, receive authorizations from VR agency staff, upload deliverables 
and reports for VR agency review by ACCES-VR staff, and invoice for services. The Fiscal 
Administrative Services Team (FAST) within ACCES is able to generate payment. ACCES-VR 
staff assured RSA that case management system advancements will improve the accuracy with 
which ACCES-VR reports purchased and agency-provided pre-employment transition services.  
 
ACCES-VR communicated that it is in the process of developing staff job classifications 
necessary to directly provide pre-employment transition services; however, it is experiencing 
challenges and delays due to requirements to hire civil service approved job classifications and 
for service rates to be approved by NYSED and OSC. Specifically, ACCES-VR identified the 
need to expand the provision of pre-employment transition services to more than 13.2 percent of 
the students reported through the creation of civil service job classifications that would directly 
provide pre-employment transition services. ACCES-VR engaged in discussions with NYSED’s 
office of HR management, which communicated with the New York State Department of Civil 
Service and requested that ACCES-VR be able to hire from a civil service title/series also used 
by another program office. The ultimate responsibility and final decision-making authority for 
classifying new positions and hiring rests with NYSED and the New York Department of Civil 
Service. It is important to note that ACCES-VR was successful in creating a VR counselor 
trainee position after two years of negotiation with NYSED’s office of HR management and the 
New York State Department of Civil Service, which led to the hiring of over 110 staff in an 
effort to decrease attrition and improve participant engagement, and the provision of services and 
supports.  
 
ACCES-VR and RSA closed a FFY 2021 non-compliance pre-employment transition services 
corrective action plan (CAP) on February 3, 2025, through which RSA was monitoring the 
progress of ACCES-VR and the New York State Commission for the Blind (NYSCB) toward 
expanding the provision of pre-employment transition services and meeting the reserve and 
expenditure requirement. The State of New York met the reserve and expenditure requirement in 
FFYs 2022 and 2023.

Service Provision and Reporting System Limitations 
 
During PYs 2020 through 2022, the number of applicants and individuals determined eligible for 
VR services increased; however, the number of individuals who received services fluctuated 
with a decrease from 54,151 individuals in PY 2020 to 50,882 individuals in PY 2022. Although 
applicants entered the VR system and were determined eligible, 49.3 percent of individuals who 
exited the VR program did so after their IPE was developed but without employment, and 15.1 
percent exited with employment in PY 2022.  
 
An examination of the training, career, and other services data that ACCES-VR reported for PY 
2022, revealed that 13.9 percent of participants in receipt of training services received 
miscellaneous training, 8.2 percent participated in postsecondary education, 4.8 percent received 
occupational or vocational training, and 1.8 percent engaged in work-based learning experiences. 
These data indicated that no individuals participated in apprenticeship training, on-the-job 
training, basic academic remedial or literacy training, or customized training. In PY 2022, the 
largest percentages of individuals who were provided career services received vocational 
rehabilitation counseling and guidance (88.6 percent) and job search assistance (8.3 percent). 
The largest percentage of individuals in receipt of other services were provided transportation 
(8.3 percent). Given this performance, RSA and ACCES-VR discussed the need to increase 
career and training services and conduct labor market analyses to determine the State’s labor

11 
market demands and identify opportunities to prepare individuals with disabilities for careers 
with the potential for advancement, rather than only entry-level employment.  
 
The VR agency communicated that case management system limitations resulted in the 
inaccurate reporting of agency-provided and “no cost” services. Specifically, the new case 
management system captures only those services included in an IPE associated with an 
authorization, an “actual service” case management system page or “activities provided case 
note.” During the migration to the new case management system, staff were unaware that they 
needed to capture services provided by ACCES-VR staff on an “actual services” case 
management system page, which is similar to an authorization without a voucher,  
in order for services to be reported as provided on the RSA-911. The agency recently added the 
“activities provided case note” functionality in order for staff to more easily capture and report 
customized training, self-employment, and apprenticeships in addition to agency-provided pre-
employment transition services RSA recommended that ACCES-VR ensure all individualized 
VR services, whether purchased by the agency, provided directly by agency staff, or obtained 
through comparable benefits, are included on the IPE  and accurately reported on the RSA-911.  
 
RSA and ACCES-VR also discussed the need to revise policies to address limitations (e.g., 
application timelines and postsecondary education completion deadlines, college funding limits  
(404.00 and 405.00), present and available (202.00), and financial need policy (1125.00) that 
may have impeded the provision and reporting of services and expenditures.  
 
VR agencies may implement strategies to increase participant engagement from application to 
IPE development and service provision. However, if ACCES-VR does not have an 
infrastructure, policies, and procedures in place to support and provide the necessary and 
comprehensive services to assist participants to achieve their career goals, individuals will not 
have an incentive to remain engaged in the VR process, and unsuccessful closures may increase.  
 
Technical Assistance 
 
 RSA clarified that ACCES-VR must be able to accurately report each service received by 
an individual on the RSA-911.  
 RSA and ACCES-VR discussed IPE policy and procedure 206.00 and substantive 
changes to an IPE that require an IPE amendment. Specifically, any changes to an 
employment outcome, VR services to be provided, or the providers of the VR services 
constitute substantive changes that necessitate an IPE amendment. At the time of the 
review, ACCES-VR’s policy defined some services as minor changes (e.g., job coaching, 
community work experiences, supported employment retention services, supported 
employment job retention hrs./wk., youth extended services, job retention/quality wage 
incentive for providers, fees and licenses during employment services) that did not 
require an IPE amendment. RSA clarified that if services are not reflected on the IPE, this 
could be affecting the services captured and reported. 
 RSA and ACCES-VR reviewed RSA-TAC-12-04: Provision of Vocational Rehabilitation 
Services to an Individual by More Than One Agency. RSA clarified that two VR 
agencies (in the same or differing States) can provide VR services to the same individual 
simultaneously so long as the agencies do not duplicate services. RSA also clarified that

12 
ACCES-VR may not impose a duration of residency requirement with respect to the 
eligibility of any individual who is present and available for services in the state (Section 
101(a)(12) of the Rehabilitation Act; 34 CFR 361.42(c)(1)). Furthermore, ACCES-VR 
cannot further define “present and available” or limit services for individuals in the State 
of New York for postsecondary education, or individuals who reside out of State “until 
they are ready to seek placement services in New York and no longer have a VR case in 
another State” (ACCES-VR’s eligibility procedure 202.00). 
RSA clarified that there is no Federal requirement that the financial need of individuals 
be considered in the provision of VR services. If a State unit chooses to consider financial 
need, it must maintain written policies that explain the method for determining the 
financial need of an eligible individual and specific types of VR services for which the 
unit established a financial needs test. The policies must be applied uniformly and must 
ensure that the level of participation in cost of VR services is reasonable, based on 
financial need, including disability-related expenses paid by the individuals, and not so 
high to effectively deny an individual a necessary service (34 C.F.R. § 361.54/RSA TAC 
22-03). 
 
 
Recommendations 
 
 RSA recommended that ACCES-VR review and revise agency policies implementing 
service limitations for consistency with Federal requirements, and ensure that they are 
hyperlinked to ACCES-VR’s policy for exceptions and waivers to the limitations.  
 
RSA recommended agency policies clarify that all individualized VR services (34 C.F.R. § 
361.48(b)) purchased, agency provided or provided through comparable benefits be included on 
an IPE, to ensure the accurate reporting of services received on the RSA-911. 
 
RSA recommends that ACCES-VR discontinue considering the calculation of family 
contributions identified on the Free Application for Federal Student Aid as a source of financial 
contribution or participation for VR services, particularly when through application of the 
agency’s financial needs policy, the participant has been determined to have no financial 
responsibility or participation in the cost of services. The FAFSA is the means to meet the 
requirement to apply for a PELL grant or other Federal grant assistance prior to the VR agency 
expending VR program funds (Section 103(a)(5) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R.  
§ 361.48(b)(6)). 
 
Inaccurate Reporting of Supported Employment Services and Expenditures  
 
During FFYs 2019 through 2021, ACCES-VR received Supported Employment program part A 
and B funds, as well as those Supported Employment part A and B funds transferred by NYSCB; 
however, Supported Employment program expenditures reported BY ACCES-VR on the RSA-
911 decreased from $2,832,342 in PY 2020 to $322,286 in PY 2022. Although the number and 
percentage of supported employment services and supported employment outcomes reported on 
the RSA-911 decreased from PYs 2021 to 2022, ACCES-VR’s expenditure of VR program 
funds for supported employment services increased slightly from $5,912,743 to $5,924,965

13 
during the same period. Per the RSA-911, the number and percentage of extended services 
provided by ACCES-VR to youth with disabilities placed in supported employment increased 
from PYs 2020 to 2022, as did the funds expended on those services ($5,924,965 in PY 2022).  
 
ACCES-VR communicated that its migration to a new case management system in April 2023 
affected the accuracy of supported employment-related data reported on the RSA-911. 
Specifically, system limitations restricted the capturing and reporting of supported employment 
services for cases with a “SE checkbox on the IPE.” As such, cases migrated from the legacy 
system to the new case management system were not reflected or captured as supported 
employment cases. ACCES-VR ran error dashboards” to identify needed corrections, including 
the identification of supported employment plans and completion of the supported employment 
checkbox. Despite ACCES-VR’s efforts, internal controls need to be strengthened to ensure data 
is accurately validated and reported, including supported employment services and expenditures 
using VR and Supported Employment program funds. 
 
ACCES-VR receives a $15,160,000 State appropriation to provide supported employment 
services, including extended services to adults with most significant disabilities. In April 2023, 
ACCES-VR created a new case type – Adult Supported Employment (ASE) to track and report 
these funds (Supported Employment Procedure 1310).  
 
Technical Assistance 
 
 RSA clarified that any staff time spent on adult extended services cannot be charged to the 
VR or Supported Employment grant awards. All costs related to serving adults in extended 
services (e.g., staff, case management system, other costs) must be cost allocated to the 
State appropriation for extended services to adults with most significant disabilities.

 RSA recommended that ACCES-VR develop internal controls to ensure VR and 
supported employment services and expenditures using VR and Supported Employment 
program funds are accurately reported. 
 RSA recommended that the New York State statute 247.14 be revised to remove

Finding 2.1 Insufficient Verification and Compliance with Reporting Requirements  
Issue: Is ACCES-VR verifying and submitting accurate reports in compliance with the 
requirements at 34 C.F.R. 361.40(a) and exercising its responsibility and maintaining effective 
internal controls over the Federal award to provide a reasonable assurance that ACCES-VR is 
managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and 
conditions of the award in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.303.  
Requirement: Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, VR agencies are required to develop an internal 
controls process to provide reasonable assurances regarding the effectiveness and efficiency of 
operations, reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and to be implemented as a 
measure of checks and balances to ensure proper expenditures of funds, including the evaluation 
and monitoring of compliance with statutes, regulations and the terms and conditions of Federal 
awards. Furthermore, a State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified 
or Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program.  
In accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.40(a), VR agencies are required to submit reports, including 
reports required under Sections 13, 14, and 101(a)(10) of the Rehabilitation Act in a manner that 
provides a complete count of the applicants and eligible individuals receiving services, including 
students with disabilities receiving pre-employment transition services, and complies with any 
requirements necessary to ensure the accuracy and verification of those reports. The RSA-911 is 
used to describe the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs in the Annual 
Report to the Congress and the President, as required by Sections 13 and 101(a)(10) of the 
Rehabilitation Act.  
Analysis: During the on-site review, ACCES-VR shared that it does not maintain a 
comprehensive system of internal controls to ensure verification and accuracy of reported data 
and services.  
ACCES-VR reported that district offices conduct their own case reviews; however, the agency 
did not provide RSA with a uniform process to conduct or aggregate data from case reviews to 
inform policies, identify training needs, ensure data accuracy, and improve and optimize 
program performance.  
ACCES-VR communicated that it uses the edit checks built into its case management system, as 
well as RSA’s edit checker, to review errors in reports, prior to submitting reports to RSA. The 
agency has a dedicated staff member to review the case management system for anomalies in the 
data. Data elements are obtained from the ACCES-VR case management system and edited for

18 
correctness and upon submission, any errors are immediately identified and flagged. Although, at 
the time of the period of review, ACCES-VR mentioned a process to submit the RSA-911, 
ACCES-VR had not implemented formal written validation procedures as a part of its internal 
controls to safeguard data integrity and promote the timely resolution of data anomalies and 
inaccuracies, or review of supporting documentation to ensure accurate reporting of data 
reported through the RSA-911.  
Section 116 of WIOA and the implementing joint regulations in 20 C.F.R. part 677 and 34 
C.F.R. parts 361 and 463, RSA-TAC-17-01 (Performance Accountability Guidance for 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) Title I, Title II, Title III, and Title IV Core 
Programs (issued June 11, 2024)), clarify the reporting requirements for MSG, credential 
attainment, and post-exit measures. During the period under review, ACCES-VR reported 
inaccurate data for MSG and credential attainment.  
As discussed under factors impeding performance, RSA pulled data for 300 cases and identified 
ACCES-VR may not be fully capturing MSGs, credential attainment, and post-exit data. Of the 
300 cases, a number of cases were inaccurately coded and reported. For example, ACCES-VR 
reported the provision of training/educational services for individuals who were not enrolled in 
education or postsecondary education services (as reported in the case file). In addition, some 
individuals were reported as receiving educational services did so without substantiating 
documentation in the case file to verify training or educational services were received. 
Additionally, within the 300 cases reviewed, RSA identified cases where ACCES-VR reported 
DE 85: Date Enrolled During Program Participation in an Education or Training Program 
Leading to a Recognized Credential or Employment, but did not report DE 84: Enrolled in 
Postsecondary Education or Career or Technical Training Leading to Recognized Postsecondary 
Credential. In addition, cases, for which postsecondary services were coded, did not include the 
reporting of an enrollment date.  
Also, as previously discussed, ACCES-VR’s lack of data validation procedures resulted in the 
failure to fully capture services that were provided and then inaccurately reported on the RSA-
911. No other written internal controls were implemented during the period of review to ensure 
adherence to implementation procedures or ensure that all information aligned with and was 
consistent across the case service record file, ACCES-VR’s case management system, and the 
RSA-911. Additionally, while the VR agency provided RSA with a case review instrument that 
includes supporting documentation for MSGs and credential attainment, the frequency of these 
reviews did not satisfy a uniform and regular review of those MSGs, credential attainment and 
post-exit measures.   
Conclusion: RSA determined that ACCES-VR is not maintaining effective internal controls 
over the Federal award that would provide a reasonable assurance that ACCES-VR is managing 
the Federal award in compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions 
of the award in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.303. RSA determined that although ACCES-VR 
has taken some actions toward proper data validation, it had not implemented internal controls, 
policies, or procedures that ensure data validation, and the accurate reporting of services 
provided to reportable students and participants during the period under review. RSA also 
determined that ACCES-VR did not submit accurate reports substantiated by required supporting 
documentation, as required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.40, or ensure accuracy and verification of

2.2 Failure to Exercise Sole Control over the Expenditure of VR Program Funds (Non-
Delegable Function)

20 
 
Issue: Whether ACCES-VR maintained sole responsibility for the expenditure of VR funds, as 
required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2). 
 
Requirements: The VR program regulations in 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1) specify minimum 
activities that are the responsibilities of the DSU. Specifically, ACCES-VR, as the DSU, must be 
solely responsible for: all decisions affecting the eligibility for VR services; the nature, scope 
and provision of services; determinations to close service records; policy formulation and 
implementation; the allocation and expenditure of VR funds; and participation of the VR 
program as a partner in the one-stop service delivery system (see also Section 
101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(V) of the Rehabilitation Act). ACCES-VR may not delegate any of these 
functions or responsibilities to another entity or individual (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(2)).  
 
Analysis: According to the conditionally approved (May 30, 2024) VR services portion of the 
Combined State Plan), NYSED is the designated State agency (DSA), and ACCES-VR is the 
DSU for the VR program, pursuant to the requirements of Section 101(a)(2) of the 
Rehabilitation Act. ACCES-VR is housed within ACCES, an organizational component of 
NYSED, making ACCES an intervening level between ACCES-VR (DSU) and NYSED (DSA). 
In the U.S. Department of Education’s Federal grants management system (GMS), which tracks 
drawdowns of Federal VR funds by grantees, the State identifies the ACCES-VR assistant 
commissioner (i.e., VR director) and the deputy commissioner of ACCES (intervening DSA 
level within the DSA) as the State recipient director and State authorizing official, respectively. 
During the monitoring activity, RSA learned that the Office of the New York State Comptroller 
(OSC) also has access to the GMS account. By submitting the State Plan, NYSED and ACCES-
VR assured RSA that they would satisfy all Federal requirements governing the VR program, 
which would include those related to ACCES-VR’s non-delegable functions discussed herein. 
 
The fiscal monitoring sessions attendees included: NYSED management staff (DSA level), 
ACCES’s Financial Services Administration Team (FAST) (intervening DSA level), and the 
ACCES-VR Director (DSU level). During those meetings, FAST staff indicated that the NYSED 
(DSA) and/or ACCES (intervening DSA) levels are overall responsible for the fiscal 
management of ACCES-VR (DSU); specifically, NYSED and/or ACCES are overall responsible 
for reviewing incurred costs, allocating costs, administering payroll, Federal reporting, and 
drawing down Federal funds on behalf of ACCES-VR. ACCES also is responsible for creating 
and submitting the ACCES-VR budget to NYSED and the Bureau of Budget Coordination, 
which reviews and approves the base budget for ACCES-VR, as well as the VR program’s 
projections and expenditures. The development of the VR program cost allocation plan is a 
centralized function within NYSED. In addition to NYSED’s and ACCES’ fiscal responsibilities 
for ACCES-VR, FAST staff also informed RSA that OSC is exclusively responsible for certain 
key VR program financial transactions (e.g., cash draws and payment issuance) as described 
further below and in the “Analysis” section of fiscal findings 3.2 and 3.3. Therefore, it is clear 
that the fiscal management of the VR program, particularly with respect to the allocation and 
expenditure of VR program funds, is performed by a four-tiered hierarchy in New York with 
OSC at the top, followed by NYSED (DSA), ACCES (intervening DSA level), and ACCES-VR 
(DSU) at the bottom.

recent undertaking, and it was unclear to RSA, based on the information provided during the 
monitoring activity, whether ACCES-VR was solely responsible for the allocation and

centralized function. For example, according to data reviewed by RSA, NYSED made almost

During the monitoring activity, RSA also inquired about the methodology for procuring VR

contracts” because vendors were not able to deliver the services at the rates in the existing 
contracts. Therefore, based on this example, RSA is concerned that ACCES-VR does not have 
the ability to exercise its non-delegable function over the provision of VR services (i.e., the 
provision of pre-employment transition services to potentially eligible students with disabilities), 
as it must pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(i) and (2); instead, it appears that OSC makes the 
final decisions regarding procuring those services, which has the net effect of that office making 
the final decisions about the provision of those VR services. To be clear, 2 C.F.R. § 200.317 
requires ACCES-VR to follow its State procurement policies and procedures; however, these 
procurement procedures must not interfere with the agency’s ability to satisfy fundamental 
requirements under the VR program, such as the provision of pre-employment transition services 
to potentially eligible students with disabilities, as required by Section 113(a) of the 
Rehabilitation Act. 
 
Section 101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(b)(1)(i) require that

not describe the nature and scope of this responsibility or how the DSU is to carry it out, 34 
C.F.R. § 361.13(c) requires that certain functions be reserved solely to the staff of the DSU, 
meaning that ACCES-VR cannot delegate them to any other agency or individual. These “non-
delegable” functions relate to decisions affecting, among other things, the allocation and 
expenditure of funds and the provision of services, as discussed above.

Because the State has centralized certain financial management and procurement functions

As discussed above, the facts demonstrate that the centralization of the financial management

ACCES-VR is not solely responsible for the expenditure of VR program funds for personnel

exercising its non-delegable responsibility over the financial management of VR program funds 
properly with respect to expenditures, it would have been aware of all transactions occurring in 
GMS and would have been able to explain to RSA the need for the frequent drawdowns 
described above, but it could not.  
With respect to the centralization of procurement approvals with the comptroller, the facts 
demonstrate that these levels of approval interfered with ACCES-VR’s non-delegable functions 
to expend funds for the provision of pre-employment transition services to potentially eligible

potentially eligible students with disabilities – a service required by Section 113(a) of the 
Rehabilitation Act with Federal VR funds reserved for that purpose. As a result, ACCES-VR 
ended its “potentially eligible pre-ETS contracts.” Decisions regarding the awarding of this 
particular contract fall within the scope of the provision of VR services, as well as the 
expenditure and allocation of VR funds. Therefore, decisions related to these matters require

disabilities, the delivery of those VR services, or the expenditure of VR program funds for those 
purposes (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(i), (1)(iv), and (2)), as the above facts demonstrate. 
Therefore, it is clear that ACCES-VR is not solely responsible for that decision-making process 
as it must be. 
Conclusion:  RSA found that ACCES-VR was not solely responsible for certain critical 
functions of the VR program, namely the allocation and expenditure of funds and the provision

ACCES-VR and may not be delegated to another agency or individual. During the on-site visit, 
RSA found that centralized procedures in the State interfered with ACCES-VR’s non-delegable 
functions, making it impossible for it to exercise sole control over expenditures and decisions 
related to the provision of pre-employment transition services to potentially eligible students 
with disabilities and expend the funds for those services.

2.3 VR Program Participants Denied the Provision of Timely VR Services and the Ability 
to Exercise Informed Choice  
 
Issue: Whether ACCES-VR implemented policies, procedures, or practices that delay the 
provision of VR services or prevent VR program participants from exercising informed choice 
throughout the VR process, including the selection of services and service providers, as required 
by 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.45(a)(1), 361.45(d)(2), and 361.52.  
 
Requirements: The VR program regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(a)(1) require the VR agency 
to assure, in its VR services portion of its State Plan, that it will develop and implement 
individualized plans for employment (IPEs) in a timely manner for each individual determined 
eligible for VR services (or for each eligible individual for whom the agency is able to provide 
services if the agency is operating under an order of selection).

26 
 
In developing and implementing IPEs, 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(d)(2) requires that the VR agency 
must give eligible individuals the opportunity to exercise informed choice in selecting—   
 
 The employment outcome, including employment settings; 
 The specific VR services needed to achieve the employment outcome, including the 
setting in which those services will be provided; 
 The entity or entities that will provide the VR services; and  
 The methods available for procuring the VR services. 
 
34 C.F.R. § 361.52(a) requires the VR agency to provide information and support services 
necessary to assist VR applicants and recipients of VR services in exercising informed choice 
throughout the VR process, consistent with the provisions of Section 102(d) of the Rehabilitation 
Act. VR agencies must develop written policies and procedures that enable an applicant or 
recipient of VR services to exercise informed choice throughout the VR process (34 C.F.R. § 
361.52(b)). These policies and procedures must provide for, among other things most pertinent to 
this Finding, developing and implementing flexible procurement policies and methods that 
facilitate the provision of VR services and that afford recipients of services meaningful choices 
among the methods used to procure VR services (34 C.F.R. § 361.52(b)(3)). 
 
Consistent with the requirement at 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(d)(2), the VR agency’s policies and 
procedures also must provide for assisting eligible individuals in acquiring information that 
enable them to exercise informed choice in the development of their IPE with respect to the 
selection of the—  
 
 Employment outcome; 
 Specific VR services needed to achieve the employment outcome; 
 Entity that will provide the services; 
 Employment setting and the settings in which the services will be provided; and 
 Methods available for procuring the services (34 C.F.R. § 361.52(b)(4)). 
 
Analysis: During the monitoring activity, RSA inquired and obtained further information about 
the provision of VR services by ACCES-VR, including its policies and procedures governing the 
provision of postsecondary education services to eligible individuals with disabilities who need 
such services to achieve the employment outcome identified on their approved IPEs. RSA 
interviewed ACCES-VR management regarding the agency’s policies and procedures governing 
postsecondary education to VR program participants, and RSA reviewed a memo issued by 
ACCES-VR to its district office managers, dated July 20, 2018, and titled, “College Contracts 
Returning Students Only.” In that memo, ACCES-VR informed its staff that it would allow 
authorizations for the fall 2018 semester only for returning students at those five postsecondary 
education institutions that had not returned their  contracts with NYSED; however, “[n]ew 
students who are planning to attend one of [the five] colleges for the first time under ACCES-
VR sponsorship will need to select an alternate college that is open for authorization by ACCES-
VR [(i.e., under contract with NYSED)] or incur the costs for college study on their own.” In 
other words, with the exception of those students returning to one of the five identified schools 
for the 2018-2019 school year, all students needing financial assistance from ACCES-VR were

27 
expected to either attend a school in the State University of New York (SUNY) or City 
University of New York (CUNY) system or a private or out-of-State school under contract with 
NYSED. The five postsecondary institutions that had not entered into contracts with NYSED 
were Southeastern University, Western New England University, Caldwell University, Inc., 
Colorado Technical University, and Laureat Education Inc. It is important to note that ACCES-
VR’s memo did not permit exceptions based on the programmatic or disability needs of VR 
program participants.  
 
Prior to the July 20, 2018, memo, ACCES-VR issued “Parameters for Private College 
Sponsorship Fall 2018.” According to that issuance, as of June 8, 2018, only 13 of 223 colleges 
had contracts for the 2018-2019 academic year, making them eligible to receive service 
authorizations from ACCES-VR staff. ACCES-VR informed their staff, they could not initiate 
service authorizations for any school that refused to sign a contract. As with the July 20, 2018, 
memo, this issuance did not provide any exceptions for an individual’s disability or 
programmatic needs. 
 
ACCES-VR explained to RSA that it issued the memo and accompanying issuance because 
NYSED has an internal control requirement that programs under its purview, including ACCES-
VR, must contract with private New York State and all out-of-state institutions of higher 
education in order to provide postsecondary education for participants at those institutions when 
the cost is anticipated to exceed $50,000. According to NYSED procurement policies, once 
contracting is initiated with a school, ACCES-VR cannot issue a billable service authorization 
for postsecondary education with a school until the contract is fully executed. ACCES-VR 
further explained that NYSED’s internal control is based on New York State Finance Law § 112 
which requires the Office of the New York State Comptroller – not NYSED or ACCES-VR –
approve all contracts entered into by State agencies, including those by ACCES-VR (see related 
Finding 2.2). 
 
As the DSA, NYSED may implement procurement procedures for the programs under its 
purview. Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.317, ACCES-VR, as the DSU, must follow the same State 
procurement procedures it uses when expending State funds when procuring goods or services 
with Federal VR funds. Therefore, since the NYSED procurement procedures are applicable to 
all programs under its purview, ACCES-VR must satisfy those procedures when procuring 
postsecondary education services with Federal VR funds, consistent with the requirements of 2 
C.F.R. § 200.317. Having said this, ACCES-VR also assures, in its VR services portion of its 
approved State Plan, that it will satisfy all Federal requirements governing the VR program, 
including that it will provide applicants and eligible individuals with disabilities with 
information and support services to assist them in exercising informed choice throughout the VR 
process, consistent with Section 102(d) of the Rehabilitation Act (section 101(a)(19) of the 
Rehabilitation Act). Section 102(d) of the Rehabilitation Act requires ACCES-VR to develop 
and implement written policies and procedures that enable each applicant or eligible individual 
to exercise informed choice throughout the entire rehabilitation process. ACCES-VR must 
develop these policies and procedures in consultation with its State Rehabilitation Council and 
these policies and procedures must, among other things—

28 
 Provide or assist eligible individuals in acquiring information that enables them to 
exercise informed choice in the development of their IPE with respect to the selection of 
the employment outcome, VR services and service providers, the employment setting 
and the settings in which the services will be provided, and methods for procuring 
services; and  
 Develop and implement flexible procurement policies and methods that facilitate the 
provision of services and afford eligible individuals meaningful choices among 
procurement methods. 
 
The link between the employment outcome and informed choice is further reinforced by the 
description of VR services in Section 103(a) of the Rehabilitation Act as “any services described 
in an individualized plan for employment necessary to assist an individual with a disability in 
preparing for, securing, retaining, or regaining an employment outcome that is consistent with 
the strengths, resources, priorities, concerns, abilities, capabilities, interests, and informed choice 
of the individual.” Informed choice is a decision-making process that occurs throughout the 
individual’s experience in the VR program. The role of the VR agency is to administer the VR 
program in a manner that supports the joint efforts of the individual and the VR counselor. 
Agencies can provide such support by implementing policies and procedures that provide the 
maximum opportunity for individuals to exercise informed choice, for the VR counselor to 
support individuals in that effort, and for the development of employment and service options 
that meet a wide range of individual needs.  
 
The postsecondary education policy implemented by ACCES-VR in 2018, which is consistent 
with NYSED’s procurement procedures, fails to satisfy the requirement at 34 C.F.R. § 
361.52(b)(3) because it is not flexible and does not afford eligible individuals with disabilities 
meaningful opportunities to exercise informed choice. As noted above, the ACCES-VR memo, 
consistent with NYSED’s procurement policies and procedures for postsecondary education, 
offers no exceptions for individual needs. Only “returning students” who had received financial 
support in prior years were exempt from the policy. To be flexible, and thus satisfy Federal 
requirements, ACCES-VR’s policies and procedures must allow for exceptions based on 
individual needs. For example, an individual with a most significant disability may need to 
attend only online because of transportation and other disability-related difficulties with in-
person education. ACCES-VR’s policies and procedures must be sufficiently flexible to permit 
the individual to attend school that offers an online program in the agreed-upon field, as 
identified on their IPE, without requiring the individual to incur the cost of the postsecondary 
education. When a policy does not permit such flexibility, the individual has two choices: forego 
attending school or incur significant debt for the cost. This does not constitute meaningful 
informed choice, as required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.52(b)(3).   
 
Moreover, in such an instance, ACCES-VR’s procurement procedures, which align with 
NYSED’s procurement procedures, have the net effect of imposing a financial participation on 
the individual who desires to attend an “off contract” school. Although ACCES-VR may develop 
written financial need and financial participation policies for the provision of VR services, the 
policies may not require cost participation so high as to effectively deny an eligible individual a 
necessary service (34 C.F.R. § 361.54(b)(2)(iv)(C)).  In its own memo, ACCES-VR instructs its 
staff to withhold VR financial support to any VR participant (other than the exempt students

29 
described previously) who desire to attend a school that refused to sign a contract, thereby 
forcing the student to either not receive a necessary VR service or incur substantial personal cost, 
neither of which is consistent with the requirements of 34 C.F.R. § 361.54(b)(2). For example, 
more recently, Southern New Hampshire University has not allowed new students to use interim 
authorizations while their contract is still pending, forcing some VR participants to pay out of 
pocket. RSA also learned that other colleges have withheld or threatened to withhold diplomas, 
transcripts, or class registrations, and have even threatened to evict customers from campus 
housing until billable authorizations are received. In addition, VR program participants at Sacred 
Heart University, the University of Rochester, and Adelphi University have faced similar 
scenarios, with the procurement policies resulting in at least one delayed graduation. 
 
While the requirement to provide informed choice is not unfettered, the Rehabilitation Act and 
its regulations make clear that individuals must be active participants throughout the entire VR 
process, including with respect to selecting service providers. However, as implemented, 
ACCES-VR does not provide individuals informed choice. As these examples demonstrate, 
ACCES-VR’s postsecondary education procurement procedures, which align with NYSED’s 
procurement procedures, are not flexible and does not provide meaningful choice to VR program 
participants. Furthermore, the procedures, in effect, establish a financial participation 
requirement that is so high that it effectively denies individuals necessary VR services. For these 
reasons, the procedures fail to satisfy Federal VR requirements governing informed choice at 
Section 101(a)(19) and 102(d) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.52. 
 
In addition to the above-described informed choice failures, ACCES-VR’s procurement 
procedures, which align with NYSED’s procurement procedures, impede ACCES-VR’s ability 
to implement IPEs in a timely manner, as required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(a)(1). As discussed in 
more detail in Finding 2.2, New York State Finance Law § 112 requires that OSC must approve 
contracts entered into by State agencies, including ACCES-VR, that exceed $50,000. According 
to information RSA learned during the monitoring activity, when ACCES-VR provides services 
(e.g., non-degree training, including cosmetology training and commercial driver license (CDL) 
training, vehicular modifications, and other goods and services), and the amount paid to one 
vendor over a period of time exceeds $50,000, ACCES-VR must enter into a contract and all 
services cease until the contract is fully executed.  
 
As a result, RSA learned that these procedures have caused delays or interruptions in the 
provision of VR services, contrary to the requirement of 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(a)(1). For example, 
RSA learned that Hair on Hudson, a cosmetology school, has reached its contract authorization 
limit and, under State law, requires a formal contract amendment to increase the amount of the 
contract.  During the time of this monitoring, at least five students were waiting more than six 
months to start their training until the State completed the contracting process, thereby delaying 
services to these students to the only cosmetology school within a 45-minute commute. As 
another example, RSA learned that a CDL training school in one district had between five and 
ten individuals on a waiting list for services while the vendor negotiated a contract to provide the 
services, thereby significantly delaying the training. As noted previously, ACCES-VR must 
satisfy its State procurement policies and procedures pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.317; however, 
those State procurement policies and procedures must not interfere with ACCES-VR’s ability to 
satisfy Federal VR program requirements governing the ability to provide timely services.

Conclusion: RSA found that ACCES-VR did not always provide VR participants with 
opportunities to exercise informed choice throughout the VR process and sometimes delayed or 
interrupted the provision of their VR services when implementing State procurement policies and 
procedures. Specifically, ACCES-VR’s processes and procedures, which align with NYSED’s 
procurement processes, procedures and practices limited VR participants’ ability to exercise 
meaningful choices due to their inflexible nature. Moreover, these procurement policies caused 
unnecessary delays or interruptions in the provision of VR services.

Finding 3.2 Program Income Requirements Not Met 
 
Issue: Whether the requirements regarding reporting and use of program income were met.  
 
Requirements: Grantees must account for program income in accordance with the provisions of 
the Uniform Administrative Requirements, Cost Principles, and Audit Requirements for Federal 
Awards (Uniform Guidance), codified at 2 C.F.R. § 200.1 and 2 C.F.R. § 200.307. Consistent 
with requirements set forth in both the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. § 200.307(e)(2)) and 
applicable program regulations, grantees must use program income only as an “addition” to the 
Federal award. In so doing, grantees must use program income for the same purposes authorized 
under the program. 
 
To the extent that program income funds are available, the grantee must disburse those funds 
(including repayments to a revolving fund), rebates, refunds, contract settlements, audit 
recoveries, and interest earned on such funds, before requesting additional funds from the 
Department in accordance with the following program regulations: 
 
 State Vocational Rehabilitation Services (34 C.F.R. § 361.63(c)(3)(ii)); 
 State Supported Employment Services (SE-A and SE-B) (34 C.F.R. § 363.24(b)); and 
 
The program income requirements are also included as a Grant Award Notification Attachment 
and are part of the terms and conditions for the VR and Supported Employment grant awards. 
 
Analysis: The fourth quarter SF-425 for award H126A190047 shows cash on hand on line 10c 
($1,176,542). If more than three business days of cash are on hand, RSA requires an explanation 
on SF-425 line 12, “Remarks,” explaining why the drawdown was made prematurely or other 
reasons for the excess cash. Grantees must minimize the time elapsing between the drawdown of 
Federal funds and disbursement by the grantee in accordance with Federal requirements (31 
C.F.R. § 205; 2 C.F.R. § 200.305(b)). Given that the last draw prior to the end of the reporting 
period was on September 17, 2019, there was no need to draw excess cash in advance. In its 
sixth quarter SF-425 for H126A190047, the agency reported $1,818,223 in 10.l (total Federal 
program income earned). The agency reported $1,185,409 in line 10.n (Program income 
expended in accordance with the addition alternative), and $632,814 in 10o (unexpended 
program income). However, in its sixth quarter report for H126A190047, the agency reported 
$3,152,183 in 10.l (total Federal program income earned); the agency reported $1,460,149 in line 
10.n (Program income expended in accordance with the addition alternative), and $1,692,034 in

($1,059,220) and showing an additional program income earned after the end of the year of

Finding 3.3 Contract Management, Monitoring and Oversight Unallowable Use of Funds 
Reserved for the Provision of Pre-Employment Transition Services and VR Services 
 
Issue: Whether ACCES-VR maintains effective internal control over Federal awards to provide 
reasonable assurance that the agency is managing the Federal awards in compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the awards, including controls related to 
developing, awarding, implementing, and monitoring contracts. 
 
Requirements: Federal regulations state that the non-Federal entity is responsible for oversight 
of the operations of the Federal award supported activities. The non-Federal entity must monitor 
its contracted activities under Federal awards to ensure compliance with applicable Federal 
requirements and that performance expectations are achieved. Monitoring by the non-Federal 
entity must cover each program, function, or activity (2 C.F.R. §§ 200.302(a) and 329(a)). VR 
program implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.3 and 12 require ACCES-VR to employ 
methods and practices necessary for the proper administration and for carrying out all functions 
under the State plan. These methods include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and 
financial accountability. As such, ACCES-VR must monitor and evaluate grant supported 
activities to ensure compliance of all activities performed under the VR program.  
 
In accordance with Section 113 of the Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.48(a), each State must ensure the 
designated State unit, in collaboration with the local educational agencies involved, provide, or 
arrange for the provision of, pre-employment transition services for all students with disabilities, 
as defined in 34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(51), in need of such services, from Federal funds reserved in 
accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.65, and any funds made available from State, local, or private 
funding sources. Funds reserved and made available may be used to provide potentially eligible 
and eligible students with disabilities the five required pre-employment transition services, 
authorized activities, and pre-employment transition coordination activities (34 C.F.R.

39 
§ 361.48(a)).

possible, be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART). Although 
MOUs don’t move through the State Comptroller’s Office, the use of SMART goals within the 
MOU process is beneficial to ACCES. At a minimum, SMART contract terms address the 
following:

Scope and purpose of the contract; the contract period; services to be performed

terms (e.g., corrective action, repayment of overcharges, full fiscal review of all 
invoices, and contract termination) 
Measurable: 
Quantities of commodities or service to be provided; number and nature of clients 
served; target dates; unit or fixed prices for goods and services; level of expertise 
required to perform the required work, such as professional licenses, certifications, 
and educational degrees 
Achievable: 
Realistic deliverables from the contractor or specific circumstances in which the

Relevant: 
Performance requirements essential for the contract; record keeping and reporting

Analysis: NYSED has an executed MOU with Mohawk Valley Community College (MVCC), 
agreement 023-010 effective May 1, 2023, through April 30, 2026, and not to exceed 
$9,000.000. The MOU does not cite the minimum number of students to be served but does state 
that the maximum number of students to be served is 1,050, which results in a maximum cost of 
$8,571 (rounded) per-student. However, since the number of students to be served is a maximum 
and no minimum number is provided, the contractor could recover costs for completing activities 
described in the scope without providing any pre-employment transition services. 
 
RSA reviewed the agreement and noted several areas of non-compliance as follows: 
 
Measurable Performance Standards 
 
The scope of work in the MOU has three main components: that MVCC will “create a targeted 
strategy that will assist students with disabilities in receiving the five required pre-employment 
transition services; that MVCC will partner with local schools and community-based 
organizations to identify students with disabilities who are eligible to receive pre-employment

transition services; and that MVCC will hire staff to provide pre-employment transition

actually stated in the agreement. As written, it gives the appearance that the vendor could collect 
$9,000,000.00 merely for developing a targeted strategy, partnering with schools and 
organizations, and hiring staff in accordance with the agreement, without necessarily providing 
any pre-employment transition services. The obligations section of the MOU references the 
delivery of pre-employment transition services. 
 
To be effectively measurable and meaningful, a performance standard should contain a baseline 
and either an amount or a percentage of change or variance (increase/decrease) from the 
baseline. Further, performance objectives should be specific to pre-employment transition 
services for students with disabilities, the requirements under the Rehabilitation Act, as 
amended, and its implementing regulations.  
 
Some examples of objectively measurable performance standards are— 
 
 Increase job exploration counseling services for students with disabilities, from 16 
students (baseline) to 24 students (measurable increase) by (date)  
 Conduct 12 workplace readiness sessions (baseline) by (date) (measurement is the actual 
number of sessions conducted by the stated date). 
 Decrease the high school dropout rate for students with disabilities (baseline) by 50% 
(measurable decrease) by (date).  
  
Reasonable Costs 
 
The scope of the MOU indicates that MVCC will hire staff, faculty, and mentors; however, the 
MOU does not include requirements for the portion of MVCC staff time that is to be spent 
providing direct pre-employment transition services, or on other duties as assigned. Specifically, 
the MVCC MOU budget includes 23 FTEs at an approximate cost of $5,500,000 for 
salaries/wages and fringe benefits over the three-year contract term. The FTEs assigned to this

support (.5 FTE), career services specialist (1.5 FTE), accessibilities services (1.0 FTE), program 
coordinator high school (15.0 FTEs), applied learning coordinator (1.0 FTE), and applied 
learning coordinator high school (1.0 FTE). At the time of this report, RSA was unable to verify 
the portion of FTE time that was allocated to providing services through this contract; the portion 
of time spent directly providing the five pre-employment transition services; how MVCC staff 
time is cost allocated to the programs (e.g., MVCC and high schools) to which they are assigned; 
or who is validating MVCC staff time charged to the MOU. 
 
Additionally, since the MOU does not require the vendor to serve a minimum number of students 
to be served, ACCES-VR is unable to make a valid determination that the costs or staff time 
allocation are reasonable. The budget also includes $63,000 for travel and transportation, 
$74,000 for mentoring, and $405,000 for stipends. Again, without knowing the number of
