DECISION AND AWARD
Pursuant to the Randolph-Sheppard Act, 20 U.S.C. §§ 107 through 107f (R-S Act), an arbitration was convened in the above-captioned matter, as authorized by 20 U.S.C.§ 107d-2. Petitioner South Carolina Commission for the Blind (SC), the designated State Licensing Agency (SLA) pursuant to the R-S Act, designated Ms. Susan Rockwood Gashel as arbitrator, and the United States Marine Corps (MC) designated Ms. Marcia Bachman as arbitrator. Mr. John S. West was jointly designated by arbitrators Gashel and Bachman as Panel Chair. The hearing took place on March 22, 2022, in Washington, D.C. The SC was represented by Peter Nolan and Andrew J. Schumacher. The MC was represented by John McHugh and Amanda Belanger.
ISSUE
Whether the MC’s conduct of contract Solicitation No. M95494-18-R-0003 and its evaluation of proposals violated the R-S Act and implementing regulations by failing to afford the SLA the R-S Act’s priority to establish vending facilities on Federal property as required by 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.30(a) and 395.33(a) and (b) and by placing a limitation on the operation and placement of vending facilities without advance justification from the Secretary of Education as required by 34 C.F.R. 395.30(b). (SC Ex. 3, DoE Convening Letter at p. SCCB_0007.)
STANDARD OF REVIEW
This Panel is directed by the United States Department of Education (DoE) to, in accordance with the provisions of subchapter II of chapter 5 of Title 5, give notice, conduct a hearing, and render its decision. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2(a). That subchapter provides, at 5 U.S.C. § 556(d), that the proponent of an order has the burden of proof and an order may issue “in accordance with the reliable, probative, and substantial evidence.” Id.
Accordingly, the SC has the burden of proof to prove by substantial evidence that the MC violated the R-S Act. “This is something more than a mere scintilla but something less than the weight of the evidence.” Pennaco Energy v. U.S. Dep’t of Interior, 377 F.3d.
1147, 1156 (10th Cir. 2004).

CONCLUSIONS OF LAW
The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA), the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) and the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation (DFARS) apply “except in cases of other procurement procedures expressly authorized by statute.” 10 U.S.C. § 2304(a)(1). The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst.  V. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001); NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003); NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). Accordingly, the FAR and DFARS regulations do not apply to R-S Act procurements, insofar as they conflict with the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
The SLA is not required to file a pre-award bid protest, because the law does not require the doing of a futile act. Whether filed in the Government Accountability Office (GAO), the United States Court of Claims, or other venue, such a protest would be dismissed as not ripe for adjudication. If filed in GAO, “dismissal of a protest challenging an SLA’s proposal from the competitive range is appropriate.” GAO Decision B-400583, B-400583.2. With respect to United States Court of Claims, see State v. United States, 192 F. Supp. 3d 1184, 1193 (D. Kan. 2016) (In 2005, the Federal Circuit held that, when a complaint alleges a violation of the R-S Act, the Court of Federal Claims lacks Tucker Act jurisdiction to hear the case until the R-S Act’s administrative remedy—arbitration—is exhausted.)  See, also, Georgia Business Enterprise Program, GAO B-416182.2, Nov. 23, 2018, (protest dismissed where SLA alleged that its proposal was improperly excluded from the competitive range as the protest implicates a potential violation of the R-S Act). See, also, Colo. Dep’t of Human Serv. v. U.S., 74 Fed. Cl. 339 (2006) (Court of Claims lacks jurisdiction under Tucker Act and the Administrative Procedure Act until an arbitration is concluded).
When a Federal property managing agency is evaluating a cafeteria solicitation where an SLA places a bid, the agency must evaluate the bid pursuant to the R-S Act; it is impermissible by law for an SLA bid to be evaluated pursuant to “best value” or lowest price technically acceptable offer. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e) (Secretary of Education is given sole authority to determine, on an individual basis ... that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality...”); Randolph- Sheppard Vendors v. Harris, 628 F.2d 1364, 1367 (D.C. Cir. 1980); Commonwealth v. U.S., No. 04-831C, at *11 (Fed. Cl. Oct. 13, 2004).
34 CFR 395.33(a) provides for an individualized determination by the Secretary of Education, after consultation with the Federal agency, to ensure that the operation provides maximum employment opportunities to blind vendors to the greatest extent possible. The MC failed to consult with the Secretary of Education as required by 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a). Accordingly, the Panel concludes the MC violated the R-S Act when it failed seek a determination from DoE’s Secretary that the cafeteria operation “can be provided at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided.” 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a). Only DoE has the authority to impose a limitation on the placement or operation of a vending facility. 20 U.S.C. § 107. NISH v. Cohen, 95 F. Supp. 2d 497, 502 n.7 (E.D. Va 2000).
The uncontroverted evidence at the hearing, including the uncontroverted errors in the SSEB Report (MC Exhibit 16), as well as the MC’s withholding of documents without legal authority to do so, supports the adverse inferences imposedupon the MC, as a matter of law.
ANALYSIS
The R-S Act and its Implementing Regulations Establish that the Convened Arbitration Panel Is the Proper Venue
The MC objects to the Panel’s consideration of the MC’s evaluation of the SC’s proposal. (MC’S Post-Arbitration Hearing Brief (MC Brief), p. 21.) The MC objects to the SC’s failure to file an action with the GAO or the Court of Federal Claims. MC Brief, p.
22. The MC claims the Panel lacks authority to “disturb an agency contracting officer’s evaluation findings and decisions pursuant to the CICA.” MC Brief, p. 26. In fact, the MC objects to any evaluation by the Panel. MC Brief, p. 27.
The MC’s objections and arguments have no basis in law. As set forth in section B below, were an SLA to file an action with GAO or the Court of Claims, it would be dismissed as premature, because the SLA has yet to exhaust its administrative remedies. The MC ignores long-settled law. The CICA does not apply where other procurement procedures are expressly authorized by statute. 10 U.S.C. § 2304(a)(1). The R-S Act is such a procurement procedure. Automated Comm. Syst. v. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78 (2001); NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003); NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001). This means that the FAR and DFARS regulations cannot apply where they conflict with the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
The R-S Act establishes a priority for blind vendors to operate all cafeteria and cafeteria-related contracts on Federal property.  Every Federal agency “shall take all steps necessary to assure” that “one or more vending facilities for operation by blind licensees shall be located on all Federal property.” 34 C.F.R. 395.30(a). The R-S Act provides for arbitration by a Panel constituted as this Panel is, of an SLA complaint filed on the basis that the SLA determines that a Federal property managing department has failed to comply with the R-S Act. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-1(b), 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2. Simply put, the MC has failed to provide any statute or case law that supports its claim that the MC’s contracting officer has unfettered discretion to determine application of the R-S Act. That is this Panel’s charge. Moreover, by the withholding of discovery, as more fully set out in sections E and F below, the MC has made it impossible for this Panel to determine whether or not the contracting officer correctly evaluated Petitioner’s proposal. Thus, contrary to the MC’s assertion at MC Brief, p. 27, no court (and certainly not this Panel) could possibly give deference to the contracting officer’s decision in an R-S Act arbitration, where the MC has made it impossible to meaningfully compare bids.
The MC’s Waiver Arguments are Unavailing - the Court of Claims and the GAO Routinely Dismiss R-S Act Complaints unless Administrative Procedures (i.e., this Arbitration) are Exhausted
The MC repeatedly objects to the SC’s failure to file a pre-award bid protest with GAO or Court of Claims. MC Brief, p. 22, 25, 26, 40-42, 43-45. The MC cites a number of cases that are superseded by Kentucky v. United States, 62 Fed. Cl. 445, 460 (2004), aff’d sum nom Kentucky Educ. Cabinet Dep’t for the Blind v. United States, 424 F.3d 1222 (Fed. Cir. 2005):
this court concludes that the statutory scheme of the RSA requires exhaustion of administrative procedures before an aggrieved SLA may raise an RSA claim in this court.
As a matter of routine, the GAO dismisses bid protests brought by SLAs. GAO B- 400912.2, GAO B-400583; B400583.2. The Court of Claims has, since 2004, rejected bid protests filed by SLAs.  See, Colo. Dep’t of Human Serv. v. U.S., 74 Fed. Cl. 339
(2006), where the Court made it clear that it lacks jurisdiction under the Tucker Act and the APA until after an arbitration is concluded. See, also, State of Kansas v. U.S., 192 F.
Supp.3d 1184, 1194-95 (D. Kan. 2016), aff’d in part sub. nom.  Kansas by & through Kansas Dep’t for Children & Families v. SourceAmerica, 874.F.3d 1226 (10th Cir. 2017).
At p. 41, the MC cites to Blue & Gold, Fleet, L.P. v. United States, 492 F. 3d 1308, 1313 (2007), in support of its position. The Panel concludes that the MC’s argument that the SC cannot contend that discussions should have been held is frivolous and unwarranted by existing law because the waiver doctrine cannot possibly apply where GAO and the Court of Federal Claims would summarily dismiss. After all, “the law does not require the performance of vain or useless things.” Augusta v. Augusta-Richmond, 249 F. App’x 93,
99, n 6 (11th Cir. 2007).
This Panel is Authorized and Required to Address the Issues Identified in the United States Department of Education September 11, 2019 Convening Letter
The MC claims, without statutory, regulatory or case law in support of its position, that “the regulatory requirements at [34 C.F.R. §] 395.30 are not applicable to the legal issues in this matter.” MC Brief, p. 47, n. 23.
In any event, this Panel is charged by DoE to determine 34 C.F.R. § 395.30 applicability to the MC’s evaluation of this solicitation.
The analysis starts with the definition of “vending facility” at 20 U.S.C. § 107(e) to include cafeterias. DoE created two ways for Federal agencies to implement the R-S Act: The first is through a permit system for all vending facilities that are not cafeterias. 34
C.F.R. § 395.34. The second is to implement 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e), which provides for the Secretary of Education to prescribe regulations, which provide for the Secretary of Education, on an individual basis, after consultation with the head of the installation, to determine whether or not the “operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided...”
Because cafeterias are, in fact, vending facilities, all of the regulations at 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.1 through 395.38 apply to cafeterias, with the sole exception being those applicable to vending facilities that are not cafeterias (34 C.F.R. §§ 395.34, 395.35). Thus, there is no legal justification for a claim that any part of the regulation does not apply to cafeterias, with the sole exception identified above.
Accordingly, as directed to by DoE, the Panel will determine whether the MC complied with the requirements of 34 C.F.R. §§ 395.30(a), 33(a) and (b), and 30(b).
30(a) – the MC Failed to Take All Steps Necessary to Ensure that Wherever Feasible, One or More Vending Facilities are Located on All Federal Property
At MC Brief, p. 47, the MC states that “Petitioner has conflated the regulatory requirements” and that 34 C.F.R. § 395.30 is “not applicable” to the issues in this matter. The MC is incorrect. Federal agencies have an affirmative duty to establish and maintain facilities for operation by blind licensees on all Federal property, wherever feasible. This means that the SC had the priority granted by the R-S Act, and that the Secretary of Education’s role was to determine, “on an individual basis and after consultation with the head of the appropriate installation, that such operation can be provided at a reasonable cost with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided to employees...” 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e). Since the MC rejected the SC’s bid without seeking the Secretary’s approval as to whether it was feasible for a blind-operated vending facility to operate, the MC violated 34 C.F.R. § 395.30(a).
33(a) The MC Usurped the Secretary’s Authority to Determine, on an Individual Basis, that the Operation Can be Provided at a Reasonable Cost, with Food of a High Quality Comparable to that Currently Provided Employees
This regulation requires two things of all Federal agencies soliciting bids for cafeteria contracts: First, DoE’s Secretary makes the determination, on an individual basis, that a blind licensee can operate the cafeteria at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality comparable to that currently provided employees, and secondly, that employment opportunities for blind vendors are to maximized to the greatest extent possible. The MC failed on both counts; it made the decision to reject the SLA’s bid without submitting the matter to DoE’s Secretary, and it eliminated blind vendors’ employment opportunities. Tex. Workforce Comm’n v. U.S. Dep’t of Educ., Rehab. Servs. Admin., 354 F. Supp. 3d 722, 725 (W. D. Tex. 2018), Georgia v. United States, 398 F. Supp.3d 1330 (S.D. Ga. 2019), Commonwealth v. U.S., No.: 04-831C, at *11 (Fed. Cl. Oct. 13, 2004).
33(b) Due to the MC’s Failure to Provide Necessary Discovery this Panel was Unable to Meaningfully Evaluate whether All Bids Were Judged by the Solicitation’s Criteria
The SC is correct that the R-S Act’s must be interpreted so as to effectuate the R- S Act priority. 20 U.S.C. § 107d-3(e) directs the Secretary to prescribe regulations to ensure that the R-S Act’s priority is applied to cafeterias, and gives the Secretary the final decision-making authority. To interpret 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b) to permit the MC to unilaterally determine the competitive range so as to exclude an SLA’s bid would nullify the priority. It is black letter law that a “regulation must be consistent with the statute it implements, any interpretation of a regulation must accord with the statute as well.” Hilario-Paulino v. Pugh, 194 Fed. Appx. 900, 902, n.1 (11th Cir. 2006).
As more thoroughly discussed in section D below, the MC not only failed to place the SC’s bid in the competitive range, but it also failed to provide discovery thus preventing this Panel, on behalf of the DoE’s Secretary, from assessing the contracting officer’s compliance with the R-S Act. These failures violated 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(b). We simply do not know whether the MC fairly judged the SLA’s bid, because it has refused to comply with every discovery order. No witness testified on behalf of the MC. Mr. James’ uncontroverted testimony is that meaningful discussions would have bridged the gap between proposals. Given the SC’s dissatisfaction with the MC’s action taken, it was authorized to file a complaint with DoE, and this Panel is empowered to issue its decision, based on the Secretary’s authority.
30(b) The Solicitation’s Terms and the MC’s Evaluation of the SLA’s Bid Placed a Limitation on the Operation of a Vending Facility Without First Obtaining Authority from the Secretary to Do So
While the Solicitation states that the R-S Act applies “to this acquisition for the operation of a military dining facility,” the MC’s inclusion of the “best value” standard placed a limitation on the operation of a vending facility without first obtaining the Secretary’s approval. Although the Solicitation does contain the R-S Act standard, it is ambiguous in that it placed two conflicting standards in the solicitation, without first obtaining the Secretary’s approval, in violation of the R-S Act. The MC failed to seek the Secretary’s determination as to whether it could replace the criteria for award, thus placing a limitation on the operation of a vending facility, and contravening 20 U.S.C. § 107(b)(2), which requires the Secretary’s approval before placing such a limitation.
The R-S Act Requires that Federal Agencies Establish a Competitive Range when Evaluating an SLA’s Proposed Submission
The R-S Act Cannot be Voided by Failing to Place an SLA’s Bid in the Competitive Range
The R-S Act’s implementing regulations require that contracting officers establish a competitive range when evaluating contracts that pertain to the operation of a cafeteria. A contracting officer does not have the authority to eviscerate the R-S Act by simply foregoing the establishment of a competitive range. If all Federal agencies decided to forego the establishment of a competitive range in evaluating all procurements where the R-S Act requires that the SLA be invited to respond for a cafeteria contract, there could be no cafeteria contracts operated by blind vendors. The R-S Act cannot be so interpreted, for to do so would nullify the statute. The MC is wrong that the solicitation at issue is governed by FAR Part 15 (p. 37). Because the CICA does not apply to the R-S Act bidder, the bid must be evaluated pursuant to the R-S Act standards.
At MC Brief, p. 6, fn. 6, the MC references a 2006 "Joint Report to Congress" signed by representatives of the Department of Defense, Department of Education, and the Chairman of the Committee for Purchase from People Who Are Blind or Severely Disabled (Joint Report), which made certain policy recommendations regarding the intersection of the R-S Act and the Javits-Day-O’Wagner Act (JWOD) and to which types of contracts those statutes' sometimes-competing priorities should apply. The Joint Report itself did not go through the notice, comment, and review procedures required of regulations. Moreover, the Department of Defense issued a Memorandum in 2007 directing that the Joint Report "should not be cited in individual solicitations until it is implemented in complementary regulations by the ED and DoD." No such complimentary regulations have been issued. Thus, the Joint Report is not dispositive, or even authoritative.
CICA does not apply; neither do FAR nor DFARS. R-S Act regulations apply. At the time regulations were promulgated, the term “competitive range” meant that any proposal “must be considered to be within a competitive range so as to require negotiations unless it is so technically inferior or out of line with regard to price that meaningful discussions are precluded.” To the Sec’y of the Air Force, 1968 U.S. Comp. Gen. LEXIS 37, 48, 48 Comp. Gen 314 (Comp. Gen November 13, 1968). We must interpret the R-S Act’s regulations (adopted in 1977) by reference to how the term “competitive range” was used at the time the regulation was drafted. This Panel must carefully consider the text, structure, history, and purpose of the regulation. Kisor v. Wilkie, 139 S.Ct. 2400, 2415 (2019). When Congress last amended the R-S Act, in 1974, commanders of military installations were found to be “singularly insensitive to the need to develop the Program.” S. REP. NO. 93-937, p. 10. The Panel must review how the term “competitive range” was used at the time the regulation was drafted.  District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570, 581-82 (2008). According to the 1995 version of 48 C.F.R. § 15.609, “When there is doubt as to whether a proposal is in the competitive range, the proposal should be included.” The MC’s use of the more restricted definition of competition in the current FARS does not implement the R-S Act priority.
Since at least 1944, when the Supreme Court in Skidmore v. Swift Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), decided this issue, agency interpretations reached without benefit of notice and comment procedures may be considered as persuasive evidence:
We consider that the rulings, interpretations and opinions of the Administrator under this Act, while not controlling upon the courts by reason of their authority, do constitute a body of experience and informed judgment to which courts and litigants may properly resort for guidance. The weight of such a judgment in a particular case will depend upon the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade, if lacking power to control.
Skidmore v. Swift Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The handbook in question was issued in 1988, and remained in effect until January 18, 2017, when it was withdrawn, along with numerous other policy issuances that “are outdated due to changes in law, regulations, or reporting requirements resulting from the amendment to the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 made by the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act.” The Panel concludes that, because of the MC’s non-compliance with reasonable discovery requests, that this issue is not essential to the resolution of this case. Accordingly, we decline to address it.
The Evidence Withheld From the Panel Makes its Impossible for the Panel to Properly Evaluate the SC’s Bid Could Have Been Made Acceptable Through Meaningful Discussions
The MC has made it impossible for this Panel to meaningfully review the contracting officer’s decision by withholding, even from in camera review, the SSEB report. The MC’s argument is that the Panel has no authority to set aside the contracting officer’s decision. The problem with this position is that the Panel has already held that CICA, FAR, and DFARS do not apply to the evaluation of the SLA’s proposal. Because the MC did not seek a finding from the Secretary that the SC’s bid was unable to operate the cafeteria at a reasonable cost, with high quality food, this Panel was specifically convened to evaluate the contracting officer’s evaluation of the bids responsive to the Solicitation.
To Remedy the MC’s Refusal to Provide Legally Required Discovery and to Avoid Prejudice to Petitioner, the Panel’s Adverse Inferences are Reasonable and Logically Related to the Withheld Documents
The MC Received Due Process of Law
The Panel has the Authority to Require Production of Documents
In 1978, DoE promulgated its Revised Interim Policies and Procedures for Convening and Conducting an Arbitration Pursuant to Sections 5(b) and 6 of the R-S Act as amended (Policies & Procedures). Section 9(c) thereof states that R-S Act panels do “not have the authority to compel by subpoena the production of witnesses, papers, or other evidence.” The Policies & Procedures were not enacted as regulation. According to the R-S Act, “the panel shall, in accordance with the provisions of subchapter II of chapter 5 of Title 5, give notice, conduct a hearing, and render its decision which shall be subject to appeal and review as a final agency action for purposes of chapter 7 of such title 5.” 20 U.S.C. § 107d-2. According to 5 U.S.C. § 556(c), this tribunal is authorized, inter alia, to issue subpoenas, “rule on offers of proof and receive relevant evidence.” As was the case in Bank of La. v. Fed. Deposit Ins. Corp., 919 F.3d 916, 927 (5th Cir. 2019), “The pertinent statute expressly incorporates the procedural and evidentiary accoutrements typical of administrative proceedings— including the power to issue subpoenas, take evidence, and order depositions.” Accordingly, the Panel holds that the MC’s argument that this Panel lacks authority to order discovery is without merit, as the APA’s discovery procedures are expressly incorporated into the R-S Act, and, therefore, the Policies & Procedures that conflict with the APA are pre-empted.
This Panel Gave the MC Ample Opportunity to Present its Arguments
The MC failed and refused to provide discovery to the SC so that this Panel could properly evaluate whether the contracting officer’s decision, not to place the SC’s bid in the competitive range, violated the R-S Act. At MC Brief, p. 8, fn. 8, the MC makes a claim regarding “an irrefutable fact in the record.” Yet, the MC’s litigation conduct (no witnesses, no discovery) has made it impossible to refute any alleged facts.
At MC Brief, p. 27, the MC contends that it has been denied due process of law, claiming that it had no opportunity to respond with its position prior to the Panel’s imposition of adverse inferences. Due process requires notice and an opportunity to present any objections. United Student Aid Funds, Inc. v. Espinoza, 559 U.S. 260, 261 (2010). As is evident from the above, the Panel provided the MC with due process.
Thus, the MC has made an utterly baseless due process claim, as it submitted its objections to proposed sanctions, and had an opportunity to argue those objections before the hearing convened. In fact, it is the MC that has decided to deny process to the SC by refusing to comply with the most basic of discovery requests.
The Panel issued its Order regarding sanctions on March 16, 2022.
No Bid was Superior to the SC's Bid
“The logic supporting the adverse inference rule is that a party fails to produce evidence in its control in order to conceal adverse facts.” BNSF Railway Co. v. Brotherhood of Maintenance of Way Employees, 550 F.3d 418, 424 (5th Cir. 2008).
Herein, the MC wants to contend that the SC failed to provide the Panel with any evidence of what a passing grade looked like in comparison. Without the requested discovery, and in particular the SSEB Report, the Panel was prevented from determining whether other bidders had greater deficiencies. Accordingly, the Panel had no option other than to impose an adverse inference based on the fact that the MC knowingly and purposefully withheld information that had the potential to be adverse to its stated position, without legal justification therefor. The only logical adverse inference is that no bid was superior to the SC’s bid, thus the Panel’s imposition of this adverse inference is permissible by law and required by the interests of justice.
The SC’s Bid was Scored Differently from the Winning Bidder’s Offer to the Detriment of the SC
At MC Brief, p. 29, the MC claims that the Trade Secrets Act and the Procurement Integrity Act authorize it to withhold discovery. The MC does so without explaining how these two laws impact the specific facts of this case.
This failure authorizes us to draw an adverse inference:
The drawing of an adverse inference against a party who fails to come forward with relevant evidence within its control is a reasonable and well-recognized evidentiary rule Norfolk Western Ry. v. Transp, 17 F.3d 696, 702 (4th Cir. 1994) (citations omitted).
The Trade Secrets Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1905, prohibits Federal agencies from disclosing confidential information, “to any extent not authorized by law.” The exceptions to the prohibition at 18 U.S. Code § 1833, expressly authorize disclosure to any Federal or State government official or attorney, directly or indirectly, that is made in confidence and in a filing in any lawsuit or other proceeding if that filing is under seal (e.g., under a protective order). The Panel issued such a protective order.
The Procurement Integrity Act expressly prohibits disclosure of contractor bid information before the award of a Federal agency procurement.  41 U.S.C. § 2102 (emphasis added). Since the Panel issued a Protective Order, and since the documents were sought post award, it was logical for this Panel to impose an adverse inference:
Nor was it illogical for the Board to presume that a party with significant interests at stake would voluntarily produce for inspection information in its possession that is favorable or even neutral to its position, especially where that party itself had earlier advanced the information as dispositive.
Norfolk at 177 F.3d 702.
The Panel was prevented from comparing the SC’s score with the scores of the other bidders, and particularly with respect to the scoring of the incumbent, and ultimately successful, bidder. Accordingly, an adverse inference that SC’s bid was scored differently is logically required; thus, the Panel’s imposition of this adverse inference is permissible by law and required by the interests of justice.
IV.	CONCLUSION
Here, the SC bid was rejected, and the MC has refused to provide the Panel with documents under its exclusive control that would enable this Panel to evaluate whether the MC’s assertions regarding the MC’s bid were valid. Moreover, the MC did not consult with the Secretary of Education at any time about whether the SC could provide good food at a reasonable price. The Panel Majority finds that the MC violated 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a) when it failed to consult with the Secretary of Education. Further, it finds that even if the state licensing agency's bid is not within the competitive range set by the contracting agency, the matter still must be returned to the Secretary to decide, after consulting with the contracting agency, if the blind vendor can provide an operation at a reasonable cost, with food of a high quality. The statutory and regulatory scheme make it clear that an SLA's bid cannot be rejected without consulting with the Secretary of Education who makes the final decision. 34 C.F.R. § 395.33(a).
Having found that the MC violated the R-S Act, the next issue is the remedy to be afforded to the SC. The MC contends that the Panel’s role is solely limited to determining whether the R-S Act was violated. With this constraint in mind, the Panel Majority finds:
AWARD
The solicitation at issue in this case pertains to the operation of a cafeteria and is therefore subject to the requirement of the R-S Act and its implementing regulations.
The MC violated the R-S Act and its implementing regulations when it failed to properly apply the R-S Act priority for blind vendors to the Solicitation and Contract at issue in this arbitration.
The MC violated the R-S Act and its implementing regulations in failing to maximize opportunities for blind vendors.
The MC shall cause the acts or practices found by this Panel to be in violation of the R-S Act and its implementing regulations to be terminated promptly; and shall take such other action as may be necessary to carry out the decision of the Panel Majority.
To that end, the Panel Majority finds as a matter of law that the MC is obligated under the R-S Act and its implementing regulations to resolicit the Solicitation at issue and grant the SC and its blind vendor the priority afforded by the R-S Act.

DISSENT
WHY THE DECISION AND AWARD FOR R-S/19-01 CANNOT BE ADOPTED
The Secretary of Education must reject the Decision and Award for arbitration case R-S/19-01 and deny the September 2018 complaint filed by the South Carolina Commission for the Blind (SCCB) under the Randolph-Sheppard (R-S) Act. The Decision and Award is wrong with regard to the law and the facts.
SCCB's complaint was without merit, should have been dismissed for lack of jurisdiction and failure to state a claim under the R-S Act (20 U.S.C. 107 et seq.) and its implementing regulations (34 CFR Part 395). SCCB's complaint must be denied, now.
The Decision and Award misapplied and misinterpreted the R-S Act and its implementing regulations, and advocated new rules that are not in the law.
The Decision and Award did not identify any act or practice of the United States Marine Corps (USMC) that violated any provision of the R-S Act or its implementing regulations as they are actually written. USMC has nothing to terminate.
In January 2018, SCCB submitted a $1.2 Billion offer for a contract to operate military dining facilities at USMC installations in the eastern United States. The USMC rated SCCB's offer "Unacceptable" and rejected it.
As will be elucidated below, SCCB's offer did not meet the minimum R-S standards. SCCB was not capable of performing the contract and not capable of correcting the deficiencies in its offer even if the USMC had held discussions.
Even if there had been no other offer, SCCB had no right to priority, competitive range, discussions, Secretary of Education review or award under any provision of the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
PROBLEMS WITH DECLARING NEW RULES. Based on flawed assumptions, the Decision and Award declares (1) only state governments can operate military dining facilities, and (2) only the Secretary of Education can evaluate a state government's offer and "make the final contracting decision" about who wins a Defense contract.
The Decision and Award devises new rules that would say: competitive range and discussions are always required; the Contracting Officer can never reject a state licensing agency offer no matter how bad it is technically and no matter how unduly high priced; and only the Secretary of Education can reject a state licensing agency offer. This is simply not written anywhere in the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
To implement policy goals, the R-S Act at 20 U.S.C. 107(b) and 107d-3(e) requires the Secretary of Education issue "regulations" after consulting all the federal agencies that manage federal property. The R-S Act at 20 U.S.C. 107d-2 and the R-S regulation at 34 CFR 395.37 authorize arbitration panels only to identify acts or practices of a federal agency that violate the R-S Act or its implementing regulations. Arbitration panels are not authorized to make up new rules or policy ideals.
The result of the new rules announced in the Decision and Award would give the Secretary of Education (and arbitration panels acting in the name of Education) "priority" over the Secretaries of the Military Departments and the Commandant of the USMC to decide (1) the dollar amount of Defense appropriations that must be expended on food services and (2) what quality and quantity of food is good enough for Marines and other members of the armed forces. This is not viable precedent.
PROBLEMS WITH LEGAL REFERENCES IN SCCB's COMPLAINT.
SCCB complained its offer was rated differently than the winning offer and complained the USMC should have established a competitive range and held discussions. The R-S Act and regulations do not prescribe how to evaluate offers, set competitive range or hold discussions. The Competition in Contracting Act (CICA) and Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) govern contracting decisions such as these. The complaint cites the FAR and Defense FAR Supplement (DFARS) as authority for its complaints. Even SCCB recognized these are not R-S violations.
SCCB also complained, "The USMC's decision to eliminate SCCB's offer from the competition was not fully justified in writing to the Secretary of Education, and the Secretary of Education was given no opportunity to determine whether the USMC's decision was justified." SCCB cited 20 U.S.C. 107(b)(2). However, that provision of the R-S Act (requiring Secretary of Education approval of a "limitation" on vending facilities) deals only with placement and operation of physical space as a vending facility. This provision of the R-S Act does not mention state licensing agencies or blind persons.
Neither SCCB nor the Decision and Award identified any provision of the R-S Act or implementing regulations that authorizes the Secretary of Education to determine whether elimination of an offer is justified. SCCB's complaint was just misguided.
PROBLEMS WITH LEGAL REFERENCES IN THE DECISION AND AWARD.
IS "BEST VALUE" AN UNLAWFUL LIMITATION? NO.
The Decision and Award asserts the USMC violated 34 CFR 395.30(b) when the USMC did not get permission from the Secretary of Education to use the term "best value" in the solicitation. This is not correct.
Section 395.30(b) applies to limitations on the placement or operation of a vending facility at a particular physical location due to a written determination by the federal agency responsible for that physical location that placing or operating a vending facility there would be adverse to the interests of the United States. The USMC made no such determination and, in fact, announced all of the cafeterias covered by the solicitation were available for operation by blind vendors if the state licensing agency submitted an offer that met the R-S standards quoted in the solicitation.
The USMC solicitation also defined "best value" in terms most favorable to SCCS: an offer (e.g., SCCB's) that was higher priced than others could be selected for the contract. SCCS simply needed to show it had the capability to perform the contract. SCCB failed to win the contract because of lack of capability to perform.
The Decision and Award misapplies Section 395.30(b) and displays a lack of understanding of federal Government contracting. The Federal Acquisition Regulation, 48 CFR, Chapter 1, Subpart 2.101, defines "best value" to mean, "the expected outcome of an acquisition that, in the Government's estimation, provides the greatest overall benefit in response to the requirement." In essence, by objecting to the use of the term "best value" as the goal of the USMC solicitation, the Decision and Award suggests that the R-S Act does NOT produce good value.
As pointed out in the Facts section of the Decision and Award, the USMC solicitation included language verbatim from the R-S regulations at 34 CFR 395.33(a) and 395.33(b), to enable the "best value" outcome. SCCS could have won this competition if SCCS had submitted an offer that met the minimum technical needs of the USMC and met the R-S standards quoted in the solicitation. SCCS didn't.
The R-S regulation at 34 CFR 395.33(b) requires the solicitation must state the evaluation criteria against which all offers (including SCCB's) will be judged, first, to determine whether each offerer is capable of performing and, second, to determine whether SCCS can perform the contract "at" the same high quality and reasonable price available from other providers of cafeteria services. This is just another way of expressing a "best value" standard in federal Government contracting.
In issuing 34 CFR 395.33(b), the Secretary of Education pre-approved the use of the "best value" standard. The Decision and Award mistakenly concluded "best value" is an unlawful "limitation" on blind vendor operation of a cafeteria.
IS "CONSULTATION" REQUIRED FOR EVERY DECISION? NO.
Also, because the USMC did not get approval from the Secretary of Education for the USMC's evaluation of SCCB's offer and the award without discussions, the Decision and Award for R-S/19-01 concluded the USMC failed to give SCCS "the R-S Act's priority to establish vending facilities on federal property as [allegedly] required by 34 C.F.R. 395.30(a) and 395.33(a) and (b)." This is a patent misinterpretation of what those sections say, as they are actually written.
When describing who gets the benefit of the R-S priority, the cited regulatory sections expressly refer to "blind licensees" and "operation by blind vendors." None of the cited regulatory sections mentions a "state licensing agency," other than an obligation in 34 CFR 395.33(b) to invite the state licensing agency to submit an offer in response to a solicitation. Inviting an offer is the only requirement imposed on a federal property managing agency with respect to how it treats a state licensing agency.
Section 395.30(a) prescribes that, "to the extent feasible," when making space allocation decisions while planning construction or renovation of a building, a federal agency is to give priority to setting aside empty physical space in which a blind vendor can install industrial equipment and operate a vending facility.
As a starting point, the USMC in fact announced that all of the cafeterias covered by the USMC contract were open for blind vendor operation. One hundred percent of these cafeterias were available for blind vendor operation.
The only reason the dining facilities affected by this contract are not being operated by a blind vendor is that the "best" offer SCCS and its proposal writing team were able to prepare failed to meet the requirements of the solicitation, the R-S Act, and its implementing regulations. The failure was by SCCB; not the USMC.
Be that as it may, there is no requirement for the USMC to consult the Secretary of Education with regard to a decision that it was "not feasible" to locate a vending facility in any particular space. Instead, 20 U.S.C. 107a(c) expressly mandates that state licensing agency selection of a vending facility for blind operation is subject to the "approval" of the federal agency that manages the property where the facility is located. Education must obtain USMC approval before anyone gets priority.
Section 395.30(a) has nothing to do with source selection. Even if it did cover source selection, there is no requirement for the USMC to consult the Secretary of Education with regard to the USMC's decision that it is "not feasible" to award to an offeror who submitted an offer that is unacceptable to the USMC.
Section 395.33(b) is the ONLY provision in the R-S Act or any of its implementing regulations that prescribes any relationship between a federal property managing agency and a state licensing agency with respect to competition for a cafeteria contract. This is the only section the USMC could have violated, if at all.
Federal agencies have only one obligation to the state licensing agency: invite the state licensing agency to submit an offer in response to a solicitation for a cafeteria contract. That is, it. SCCB was entitled to be invited; nothing more.
The state licensing agency (SCCB) is responsible for preparing and submitting an offer that demonstrates the ability of blind vendors to perform the contract requirements "at" the same quality and price comparable to that available from other providers of cafeteria services. The USMC was not responsible for SCCB's offer.
Section 395.33(b) requires all offers be judged against the same evaluation criteria. The state licensing agency gets no priority, extra time, extra credit, special treatment, assistance from the federal property managing agency, or any other advantage. When a solicitation sets a common cut-off date by which all offerors must submit their best and final offers, as the USMC solicitation did, opening up discussions with SCCB and allowing SCCB to submit a replacement (late) offer after that common cut-off date would violate the R-S requirement in Section 395.33(b). The Decision and Award did not comply with Section 395.33(b). The USMC did.
As will be discussed below, SCCB submitted an offer that demonstrated SCCB was not capable of performing the minimum requirements of the USMC contract. At the moment when the USMC rated SCCB's offer "Unacceptable" on the non-cost factors, SCCB's offer became ineligible to be considered for award. After that moment, the USMC made no further decisions "relative to" SCCB's offer.
Section 395.33(b) allows SCCB to request arbitration under 34 CFR 395.37 if SCCB is dissatisfied with any USMC act "relative to SCCB's offer"; not dissatisfaction with acts that are relative to any other offer. How the USMC rated and disposed of other offers is irrelevant to how it rated and dealt with SCCB's offer.
Section 395.33(b) prescribes only one other function the federal agency must perform: consult (inform) the Secretary of Education "if" (but only "if") the Contracting Officer has included the state licensing agency offer in a competitive range "and" ranked it among the offers from which the Contracting Officer would reasonably select one for the final contract award.
Nothing in Section 395.33(b) requires the federal agency to consult (inform) the Secretary of Education about the ratings assigned to the state licensing agency offer, establishment of a competitive range, who to include in the competitive range, or any other decision (including the decision to seek consultation).
The USMC was required to consult Education only "if" (and only after) the USMC identified SCCB's offer as one of the offers the Contracting Officer would select for the contract. In other words, because the Contracting Officer had not identified SCCB's offer as an offer within the group from which the Contracting Officer would select one to perform the contract, Section 395.33(b) prohibited the USMC from seeking consultation with Education. The Decision and Award got this backwards.
Why does Section 395.33(b) require the federal agency to consult Education after the federal agency has selected the state licensing agency for award, but not for any other decision? So the Secretary of Education can perform the functions assigned to the Secretary in Section 395.33(a) (discussed next).
Section 395.33(a) of 34 CFR prescribes functions the Secretary of Education (and only the Secretary) must perform. The Decision and Award mistakenly holds the USMC accountable to perform the Secretary's duties.
Section 395.33(b) (discussed above) prescribes the conditions for which, and the timing for when, the Secretary of Education expects federal property managing agencies to come forward with consultation (information).
Section 395.33(a) prescribes what the Secretary is to do AFTER a federal property managing agency has come forward. Section 395.33(a) engages only if the federal agency has selected the state offer under Section 395.33(b).
Under Section 395.33(a), while the contract award is pending, the Secretary of Education is to confirm whether blind vendors really will be capable of operating the cafeteria. It is not sufficient that the federal agency has tentatively selected the state licensing agency's offer under Section 395.33(b). The Secretary has a separate, subsequent responsibility to determine whether blind vendor operation can be provided at a reasonable cost and high quality comparable to the current (expiring) contract before the Secretary can establish priority for a blind vendor operation.
"Consultation" activates Education to establish a new "priority" for blind persons to obtain licenses from the state licensing agency to perform the new contract. No priority exists until the Secretary establishes it under Section 395.33(a). The federal agency has already selected the state licensing agency offer under Section 395.33(b), which barred giving the state priority. (See paragraph 5b(4)(c), above.)
Under 34 CFR 395.7, the state licensing agency (SCCB) must apply the new priority when considering whether to issue a new license to a blind person in need of employment or to someone else to perform the new contract once it is awarded. Blind persons get priority for licenses; states do not get priority for contracts.
In this pre-contract stage, while the award is still pending, Section 395.33(a) also requires the Secretary of Education must ensure the operation proposed by the state licensing agency offer will result in an operation that maximizes the employment of blind vendors. The obligation to evaluate and enforce the state's plan is on the Secretary of Education; not on the federal property managing agency.
The Decision and Award got these relationships tangled and backwards. The USMC has no direct relationship with blind persons, cannot give blind persons priority for licenses, and cannot maximize their employment. The USMC did not violate Section 395.33(a) when it rejected SCCB's unacceptable offer.
DOES THE COMPETITION IN CONTRACTING ACT (GICA) APPLY?	YES.
The Decision and Award (pages 16 - 18) theorizes (incorrectly) that the GICA and FAR do not apply to cafeteria contracts and Contracting Officers cannot be allowed to exercise discretion to decide whether competitive range is necessary or what price the agency will pay. The Decision and Award says the panel must "interpret" the law to promote new rules, rather than apply the law as it is, because discretion "eviscerates" use of perceived policy goals to transfer Defense funds to SCCB's team.
(2) Under the FAR, at 48 CFR, Chapter 1, Subchapter C, Subpart 15.306, the Contracting Officer may establish a competitive range when the Contracting Officer needs to engage in discussions. FAR 15.306(a)(3) and the GICA, at 10 U.S.C. 2305(b)(4)(A)(ii), allow the Contracting Officer to make award without holding discussions. The USMC solicitation that is the subject of this arbitration stated the USMC intended to make award without discussions based on initial offers. (FAR provision 52.215-1(f)(4) incorporated into the USMC solicitation on SCCS 0155.)
Citing to Automated Comm. Syst. V. U.S., 49 Fed. Cl. 570, 577-78
(2001), NISH v. Rumsfeld, 348 F.3d 1263 (10th Cir. 2003) and NISH v. Cohen, 247 F.3d 197 (4th Cir. 2001), the Decision and Award says the GICA and FAR do not apply to a cafeteria contract "to the extent they conflict" with the R-S Act or its regulations.
So, where is the conflict? The conflict is between: stories arbitration panels have told, and the rules Congress and the Secretary of Education actually wrote.
The R-S Act requires the Secretary of Education to issue regulations. The rule-making authority of the Federal Acquisition Regulatory (FAR) Council under the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (OFPP) Act prohibits any procurement regulation, rule or procedure from taking effect until it published in the Federal Register. The R-S Act prohibits any rule or interpretation that is not published as a regulation after consultation with all the federal agencies that manage federal property. Rule-making is, essentially, the same. Thus, the GICA and the FAR do not conflict with the R-S Act.
Do the GICA or FAR conflict with the regulations that implement the R-S Act? No. The R-S regulations issued by the Secretary of Education contain only two NEW procedures IN ADDITION TO (not in conflict with) the GICA and FAR:
In the pre-solicitation phase, a federal procuring agency shall invite the state licensing agency to submit an offer in response to a solicitation for a cafeteria contract; and
In the post-selection phase, the federal procuring agency shall consult (inform) the Secretary of Education if (and only if) the federal agency has judged the offer submitted by the state licensing agency to be within a competitive range and has ranked the state licensing agency offer among those proposals that have a reasonable chance of being selected by the Contracting Officer. (34 CFR 395.33(b).)
Under the CICA and FAR, a Contracting Officer would not normally reach out to a state government to remind them to submit an offer. Also, under the CICA and FAR, after a Contracting Officer has decided to select a state government's offer, the Contracting Officer would not normally reach out to the Secretary of another federal agency for review of whether the state government's program or its offer complies with a particular statute (e.g., the R-S Act) and implementing regulations issued by that other federal agency. These two requirements in the R-S regulations are not prohibited by the CICA or FAR. They add to the CICA and FAR, without conflict.
But. There is a notable, significant gap in the R-S regulations between the pre-solicitation obligation of a federal agency to invite the state licensing agency to submit an offer and the post-selection obligation to inform the Department of Education that the federal Contracting Officer would like to select the state licensing agency to perform the contract. The Contracting Officer is to inform the Secretary of Education when the contract award to the state licensing agency is pending. Why? So the Secretary of Education can perform the unique role assigned to the Secretary in 34 CFR 395.33(a), to establish a new priority that will require state licensing agencies to give preference to blind persons when considering applicants for licenses to perform the new contract. (See paragraph 5b(5), above; 34 CFR 395.33(a); 34 CFR 395.7.)
The R-S Act and its regulations are silent on what happens in that gap between: inviting an offer, and grouping the offers that have a reasonable chance of being selected. The R-S Act and its regulations are silent on the evaluation of offers, competitive range, award without discussions, rejection of offers, and other contracting matters. The CICA and FAR govern the evaluation of offers, competitive range, meaningfulness of discussions, award without discussions, and a myriad of other contracting decisions about which the R-S Act and its regulations are silent.
There is no conflict between the silence of the R-S Act and its regulations on the one hand and the comprehensive voice of the CICA and FAR on the other hand. Because there is no conflict between the voice of the CICA and FAR and the silence of the R-S Act and its regulations, the CICA and FAR do apply, fully.
SUMMARY. The Decision and Award identified not one section of the R-S Act or its implementing regulations that says the USMC was required to give SCCS special treatment or priority over commercial offerers in competition for a contract and not one section that required consultation when rejecting SCCB's unacceptable offer.
On the one hand, the USMC did absolutely everything that the R-S Act and its implementing regulations require. The USMC did not violate the R-S Act or regulation.
On the other hand, SCCB's offer did not comply with the R-S Act or its regulations. SCCB's offer would create employment for not one blind person in need of employment. (See paragraph 20, below.) SCCS and its team failed to write an acceptable proposal. SCCB's offer and the testimony at the hearing demonstrated SCCS was not capable of performing the contract or making its offer acceptable through discussions.

SCCB's "HANDBOOK" DOES NOT REQUIRE DISCUSSIONS.
Among the exhibits for the hearing, SCCB submitted a document referred to as "The Handbook." (SC First Amended Exhibit List.) On its cover page, the document was titled "Administration of the Randolph-Sheppard Vending Program by Federal Property Managing Agencies." (SC Ex 9, SCCB 0769 - 0818.)
Nothing in SCCB's Handbook requires the USMC Contracting Officer to include SCCB's in a competitive range or open discussions with SCCB.

SCCB and its counsel failed to lay any foundation that could lead to confidence that SCCB's Handbook is credible or persuasive.
Use of SCCB's "Handbook" by an arbitration panel or a federal property managing agency would violate the R-S Act because this document was not published as a regulation by the Secretary of Education as required by the R-S Act at 20 U.S.C.
107(b) and 107d-3(e). SCCB's Handbook has no legal merit.
SCCB did not provide a Federal Register Notice, a citation showing codification in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), or any other documentation to support a conclusion that SCCB's Handbook was published as a regulation by the Secretary of Education as required by the R-S Act at 20 U.S.C. 107(b) and 107d-3(e).
Assuming SCCB's Handbook was a document retired in January 2017 by Policy Directive RSA-PD-17-01 (SCCB 0863), the Handbook could not possibly have been applied by the USMC to a solicitation that was not issued until October 2017.
Despite all the above evidence that SCCB's Handbook has no credible or persuasive value for this arbitration, assume (for the sake of argument) that SCCB's Handbook stated some useful guidance.
The Handbook suggested only an expectation that, after evaluation of offers, federal property managing agencies will establish a competitive range to include those proposals that demonstrate the respective offerers can provide the required cafeteria services and have a reasonable chance of being selected for award. (SCCB 0807; excerpt at SCCB 0820.)
The Handbook said, considering the clear intent of the R-S Act, the determining factor in whether a state licensing agency's offer is to be included in the competitive range is the "likelihood" that the bid may be made acceptable through discussions. (Id.) The offer's chances must be more than "possible." It must be "likely."
The Handbook goes on to state "this should be interpreted to mean" the Contracting Officer will include the state licensing agency's offer in the competitive range if, in the Contracting Officer's judgment, the state licensing agency would be able to make clarifications, modifications or minor revisions that would make the state licensing agency's offer fully acceptable. (Id.) Unacceptable deficiencies are not minor.
Like 34 CFR 395.33(b), the Handbook states the Contracting Officer will apply the same evaluation criteria, standards and exceptions that apply when assessing whether to include a commercial proposal in the competitive range. (Id.)
According to the excerpt from the Handbook, a proposal will not be considered in the competitive range if is technically inferior or contains unduly high selling prices to patrons such that, in the Contracting Officer's judgment, the possibility of being made acceptable through meaningful negotiations is precluded. (Id.)
At the hearing and in its briefs, SCCB argued the panel should apply the pre- 1995 FAR Part 15 rule about competitive range ("when in doubt, leave them in") versus the post-1995 FAR Part 15 rule ("when in doubt, leave them out"). Clearly, even SCCB understands this is a dispute under the FAR, not the R-S Act.
Using the pre-1995 FAR Part 15 rule "when in doubt, leave them in" does not help SCCS. There is no doubt, SCCB's offer was unacceptable and unfixable. (See paragraphs 12 - 17, above.) There is no doubt; SCCB's offer was not competitive.
The Decision and Award warbled, "... because of the MC's non-compliance with reasonable discovery requests, ... we decline to address ..." whether the Handbook could be manipulated to "require" the USMC to put SCCB's offer into a competitive range when SCCB's offer was so unsuitable. (Decision and Award, bottom of page 18.)
Applying all of the above evidence and giving SCCB's Handbook the most favorable interpretation possible, the USMC Contracting Officer was under no obligation to include SCCB's offer in a competitive range.
SCCB's offer was so technically inferior compared to the solicitation requirements that it was rated "Unacceptable," and its price was so unduly high, that there was no possibility or likelihood of being made acceptable through discussions.
As explained above, to fix the deficiencies in the Technical Approach and weaknesses in the Management Approach, SCCS would have had to increase the price of its already overpriced offer, and the increased price would have made SCCB's Socio-Economic Plan even more unacceptable. (See paragraphs 12 - 14, above.) SCCB's offer was in checkmate; not competitive range.

QUESTIONS ABOUT SCCB's COMPLIANCE WITH THE R-S ACT.
If consultation had been held, the Secretary would be required under 34 CFR 395.33(a) and 20 U.S.C. 107(b)(1) to ensure SCCB's proposed team meets the R-S Act policy goals for blind persons in need of employment as stated in 20 U.S.C. 107(a).
In 20 U.S.C. 10?(a), the R-S Act policy goals are to provide remunerative employment and new economic opportunities to blind persons in need of employment and stimulate the blind to greater efforts in striving to become self-supporting.
The R-S regulations require state licensing agencies such as SCCB give preference to "blind persons who are in need of employment" when the state is issuing licenses to operate a vending facility or cafeteria. (34 CFR 395.?(a).)
After a federal Contracting Officer has selected a state licensing agency offer for a new contract, the R-S implementing regulations require the Secretary of Education to establish priority for blind persons to obtain the new licenses to operate the cafeterias under the new contract and ensure the state licensing agency provides maximum employment for blind vendors on the new contract. (34 CFR 395.33(a).)
SCCB's offer appears to fail the R-S goals or requirements.
The five blind vendors on SCCB's "team" identified in SCCB's Management Plan already have employment. (SCCB 0506 - 0508.) They already operate vending facilities under licenses through their state R-S programs.
The offer does not mention any plan to employ or license any blind person who needs employment in any of the states covered by the USMC contract.
If SCCB were to win the USMC contract and, thus, create a new opportunity to issue new licenses for operation of cafeterias on the new contract, no blind person in need of employment would be given preference over the vendors who are already licensed and employed. SCCB and the Virginia and North Carolina state licensing agencies would give the new licenses to vendors who already have licenses and employment to operate existing vending facilities not covered by the new contract.
SCCB's proposal does not give an opportunity for even one more blind person to become employed, licensed or self-supporting.
COMPETITIVE RANGE IS NOT REQUIRED BY R-S ACT.
Mr. James admitted a competitive range is not necessary in every case. (TR 40:8-13.)	He testified a state licensing agency who submits an acceptable offer at a reasonable price can win contract award without discussions. (TR 40:8-13.)
The uncontroverted facts provided in Mr. James' testimony are that a competitive range and discussions are not necessary or required in every case, and state licensing agencies can win cafeteria contracts without discussions if their offers are technically acceptable and reasonably priced. (Id.) SCCB's offer wasn't.
THE TRUE FACTS ABOUT THE ADVERSE INFERENCES
For the reasons stated in the order dated March 16, 2022, the panel decided to draw certain Adverse Inferences in reaction to the USMC's stance on discovery of extraneous, privileged information (e.g., evaluation of other offers) that was genuinely, most emphatically, not relevant to any legitimate issue in this arbitration. The R-S regulation at 34 CFR 395.33(b) required the panel to judge SCCB's offer against the evaluation criteria in the solicitation to see if SCCB is capable of performing. Only if SCCB had capability to perform would it then be ranked against other offers. Because other offers were irrelevant, the Adverse Inferences turned out to be irrelevant, too.
At the time of filing its request for arbitration, SCCB possessed the following documents that are relevant, probative and dispositive for this arbitration:
The USMC solicitation (SCCB 0008 - 0166);
SCCB's offer in response to the solicitation (SCCB 0167 - 0707);
The Contracting Officer's debriefing letter to SCCB dated July 17, 2018 (SCCB 0708 - 0711);
The Contracting Officer's enhanced debriefing letter to SCCB dated July 25, 2018, answering SCCB's questions (SCCB 0712 - 0715); and
The Source Selection Evaluation Board (SSEB) report. (SCCB 0716 - 0768).
The SSEB reported citations to SCCB's offer and the solicitation, narrative explanation and ratings regarding evaluation of SCCB's Technical Approach, Management Approach, Socio-economic Plan and Past Performance.
The SSEB report was redacted to protect privileged information about the evaluation of the non-cost factors relative to competing offers.
During pre-hearing discovery, SCCB requested copies of all the other offers, an unredacted version of the SSEB report showing how the SSEB rated other offers, pre­ solicitation documents containing non-public information that would provide unfair advantage in future competitions, and all contract modifications since its award in 2018.
The USMC objected to the discovery request primarily because the requested documents are not relevant to the only legitimate Question under the R-S Act: specifically, whether a federal Contracting Officer can reject an unacceptable offer from a state government without discussions and without getting permission from the Secretary of Education. The Answer, of course, is, "Yes, as a matter of law, the R-S Act and its implementing regulations do not require discussions, or permission, in order to reject an unacceptable offer from a state licensing agency." The R-S Act gives priority to blind persons versus non-blind persons when a state issues vending licenses. The R-S Act gives no priority to a state government with respect to commercial competitors.
The USMC also objected to the panel's protective order and disclosure of proprietary information of other offerors without their knowledge and approval.
Before the hearing, without reviewing available evidence and without waiting to observe what might be presented at the hearing, the arbitration panel decided to draw the following ADVERSE INFERENCES against Respondent USMC:
SCCB's bid was scored differently [i.e., got lower ratings on different contents and different plans] than the winning bidder's proposal, to SCCB's detriment; and
No bid was superior to SCCB's bid [that was "Unacceptable" to the USMC].
The ADVERSE INFERENCES do not change the contents of SCCB's offer or the reality the Contracting Officer reported in the debriefing letter at SCCS 0708-0711.
The Department of Education convened this arbitration panel to review the Contracting Officer's July 2018 decision and the acts of the USMC that actually occurred "relative to" SCCB's offer (and no other offer) from early January to the end of July 2018. (See SCCS 0001 - 0007; 20 U.S.C. 107d-2; 34 CFR 395.33(b) and 395.37.)
The July 17, 2018, debriefing letter (SCCS 0708 - 0711) recorded the prices and ratings the Contracting Officer actually used in making the decision about which SCCB requested arbitration. The debriefing letter was the key document the panel was invested to review for violation of the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
SCCB's proposed $1.2 Billion price is shown in Volume I of SCCB's offer. (SCCS 0174 - 0262.) The debriefing letter shows the same price. (SCCS 0711.)
The panel drew adverse inferences about the redacted SSEB evaluation of the non-cost portions of offers other than SCCB's. The SSEB did not evaluate the cost or price of any of the offers. Price analysts and auditors will evaluate cost proposals only for offers that the SSEB has rated as acceptable or better on non-cost factors. The solicitation stated that if any non-cost factor is rated Unacceptable, the offer is ineligible for award. (SCCS 0165.) The inferences do not reduce SCCB's unduly high price.
Inferring other offers were worse than SCCB's and got lower ratings on the non-cost factors does not make SCCB's high price any less unreasonable.
The ADVERSE INFERENCES do not fix SCCB's offer or make it acceptable. They do not change the solicitation requirements, evaluation criteria, or basis for award.
The solicitation quoted verbatim exactly the same qualifying standards stated in R-S regulations at 34 CFR 395.33(a) and 395.33(b).
According to the solicitation, SCCB could have won the contract if its offer had demonstrated SCCB is capable of operating the USMC cafeterias with food of high quality at a reasonable price. SCCB's offer failed to meet the R-S requirements.
SCCB's offer earned the ratings it got and was not susceptible to being made acceptable through discussions. (See paragraphs 12 - 17, above.) The adverse inferences do not improve the contents of SCCB's offer, reduce its price, or change its ratings. Drawing the adverse inferences cannot make SCCB capable of performing the contract or capable of making its offer acceptable. SCCB's offer proved, it wasn't.
The adverse inferences do not move SCCB's offer up so that it becomes an offer that has a chance of being included in a competitive range or a chance the USMC would select it for a contract. SCCB's offer remains unacceptable to the USMC.
Drawing the adverse inferences imagines the redacted portion of the SSEB report says the other offers were inferior to SCCB's and received Unacceptable ratings on the non-cost factors. If true, SCCB's unacceptable offer is inferred to be the "best" among a group of unacceptable offers the USMC had a right to reject. The solicitation said the USMC could reject any or all of the offers. (See paragraph 9, above.)
The ADVERSE INFERENCES convert this arbitration into a question about the Contracting Officer's judgment under the FAR and GICA.
The inferences do not improve SCCB's offer. The inferences do not erase the material failures, deficiencies, poor planning, and lack of capability SCCB's offer demonstrated when measured against the minimum performance requirements.
If it had been true that all offers were unacceptable, as the adverse inferences portray, the Contracting Officer has sole discretion to decide whether to do any of the following: amend the solicitation; cancel the solicitation; reopen the solicitation and seek additional offers; open discussions only with the additional offerors; open discussions with some or all of the initial offerors; reject some or all of the initial offerors; issue a new solicitation; or take other actions under the GICA and FAR.
Dealing with unacceptable offers is not a question under the R-S Act or its regulations. The arbitration panel and Education are in no position to guess, recommend or direct what a Contracting Officer should do with unacceptable offers.
The depth, breadth and richness of the detailed facts recounted and analyzed in this Dissent illuminate the documents available to the panel. They were more than sufficient to resolve this case. The other offers were irrelevant. SCCB's offer earned low ratings all on its own. The Administrative Procedures Act (5 U.S.C. 556), incorporated into the R-S arbitration rules at 20 U.S.C. 10?d-2, obligated the panel to exclude irrelevant, immaterial, non-probative information such as ratings of other offers.
The arbitration panel drew adverse inferences that do not improve SCCB's offer or its case. Even inferring all offers were unacceptable, the Contracting Officer did not have to establish a competitive range, hold discussions, or consult Education.
Section 395.33(b) of 34 CFR is the only section in law that prescribes obligations for a federal agency with respect to a state licensing agency offer in a competition for a cafeteria contract. The arbitration panel did not need any information or inferences about the other offers in order to answer the R-S questions, as follows:
"Does 34 CFR 395.33(b) require the USMC to consult with the Secretary of Education if SCCB's offer is not included in a competitive range? No."
"Does 34 CFR 395.33(b) require the USMC to consult with the Secretary of Education if the USMC has not ranked SCCB's offer among the group from which the Contracting Officer would select an awardee for the contract? No."
CAN EDUCATION MAKE DEFENSE CONTRACTING DECISIONS?	NO.
Under the military Pay and Benefits statute at 37 U.S.C. 402, the military Secretaries have exclusive legal jurisdiction over policy, funding and operation of military dining facilities. Under FAR Part 15, the Contracting Officer determines what is a fair and reasonable price, given the agency budget. The Secretary of Education cannot make contracting decisions for the USMC Commandant or military department Secretary.
Defense appropriations can be expended only for the purpose for which they were appropriated and cannot be guaranteed or obligated in advance of appropriations for future priority award to a state government or state licensing agency. (31 U.S.C. 1301.)
All offers must be evaluated by the same criteria that will produce the "best value" result. (34 CFR 395.33(b); 10 U.S.C. 2305.) The Contracting Officer must judge all the offers against the same evaluation criteria and ensure the state licensing agency offer is "at" (not above) a price that is comparable to that available from other providers of cafeteria services. (34 CFR 395.33(b).)  "At" means price must be equal, not higher.
Under 20 U.S.C. 10?a(c), a state licensing agency cannot select a vending facility for blind operation without approval of the federal agency responsible for the property. Under 34 CFR 395.33(b), the USMC had no duty to consult Education unless the USMC was convinced SCCB had the capability to perform the contract to the USMC's satisfaction.
Under 34 CFR 395.33(a), Education was authorized only to establish a new priority for blind persons that SCCB would have to apply under 34 CFR 395.7. The Decision and Award would obliterate the USMC's judgment about what the USMC needs, and replace it with the Secretary of Education's judgment about what the USMC has to accept.
The Secretary of Education is not a Defense procurement official.	The arbitration panel for R-S/19-01 had no authority to anoint the Secretary to act as one.
APPLICABLE LAW REGARDING REMEDY
Under the R-S arbitration rules (20 U.S.C. 107d-2(b)(2) and 34 CFR 395.37(d)), the head of the affected federal agency has exclusive jurisdiction to determine what actions (if any) are necessary to carry out the arbitration panel's decision and terminate acts or practices (if any) that violate the R-S Act or its implementing regulations.
This arbitration panel was convened to review the decision reported in the Contracting Officer's July 17, 2018, debriefing letter and acts of the USMC relative to SCCB's offer (and no other offer). (See SCCB 0001 - 0007; 20 U.S.C. 107d-2; 34 CFR 395.33(b) and 395.37.) The adverse inferences (and the other offers) were irrelevant.
Under the R-S arbitration rules, the arbitration panel's role is limited to determining whether the acts of the USMC that actually occurred in 2018 (not inferences made up in 2022) violated a provision that is actually written in the R-S Act or its implementing regulation. The panel is not authorized to issue an "award" to SCCB. The panel is not authorized to recommend the USMC terminate and resolicit a contract.
Even if one draws adverse inferences that fictionally imagine all offers were worse than SCCB's offer, the R-S Act does not tell a Contracting Officer what to do with unacceptable offers. Only the GICA and FAR inform the Contracting Officer. Under the R-S arbitration rules, the arbitration panel has jurisdiction only to look for violation of provisions expressly stated in the R-S Act or its implementing regulations. Neither the Secretary of Education nor the R-S panel has jurisdiction to decide there should be a competitive range, which offer(s) should be in it, or who should win the competition.
Under the R-S Act arbitration rules, the USMC Commandant is the single, one and only official in the federal Government charged with authority and responsibility to determine the TRUE facts for the USMC and terminate USMC acts or practices (if any) that actually violated the R-S Act in 2018. There were none, real or inferred.
CONCLUSIONS
Since the Decision and Award is based on inferred facts relative to other offers, not SCCB's offer, there are no acts or practices the USMC Commandant can terminate. No USMC acts or practices ever existed that violate the R-S Act. None exist today.
There are no laws, facts or inferences that justify award for SCCS. The R-S Act does not guarantee SCCS a contract. R-S priority rights belong to blind persons, not state governments. The USMC used the "best value" and "consultation" criteria required by the R-S Act and regulations. Consultation would have been required only if the USMC had selected SCCS for the contract. Consultation would have triggered the Secretary of Education to create new priority rights for blind persons that SCCB would have to honor if the USMC awarded SCCS the contract. The Secretary of Education has no authority to make decisions about USMC contracts or budgets. The USMC did not violate the R-S Act or its regulations. SCCB did not meet its burden of proof.
Properly applying the R-S Act and its implementing regulations exactly as written to the extensive record available for review of the decisions the Contracting Officer and SSEB reported in 2018, one can only conclude the USMC fully complied with the law. Even after drawing the adverse inferences ordered by the arbitration panel, the USMC was not required to consult Education about contracting decisions internal to the USMC. Because SCCS failed to demonstrate SCCB had the capability to perform the contract, neither blind persons nor state governments have any priority right to the USMC contract. Because SCCS failed to demonstrate capability to perform the contract, the USMC lawfully rejected SCCB's offer without discussions and without informing Education. Because the USMC had not ranked SCCB's offer as an offer the USMC would select to perform the contract, Education had no role to play. Education and the arbitration panel should have dismissed SCCB's complaint early on.
Because the Decision and Award for R-S/19-01 misinterpreted the R-S Act and its regulations and made up a new rule that says only the Secretary of Education can make final contracting decisions for the USMC, the Decision and Award must be terminated as an act or practice that violates the R-S Act and implementing regulations.
The Secretary of Education must reject the Decision and Award and deny SCCB's complaint. SCCB requested arbitration to compel consultation. Arbitration produced all the documents the USMC would provide for consultation. Using the record as though for consultation, the Secretary of Education must find SCCS lacked capability to provide quality at a reasonable price, and SCCB's offer failed to meet the R-S standards.
The Commandant of the USMC (or appropriate delegatee), having access to SCCB's offer and complaint, the solicitation, the USMC's ratings of SCCB's offer, the transcript, briefs, Decision and Award, and this Dissent, can affirm, in writing, that there are NO acts or practices within the USMC that need to be terminated in reaction to the Decision and Award for R-S/19-01 and no other actions to be taken by the USMC to carry out the decision of the arbitration panel, because the USMC did NOT violate the R-S Act or its implementing regulations when the Contracting Officer rejected SCCB's technically unacceptable, unfixable, astronomically overpriced $1.2 Billion offer.
