SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic on-site 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to 
section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In Federal fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State 
Vocational Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment 
Services program (Supported Employment program) administered by Michigan Rehabilitation 
Services (MRS). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the process by which RSA 
carried out its activities from September 10, 2024, through September 12, 2024, was defined by 
information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, documents, and 
data submitted by MRS, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, and technical 
assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
• Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities;   
• Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

5 
 
achieve continuous improvement in assisting individuals with disabilities to achieve 
quality employment outcomes; 
• Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities, 
including students and youth with disabilities; 
• Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported 
Employment programs and recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the 
VR agency’s financial management of fiscal resources and strategies for maximizing the 
use of Federal grant funds;  
• Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and 
procedures, including the development or revision of policies and internal controls 
supporting effective and efficient program operations, service delivery, and data-based 
decision making; and  
• Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic 
actions to improve and maximize program and fiscal performance and the use of Federal 
funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED 
EMPLOYMENT SERVICES PROGRAMS  
A. PURPOSE 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops 
strategies to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds to 
achieve quality employment outcomes.  
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE
  
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by MRS on the Case Service Report (RSA-911) 
and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 2021, 
and 2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and requested 
documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR services, 
quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as discussions 
with MRS of potential factors influencing program performance informed the analysis and 
helped to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the performance 
and management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from 
related sources, and discussions with MRS during the review, RSA identified various factors, 
which may be contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s performance 
leading to employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of 
Federal funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to 
MRS’ requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by the VR agency as it 
develops strategic actions to ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to 
maximize the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with 
disabilities in achieving quality employment outcomes. 
Decline in Postsecondary Education and Training Services 
 
The number and percentages of participants who have received graduate degree, bachelor’s 
degree, junior or community college, or occupational or vocational training declined over the

7 
 
period reviewed and were below the national average. For PY 2022, MRS reported 383 
participants received postsecondary education (24 or 0.1 percent at the graduate level, 239 or 1.1 
percent at the bachelor’s level, and 120 or 0.6 percent at the junior or community college level), 
representing 1.7 percent of all participants who received services (a decline from 2.1 percent 
during PY 2020). Additionally, 954 participants (4.4 percent) received occupational or 
vocational training services representing a decline from 1,070 participants (5.5 percent) that 
received these services in PY 2020.   
The number and percentage of participants who received training services in coordination with 
employers either decreased or were not reported from PY 2020 to PY 2022. Specifically, on-the-
job training (OJT) declined from 231 participants (1.2 percent) to 116 participants (0.5 percent), 
apprenticeship training dropped from 32 participants (0.2 percent) to 2 participants, and no 
participants were reported for customized training services during this period. Customized 
employment services increased from 60 participants (0.3 percent) to 130 participants (0.6 
percent) from PY 2020 to PY 2021 before dropping down to 54 participants (0.2 percent) for PY 
2022. One exception to the general decline was the number of participants receiving job 
readiness training, which increased by 81 percent, from 1,371 participants (6.7 percent) to 2,488 
participants (11.4 percent).  
  
MRS acknowledged the recent decline in participants receiving postsecondary education and 
training services, stating that the agency is re-evaluating and modifying its staff’s approach in the 
provision of services to maximize training opportunities for participants throughout the VR 
process. MRS recognized that its staff, including managers, may be using an outdated approach 
in planning VR services and inadvertently limiting opportunities for eligible program 
participants. RSA noted that MRS policies appear to significantly limit necessary support for 
participants to pursue higher education or training. For example, MRS’ policy for postsecondary 
education services, RSM 6625 Training – College and Vocational, appears to limit options for 
participants by requiring individuals to receive “the least-cost” option available when selecting 
education and training programs. Further, MRS’ policy caps education or training costs at $5,000 
per academic year, which includes, in addition to tuition, all related costs such as books and 
supplies and room and board. MRS permits policy exceptions due to disability-related needs or 
when the individual is a recipient of Social Security benefits. Due to the policy’s requirement for 
the least-costly option, participants are often required to attend community or junior college for 
the first two years before proceeding to a four-year university.  
 
Consistent with the decline in the number of participants receiving postsecondary education and 
training, MRS also experienced a decline in reported expenditures for these services with the 
exception of job readiness training. In PY 2022, MRS reported expending $1,863,496 or 7.2 
percent of all expenditures reported on its RSA-911 report for graduate, bachelor’s, community 
college and vocational/occupational training programs, down from $2,058,602 or 8.0 percent for 
the same programs in PY 2020. In comparison, the national average expenditure for the same 
education and training programs was 24.8 percent during PY 2022, or three times higher when 
compared to MRS. At the same time, MRS reported expending $1,194,212 (4.7 percent) for job 
readiness training, only $20,676 below what MRS expended for all college and university 
education combined for PY 2022. Increasing expenditures to support participants in

8 
 
postsecondary education and training may help to reverse the declining trends and maximize 
employment opportunities for individuals with disabilities.   
  
Despite decreasing trends in the expenditures, numbers, and percentages of participants receiving 
postsecondary education, MRS remained one of the nation’s highest performers for four of the 
five WIOA performance indicators for PY 2021 and PY 2022. In PY 2022, MRS was in the first 
quartile for employment during the second quarter after exit (second at 71 percent), median 
earnings during the second quarter after exit (first in the nation at $8,885), employment during 
the fourth quarter after exit (second nationally at 68 percent) and credential attainment (tenth 
nationally at 50.9 percent). Measurable skill gains (MSG) rate was the only performance 
indicator in which MRS did not rank in the top quartile (fifteenth nationally at 56.3 percent). 
However, RSA identified coding errors in MRS reporting that may have inflated the performance 
results, which are detailed later in this report. Historically, postsecondary education has been 
associated with improved employment outcomes. As such, the seemingly inverse correlation 
between declining postsecondary education services and the agency’s performance on the 
indicators may be an area for further evaluation.  
  
Opportunities for job exploration and work-based learning experiences can help students and 
participants identify career interests and investigate and engage in postsecondary education or 
vocational training that will assist them in achieving their employment goals. During the review 
period, the number of on-the-job trainings (OJT) declined from 231 (1.2 percent) to 116 (0.5 
percent), apprenticeships declined from 32 (0.2 percent) to 2 and no customized training services 
were reported. Collaboratively working with employers to increase opportunities for work-based 
learning experiences and other work training opportunities can assist VR participants in 
identifying and acquiring needed skills that set the stage for future employment success. MRS 
identified that its prior practice of foregoing work experiences or exposure before training or 
educating individuals in a field of interest generally proved less successful in generating job 
satisfaction and retention and warranted change.  
 
Technical Assistance  
 
• MRS desires to enhance access to postsecondary education opportunities for students 
with disabilities and identified this population as a priority for the agency. RSA stressed 
the importance of encouraging high school students with disabilities to apply for VR 
services at the earliest point and before they graduate, stressing that early engagement is 
crucial for ensuring these students receive the support they need to transition into 
postsecondary education and the workforce successfully. RSA and MRS explored various 
training that could improve the skill sets and employability of students with disabilities. 
• MRS often develops IPEs for students still exploring career opportunities with the 
employment outcome “career pathways” that includes a description of the assessments 
and pre-employment transition services planned before graduation. RSA clarified that 
IPEs cannot specify “career pathway” as the vocational goal and must include a general 
field of employment. Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.46(a)(2)(ii), when developing a plan 
for an eligible student or youth with a disability, the plan should contain the transition 
services and supports needed to achieve an employment outcome or a “projected post-
school employment outcome” for the student. Although the employment field can be

9 
 
more generic or generalized to a cluster field, the field of employment is required and 
may be amended at any point necessary prior to the time the student graduates from 
school. 
• MRS discussed the governor's newly launched initiative that offers free community 
college education to qualified participants, citing concerns that the initiative might 
prevent students with disabilities from applying for or continuing VR services, given this 
new initiative and source of financial support. RSA suggested that the VR agency 
promote early engagement and inform students with information about available services 
that can provide additional support to meet their needs such as work-based learning 
experiences and other pre-employment transition services that prepare them as they 
transition to postsecondary education and employment. In addition, MRS could discuss 
the availability of job search and placement services, as well as support services that may 
be needed based on the individual’s disability and unique needs.  
• RSA discussed potential strategies that MRS could employ to enhance postsecondary 
education and training opportunities and services for participants. MRS proactively began 
reviewing its policies prior to the on-site portion of the review to identify financial 
restrictions that unnecessarily inhibit access or present barriers to college and university 
training opportunities for participants.
 
 
Recommendations  
  
• MRS should explore barriers that may limit its participants from participating in training 
and educational programs, including its policy for college and vocational training 
programs (RSM 6625). RSA acknowledges that MRS submitted administrative changes 
related to the $5,000 limit for college and university training, and it continues to work 
through that process. 
• MRS expressed its strong partnership with Michigan State University and should explore 
replicating such partnerships with other colleges and universities. 
• MRS should assess the training needs of its staff to ensure they have the necessary skills 
and knowledge to assist participants in accessing postsecondary education. Relevant 
training may include career counseling, understanding educational systems, financial aid 
processes, and labor market analysis. 
• MRS should develop or update policies and procedures that specifically support 
participants in postsecondary education. This includes defining application processes, 
support services, and follow-up protocols.  
• MRS should review its current budget and financial planning to ensure that funds are 
allocated appropriately to enhance support services for postsecondary education.  
• MRS should establish goals and offer benchmarks for staff related to promoting 
postsecondary education opportunities as part of the agency's broader strategic plan, 
ensuring they align with overall mission and objectives.  
• MRS should evaluate how it allocates its funds to allow for further support for its 
participants to receive postsecondary education and training in the community using 
public and private entities.

Operations and Cost-Effectiveness of Michigan Career and Technical Institute  
MRS maintains a state-operated comprehensive rehabilitation center that administers 12 
vocational training programs, different assessment and evaluation services, residential services, 
vocational counseling, and other support services. The Michigan Career and Technical Institute 
(MCTI) was built in 1944 to assist returning wounded soldiers as World War II ended.   
During the COVID-19 pandemic, MCTI experienced reductions in the availability of services 
when public restrictions were implemented. MRS partially opened MCTI to allow for some 
services with other portions of training and assessment conducted virtually by MCTI staff. Upon 
resuming full functioning by PY 2022, MCTI did not attain the same level of participants as 
before the pandemic. From PY 2020 to PY 2022, the number of individuals served at MCTI 
declined from 765 to 500 individuals, with the most noticeable decline in individuals receiving 
career assessment services (decreasing from 327 to 122 individuals). Career and technical 
education programs decreased from serving 420 to 377 individuals during the same period. 
Despite the declines experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic, MRS anticipates increased 
participation in MCTI in the years ahead. RSA discussed MCTI with MRS VR counselors, who 
generally spoke highly of MCTI and its training programs but did not believe the pandemic was 
responsible for the decline in participants attending MCTI. Rather, they reported that delays in 
having participants evaluated at the facility and the established high standards set for participants 
by most programs at MCTI were barriers to participation. Multiple VR counselors described 
frustration and disappointment associated with waiting for several months for an assessment only 
for the individual to be told they did not qualify academically. VR counselors also noted similar 
available public and private programs offered by providers, including community colleges, did 
not have similar rigid academic standards. Additionally, some VR counselors indicated MCTI’s 
location limited participants to mostly younger individuals able to stay on campus or able to 
commute each day. VR counselors also noted that prolonged delays to begin training led 
participants to pursue training elsewhere.  
Although the number of individuals who received services at MCTI declined over the three-year 
period reviewed, the facility’s budget remained fairly consistent, ranging from $12 to $13 
million annually due to the ongoing operation costs, primarily salary and wage costs. MRS has 
invested additional funds in MCTI to establish or improve areas or programs operated at the 
facility, including its residential area and recreational areas and equipment. For FFYs 2021 and 
2022, MRS reported expending $1,778,503 and $2,671,769, respectively, toward establishment 
projects primarily for MCTI. MRS has also identified two additional large-scale projects that 
include renovating the facility due to its aging infrastructure and a second project that would add 
a new wing to MCTI. The two projects were estimated to cost approximately $50 million. RSA 
reviewed these projects and determined that establishment authority could not be used for either 
since both projects’ estimated cost exceed the value of MCTI, appraised at $14.6 million in 2023 
and VR agencies are limited under the establishment authority to costs that do not exceed the 
appraised value of the existing building (34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(17)(ii)). MRS may proceed with 
the two projects using the definition of construction that allows for the expansion, remodeling, 
alteration, or renovation of existing buildings (34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(10)(iv)), but it cannot exceed 
10 percent of its Federal award (Section 101(a)(17)(A) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. §

11 
 
361.61(c)(17)(ii)). MRS is also limited with the amount of Federal funds it may expend to 50 
percent of the total construction cost for each project (34 C.F.R. § 361.60(a)(2)). 
MRS provided the review team detailed data and statistics on the services and success rate for 
participants who receive services at MCTI, broken down by those who enrolled, students who 
completed and graduated from each program, students still enrolled, number of those employed 
and those placed in their field of training, and the percentage who obtained licensure or 
completed the required State exam for the program, when applicable. A review of performance 
in PYs 2021 and 2022 indicated MCTI reported a completion rate (85 percent), graduation rate 
(88 percent), and total placement rate (93 percent) for students enrolled during the selected 
period. However, these percentages represent a small number of individuals served by MRS each 
year. The success and benefits associated with MCTI may not outweigh the costs to operate and 
maintain MCTI without an expansion of services and the number of individuals served.  
 
Technical Assistance  
 
• RSA discussed the potential effects of the large expenditures for operation and 
maintenance of MCTI on its performance and participants’ options of pursuing 
community-based training programs and education. MRS is aware the additional 
investments that are needed to renovate MCTI infrastructure and regular upgrades for its 
career assessment services and training programs must be balanced with the costs for 
supporting community evaluations and training and education programs to ensure 
participants exercise informed choice when determining the most appropriate provider for 
assessments and services to achieve the agreed upon vocational goal. Reducing the real 
and perceived barriers of attending MCTI and exploring additional services could assist 
in maximizing its use and increasing cost-effectiveness in its operation and maintenance.  
  
Recommendations  
  
• MRS should conduct surveys that explore barriers that may limit the field staff from 
referring eligible individuals from participating in career assessment services, training 
and educational programs at MCTI.   
• RSA recommended MCTI send out regular reminders to the field staff on the availability 
of assessment services and openings in training programs with tentative start dates to 
eliminate any misunderstanding about the availability of a program or services at the 
Center. In addition, MCTI should provide the field staff with information about the 
minimum physical and academic requirements for each program. For participants that are 
lacking in areas required for the program (i.e., reading level, math score), MCTI should 
make every opportunity to determine if support services could be provided while the 
participant attends the training program to meet the educational requirements.   
• RSA recommended MCTI routinely provide the field offices with brochures, literature 
and web-based video clips that the field staff could share with consumers and the schools 
to increase awareness and interest in the programs. MCTI staff also could attend training 
at district offices to provide an overview of programs and answer questions on a regular 
basis to increase awareness and eliminate any misinformation about the programs.  MCTI 
should continue to advertise and offer open house events to encourage MRS staff and VR

program participants to visit the facility to gain a better understanding of offered services 
and the benefits of its programs.   
 
Limited Supported Employment Services and Outcomes  
 
According to its reported data, MRS has underperformed and underserved individuals with the 
most significant disabilities in the provision of supported employment services and the 
achievement of supported employment outcomes, including customized employment. In PY 
2022, MRS reported 50 supported employment outcomes, representing less than one percent of 
the agency's competitive integrated employment outcomes for the year. Similarly, MRS reported 
providing supported employment services during PY 2022 to 1.1 percent of its participants 
compared to the average of 5.8 percent nationally of all VR agencies and it expended less than 
one percent (0.9 percent) of funds reported on the RSA-911 report ($220,186) compared to the 
national average of 8.3 percent expended for supported employment services.  
 
During a pre-onsite monitoring call, Michigan’s SRC reported MRS was not meeting the need 
for supported employment services, despite its members noting sufficient interest and resources 
in the community to provide supported employment services and ongoing supports. In addition, 
MRS’ Comprehensive Statewide Needs Assessment (CSNA), completed in 2023, showed 
supported employment services as underutilized when compared to MRS’ other services and 
individuals with the most significant disabilities who could benefit from these services remain an 
underserved population. 
 
MRS elected to not receive its FFY 2025 Supported Employment Title VI funds in its recently 
approved VR services portion of the Combined State plan, understanding that it must still 
provide supported employment services. MRS requested technical assistance from RSA during 
the review to identify resources in the State and develop strategies to increase the quality and 
quantity of supported employment services and outcomes in the future, reversing a longstanding 
performance issue.  
 
MRS’ most recently approved State plan also indicated an increased need to identify and develop 
accredited community rehabilitation providers (CRPs) that provide customized employment 
services in all areas of the state using the establishment authority to train its providers to expand 
and improve customized employment services across the State. MRS developed an agreement 
with a provider to conduct training for a select number of CRPs and MRS staff to increase the 
availability of customized employment opportunities for individuals significantly and most 
significantly disabled, including individuals pursuing a supported employment outcome. During 
the three-year review period, 0.1 percent of MRS participants received customized employment 
services.  
  
Technical Assistance  
 
• MRS acknowledged its staff would benefit from receiving targeted training on supported 
employment services, including eligibility requirements, allowable services, available 
resources across each district, and the transition from supported employment services to

13 
 
extended services. RSA will collaborate with the VRTAC-QM to provide targeted 
technical assistance to MRS staff. 
• MRS is piloting customized employment training for its CRPs in six districts with the 
goal to make the training available statewide in the next three years. MRS envisions 
using customized employment primarily for its supported employment participants to 
increase its supported employment outcomes. However, MRS acknowledged difficulty 
with sustaining interest in providing this service due to CRP staff leaving and the 
investment required by providers while staff are being trained. RSA discussed the need to 
continually gauge interest of its providers, availability of customize employment 
opportunities for its consumers and evaluate areas of training that are successful to 
replicate in other areas of the State. MRS will need to revise its policies for customized 
employment, accreditation process, how technical assistance is provided, and the hourly 
reimbursement rate. These and other efforts will help increase provider capacity and 
sustainability. 
 
Recommendations  
 
• MRS should educate and enhance VR counselors’ knowledge of the Individual 
Placement and Support (IPS) supported employment model adopted by community 
mental health clinics, focusing on how to collaborate and braid resources among partners. 
MRS could and seek opportunities to expand partnering with the Michigan Fidelity 
Review Team to facilitate trainings. 
• MRS should further develop and leverage its relationship with the Behavioral and 
Physical Health and Aging Services Administration and identify opportunities to 
encourage local collaboration to expand opportunities to target individual community 
mental health agencies. 
• MRS should consider building internal subject matter expertise within its staff whose 
primary focus would be supported employment and the coordination of customized 
employment opportunities for individuals with significant or most significant disabilities.  
• MRS should consider creating specialty caseloads for VR counselors that primarily focus 
on supported employment services, including extended services for youth with the most 
significant disabilities, and develop resources in the field.   
• MRS should engage high performing districts to share with districts who are 
underperforming and consider monitoring performance through a data dashboard. Three 
of the 13 districts accounted for almost half of the individuals who had a supported 
employment goal.  
• MRS should routinely train VR counselors on existing and new supported employment 
policies and procedures. All training should include an overview of how services are 
tracked in MRS’ case management system (CMS) and reported on a quarterly basis. 
• MRS should explore additional opportunities to offer on-going benefits counseling 
training to MRS staff so they can promote a positive message about the impact of work to 
individuals receiving Social Security benefits.

Insufficient Internal Controls for Data Reporting 
RSA selected three areas for case record validation of agency data and supporting documentation 
through the conduct of case reviews. The areas selected for review involved an examination of 
supporting documentation required for closure reasons, MSG, and credential attainment using a 
random selection of case service records in at least one of these areas for PY 2022. Since MRS’ 
CMS had not fully implemented electronic document images for supporting documentation, RSA 
provided the agency the unique identifiers for each of the 30 case records approximately one 
week before arriving on-site to allow MRS to gather paper files in advance of the case review. 
RSA engaged MRS staff in the review to ensure no supporting documentation was overlooked 
and to verify RSA’s conclusions about each case record reviewed. 
RSA reviewed 10 case records of individuals who were closed “ineligible after being determined 
eligible.” In all cases, the closure code was not supported by case documentation, and none 
included an explanatory administrative note indicating that the individual had decided to pursue 
non-competitive or non-integrated employment. RSA discussed the outcome of it review with 
MRS staff and the agency attributed the misreporting of these cases to a misunderstanding by 
staff of menu options. MRS failed to recognize this error despite its quality assurance processes. 
Data as consequential to MRS’ performance and the agency’s quality assurance processes do not 
appear to evaluate the disposition reasons for exit, an important aspect of case closures, as 
reported on the RSA-911. 
MRS’ Performance Enhancement Division has developed monitoring protocols for verification 
of supporting documentation for MSG and credential attainment, MRS appears to have 
inadequately assessed the correspondence of the electronic case record with external 
documentation. The agency performed better with the collection of supporting documentation for 
MSG (6 of 10 cases) than for credential attainment (2 of 10 cases). One difference between the 
two measures is that MSG documentation subject to this review was more recently collected, 
having been documented on active cases within the previous program year. On the other hand, 
many of the credential attainment cases reviewed were reliant upon documentation that was 
reported years earlier in the life of a now closed case. The agency’s MSG reporting and 
documentation efforts in PY 2022 are likely a better reflection of improvements such as the 
implementation of electronic document attachments, agency verifications through data match, 
and improved efforts in policy and training. MRS should improve upon its monitoring protocols 
to verify that supporting documentation is not only present, but that it matches and supports what 
was conveyed in the case record.   
MRS staff acknowledged that the agency has not reported individual level pre-employment 
transition services for any of the students served through third party cooperative arrangements 
(TPCA) on the RSA-911. Failing to capture service provision at the case level among those 
served through TCPAs limits the picture of MRS efforts for students with disabilities. Similarly, 
MRS did not believe that supported employment and customized employment services were 
reflected in the RSA 911 at a level commensurate with what the agency provides. MRS agreed to 
investigate these discrepancies and devise methods to reflect agency activity with full accuracy.

Recommendations  
 
• MRS should evaluate whether it is accurately identifying and reporting all individuals 
eligible for MSG. The percentage of participants eligible for MSG has decreased each 
year to 20.3 percent for PY 2022.  
• MRS should evaluate why credential attainment appears to be improving primarily for 
secondary education and determine how to increase postsecondary education 
opportunities for credentials as well as identify potential barriers. The number of 
credential attainments has increased significantly since PY 2020 to PY 2022; however, 
this increase is primarily attributable to secondary credentials while postsecondary 
credentials increased only slightly. 
• MRS should evaluate the extensive case review process to ensure it incorporates the 
necessary supporting documentation.   
• MRS should evaluate the staff’s understanding of RSA-911 reporting, particularly data 
affecting the disposition of case closures.  
Factors Influencing Accuracy of Performance Data 
MRS reported a large number of individuals closed as ineligible after determining them eligible 
for VR services (Data table 3 -Reasons for Exit). PD-19-03 defines the use of this exit reason 
when "[t]he individual was determined eligible for the VR program; however, the individual was 
no longer eligible because he or she no longer wished to seek competitive integrated employment 
or the individual’s disability prevented the individual’s ability to seek competitive integrated 
employment." In MRS’ procedure manual, reasons for exit are defined at “7000 VR Case Record 
Closure” and indicates requirements for closing a case when the individual is ‘No Longer

MRS reported 1,900 or 16.1 percent of all reasons for exit as “ineligible after determined

excluded from WIOA performance calculations, and as a result, likely contributed to higher 
performance results for employment after exit measures in the second and fourth quarters.

From PY 2020 through PY 2022, MRS increased the number of applicants by 3,802 individuals 
(36 percent), from 10,443 individuals to 14,245 individuals, and the number of individuals with 
disabilities determined eligible by a total of 3,620 individuals (39 percent), from 9,254

individuals during the same period. In PY 2022, 1,282 individuals exited before eligibility

Administrative Challenges   
In FFY 2019, MRS and its workforce partners were notified through Executive Order (EO) 
2019-13 that it would be reorganized to a new department called the Department of Labor and 
Economic Opportunity (LEO), effective August 11, 2019. RSA reviewed the EO and worked 
with MRS and other partners at the Federal and State level to develop a Grant Transfer 
Agreement to help facilitate the transfer of its Federal funds and property obtained through 
Federal funds administered by RSA and authority for its data and fiscal reports following the 
reorganization. Pursuant to EO 2019-13, MRS, the designated State unit (DSU) for VR services 
was transferred from the Department of Health and Human Services to LEO, which would serve 
as the designated State agency (DSA), in accordance with Section (6)(b)(2) of EO 2019-13. In 
the State of Michigan, individuals who are visually impaired or blind are served through the 
other VR agency, the Bureau of Services for Blind Persons (BSBP).  
After the reorganization, MRS reported that the State established the LEO-Employment and 
Training (E&T) as an intervening agency. E&T has implemented policies affecting information 
technology (IT), personnel and human resources, communication functions and purchasing 
authority. MRS has reported confusion over the continual development and expansion of mostly 
unwritten procedures implemented by E&T that have expanded its authority without input from 
the VR agencies, resulting in an inability for MRS to maintain or retain full control over its non-
delegated authority as the DSU for the VR program in Michigan, as required under Section 
101(a)(2)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(b) and (c).  
MRS's organizational structure consists of an Executive Leadership Team, a Strategic Initiatives 
Division Business Network Division, Performance Enhancement Division, and the Staff 
Development and Policy Division. In addition, MRS has a Field Services Division that includes 
all field offices across the State divided into two geographical areas, the southeastern division, 
and the western, central and northern division. MRS also has a division that manages and 
provides direct services to eligible individuals at its State-operated rehabilitation center, MCTI. 
LEO has an approved Departmental Cost Allocation Plan (CAP) and Indirect Cost Rate plan. 
The CAP allocates primarily across three categories: Direct Executive Direction Personnel 
Costs; Executive Direction Personnel Costs; and Administration Personnel and Non-Personnel

19 
 
Costs. The CAP includes MRS as one of many divisions within LEO that are used to cover 
shared costs for centralized functions. For example, LEO maintains units such as Human 
Resources, Communications and Outreach offices that centralize functions for all of the divisions 
under LEO. The majority of E&T staff are not included in the LEO CAP. As a result, over the 
past few years E&T has begun charging its staff’s time directly to MRS (and BSBP) and 
Workforce Development (WD) using Labor Distribution Personnel Rate (LDPR) codes for its 
staff that appear unnecessary and redundant. For instance, E&T established an Outreach & 
Communication office that directly charges the two VR agencies for personnel costs for services 
without prior approval or other notification when charges have been made. LEO also maintains 
both Communication and Outreach offices that serve the two VR agencies and MRS has its own 
Communication and Outreach units.     
According to MRS, despite its requests over the past three-plus years to identify an agreed upon 
process for any work that has been centralized within the workforce development programs, 
MRS has reported that direct charges continue to be made to the VR award based on E&T staffs’ 
LDPR personnel costs, without prior notice, knowledge of the services or identified benefit to the 
VR agency. In addition, the methodology used for these charges are not consistently applied, 
with some staff charged based on actual time attributed to MRS activities while others are charge 
using the FTEs across MRS, BSBP and the WD programs, accounting for 64.5 percent charged 
to MRS, 13.8 percent charged to BSBP, and 21.7 percent charged to WD programs regardless of 
the actual benefit to the programs. However, a review of a sample of certain E&T staff who track 
time to the three programs via LDPR codes demonstrates a contrasting picture, with resulting 
allocations of 65.23 percent benefitting WD, 28.77 percent benefitting MRS, and 6.0 percent 
benefitting BSBP. In contrast to the arbitrary use of program FTE counts, the collection of actual 
LDPR data represents a basis to allocate time or costs that cannot be tracked directly. LDPR data 
distribution appears to function as the only mechanism that meets Federal cost principle 
requirements for allocation, as such data identifies proportionate use and relative benefits 
received by the three programs. There are also at least five staff with 100 percent of their time 
charged to MRS despite no longer being assigned or under the supervision of MRS.    
During the monitoring process, RSA became aware of a procedure document (How to use Labor 
Distribution (LDPR) codes in SIGMA) used by E&T staff to allocate time to WD, MRS and 
BSBP. The procedure is limited in its scope and specificity and only became known to MRS in 
May 2024 despite the effective date indicating February 25, 2021, and revised January 3, 2023. 
Further, E&T implemented the document despite numerous requests by RSA and both VR 
agencies that it be involved or have input with the development of policies and procedures to 
ensure any direct administrative personnel costs meet the requirements of 2 C.F.R. § 200.407(h) 
and 2 C.F.R. § 200.413(c), to ensure all costs are allowable and not later subject to disallowance, 
and to ensure MRS’ ability to administer the VR program in an efficient manner to carry out all 
VR functions properly (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). In addition, RSA requested these procedures clearly 
demonstrate costs submitted are allowable, reasonable, and allocable to the program (2 C.F.R. §§ 
200.403 through 200.405) and these costs be based on activities that directly benefit the awards, 
programs or cost objectives through supporting documentation indicating such costs are

MRS must retain sole responsibility for the functions affecting the VR program, as required by

Insufficient Control Over Administrative Expenditures  
MRS uses a proprietary CMS to collect and report VR services for all individuals served through 
its VR program, from referral to closure and post-employment services, including students with 
disabilities potentially eligible for services. MRS procured its CMS with VR funds and is 
responsible for its operations as the sole owner and only agency that uses this system in 
Michigan. Until recently, MRS has been the entity responsible for the CMS continued 
maintenance and system configuration. In addition, MRS was responsible for hiring the staff to 
maintain its CMS, coordinate enhancement and troubleshoot issues with the vendor and

21 
 
continually worked with MRS staff to adjust reports and improve the system based on the needs 
of the agency and the reporting requirements mandated by RSA. The technical team for the CMS 
must be responsive to the directives and priorities of MRS management and should implement 
the agency’s priorities for system configuration, enhancements, and customized reports. 
MRS reported staff hired for the maintenance and operation of its CMS were recently removed 
from its oversight and supervision and are now positioned under E&T Enterprise Information 
Management (EIM). Although these staff no longer are under the administrative control of MRS, 
the agency has reported it continues to be charged the salaries of the five employees, and a sixth 
position (CMS trainer) has remained vacant for over a year, as of the time the on-site review was

MRS discussed not having direct oversight and administration of staff hired to manage its CMS 
and the effects of moving the staff outside of its authority has created unnecessary barriers and 
delays to managing its own data. Because the salaries of these individuals are fully allocated to 
MRS, 100 percent of their time must be allocated to VR-related work, and the supervision and 
oversight of the staff must remain with MRS who could certify their timesheets. 
MRS requires direct communication and oversight with the staff tasked with system 
configuration, system improvements, and Federal reporting for the RSA-911 and RSA-17. Since 
the staff were moved outside of MRS, the agency is required to submit requested work orders 
using a SharePoint form to obtain technical assistance for its CMS. However, the selection, 
prioritization and implementation of all tasks are outside MRS’ control and identified by MRS as 
ineffective. Further, the change in structure has resulted in ineffective operations due to delays, 
inability to prioritize work, and the agency’s failure to obtain the training staff need to 
continually improve its performance.

Hiring Practices/Staff Capacity  
MRS identified the ability to retain its staff as the primary concern to continue or improve upon 
its performance and the number of individuals it serves. At the time of the review, MRS 
employed 261 VRCs out of a total of 389 MRS staff employed. There were 49 VRC vacancies 
and an additional 19 other vacancies. Due to the vacancy levels, some VR counselors and 
supervisors are taking on additional caseloads. MRS leadership noted the opportunities for 
people with counseling degrees are able to earn significantly more working for universities, 
Federal government agencies such as the Department of Veterans Affairs, and local school 
districts and mental health agencies when compared to the State salary for VR program staff.  
Since the pandemic, MRS has steadily increased the number of applicants, eligible individuals 
and approved IPEs to a point approaching pre-pandemic levels; however, MRS is struggling with 
staffing shortages. MRS indicated that the pandemic negatively impacted its staffing levels as 
some chose to resign or retire and some found employment in other fields that offered more 
flexibility with work location. At the same time, MRS noticed similar patterns with reduced 
staffing levels for many of its CRPs, partly due to the decreased referrals during pandemic, and 
some staff choosing to work in a different field or declining to return to work. 
MRS has also identified additional barriers with its human resources capacities, namely the 
control over the hiring staff or filling vacancies for several key positions. MRS identified several 
positions that have remained vacant for a year or more despite submitting the necessary postings 
for these positions. MRS reported previously, job postings could be made available within days 
for any vacant FTE. However, the agency reported that since E&T became involved the process 
has slowed significantly for reasons unknown. For example, the position for the VR Director for 
MRS was vacant for about a year before position was posted and eventually filled. The new 
Director was the Deputy Director at the time and her position became vacant and has not been 
posted despite efforts by MRS to have it filled, along with other positions within the agency. 
MRS’ inability to fill vacancy or have this process delayed can lead to higher burden placed on 
staff and declines in performance and services to individuals with disabilities.  
The prolonged vacancy rates and staff turnover was further exacerbated by new service rates 
MRS introduced for purchased services for most VR services, later identified by many of its 
CRPs as not being fair, causing some disruption in services. MRS also noted significant change 
with the personnel at schools requiring the VR counselors to establish relationships at many of 
the schools. According to MRS, there was about a 60 percent turnover rate at the schools 
involving the transition coordinators or special education teachers.  
MRS cited decreased staffing levels and many of its providers as being the one of the primary 
threats to its continued strong performance and ability to continually improve its program. The 
agency noted the benefits offered by the State, along with job security and some workplace 
flexibilities have recently been overshadowed by higher paying wage jobs available in other 
agencies and fields in the Federal and public sectors within the State. MRS further noted that as 
positions remain vacant and additional position become vacant, the burden on its staff through

Pre-Employment Transition Services Provision and Insufficient Reporting  
Engaging students with disabilities early in career exploration through pre-employment transition 
services increases the student’s opportunities when transitioning to postsecondary education, 
training and employment fields. RSA’s analysis of MRS pre-employment transition services data 
showed the total number of reported students with disabilities decreased from 16,415 to 15,627 
from PY 2020 to PY 2022, however, the number of students who received pre-employment 
transition services increased from 5,943 to 7,587, representing an increase in the percentage of 
reported students with disabilities who received pre-employment transition services from 36.2 
percent to 48.6 percent. The increase was attributed to the number of potentially eligible students 
served, an additional 1,056 students (31 percent) from PY 2020 to PY 2022.  
In addition, the total number of pre-employment transition services provided rose from 15,997 to 
23,401. This increase also led to a rise in total expenditures for purchased pre-employment 
transition services, which grew from $4,619,393 in PY 2020 to $6,268,011 in PY 2022, as 
reported on the RSA-911. Work-based learning experiences accounted for 61.3 percent of these 
expenditures in PY 2022, more than double the amount spent on this service in PY 2020. 
MRS provides pre-employment transition services through a combination of MRS staff provided 
services and purchased services using community providers. MRS does not have any fee-based 
contracts for the provision of pre-employment transition services. Rather, MRS has established 
144 interagency transfers of funds (IATFs) agreements, of which about 60 percent are for pre-
employment transition services with intermediate school districts (ISD) for the provision of 
services to students with disabilities. The other 40 percent of the IATFs are with the local mental 
health agency. In addition, MRS has two third-party cooperative arrangements (TPCAs) with 
ISDs in Macomb and Saginaw counties that have been in place for over 25 years.   
MRS has 22 pre-employment transition counselors that are responsible for providing services to 
potentially eligible students. Pre-employment transition counselors only work with potentially 
eligible students and if the student applies for VR services, the counselor will refer the student to 
one of the VR counselors to begin the VR process. The majority of students served by MRS are 
potentially eligible for services. Approximately 40 percent of students apply or are determined 
eligible for VR services.  
In PY 2022, MRS reported a total of 23,401 pre-employment transition services provided but 
identified only 1,132 of these services provided by staff, or 4.8 percent based on data from the 
RSA’s Quarterly Data Dashboards. Similarly, in PY 2023, MRS reported the provision of 24,805

25 
 
pre-employment transition services and 1,980 of these services were provided by MRS staff. All 
other services reported were purchased. MRS indicated that it believes that not all of the pre-
employment transition services provided by its staff have been reported. Due to limited staff 
capacity MRS is exploring its ability to increase access to CRPs, particularly in rural areas of the 
State, and develop additional IATFs with schools for the provision of pre-employment transition 
services.   
Following RSA’ review in FFY 2017, MRS was required to develop and implement a method for 
establishing rates of services that would be uniformly applied when developing rates for all VR 
and pre-employment transition services. MRS implemented its revised rates by PY 2022; 
however, providers objected to the rates and declined to continue working with the agency. 
During RSA’s call with the SRC, a number of current and former CRPs who serve on the council 
did not agree with the hourly rates for providers in rural areas of the State.  
RSA reviewed a sample of IATFs for the provision of pre-employment transition services, noting 
some variation in the amount and scope of services. MRS has 56 ISDs and 705 school districts 
and has an expanded age range for students with disabilities, ranging from 14 to 26, creating a 
potentially large pool of students with disabilities eligible for pre-employment transition 
services. MRS stated each agreement is reviewed and discussed with the ISD or school district 
before the academic year begin and while much of the language in the agreement remains in 
place for one year to the next, the “Scope of Vocational Rehabilitation Services to be Provided 
Under the Program” section and “Program Evaluation” are modified to meet the criteria for the 
specific district. In addition, MRS indicate it provides a “Summary of Program Data by Fund 
Source and Cash Match Spending Report” to the ISD or school district at the end of the 
academic year to evaluate the success of the agreement. Due to the identified inconsistencies 
between the outcome goals and the listed indicators or the limited details under the program 
evaluation, it is not clear how these agreements can be adequately assessed when the final 
program data and cash spending reports are issued at the end of the academic year.  
For example, the Lenawee Intermediate School District (Agreement #3112) identified the 
outcome goals as support for 10 students to participate in career exploration, 30 students referred 
and completing a work experience with a community employer, 10 students referred and 
completing trial work experiences with a community employer and 10 customers achieving 
rehabilitation closure. Under the Program Evaluation section, the agreement identifies success 
indicators: application-35; TWE-35; Eligibilities 26; IPE-20; and rehabilitation- 13. Most of the 
other IATFs reviewed included general goals that included a specific number of referrals, 
eligibility determinations and employment outcomes with minimal details and goals involving 
pre-employment transition services beyond listing the five required activities that may be 
provided.

Finding 2.1 Insufficient Internal Controls for Data Reporting 
 
  
Issue: Did MRS establish and maintain effective internal controls over the Federal award to 
provide a reasonable assurance that it was managing the Federal award in compliance with 
Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award in accordance with 2 
C.F.R. § 200.303 and the requirements at 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.47(a), 361.48(a), and 361.50. 
 
  
Requirement: Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, VR agencies are required to develop internal 
control processes to provide reasonable assurances regarding the effectiveness and efficiency of 
operations, reliability of reporting for internal and external use, and implemented as a measure of 
checks and balances to ensure proper expenditure of funds, including the evaluation, and 
monitoring of compliance with statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of Federal 
awards. Furthermore, a State VR agency must assure in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. 
 
  
An internal control deficiency exists when the design or operation of a control does not allow 
management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to 
prevent or correct processes that might lead to non-compliance with Federal and State 
requirements.  
 
  
Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.47(a), VR agencies must maintain for each applicant and eligible 
individual a record of services that includes, to the extent pertinent, documentation including, but 
not limited to, the individual’s application for VR services, the individual’s IPE, and information 
related to closing the service record of an individual who achieves an employment outcome. The 
record of service must allow for accurate and timely data reports pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.40 
and provide the level of detail required by the Secretary regarding applicants for and eligible 
individuals receiving services, including students with disabilities receiving pre-employment 
transition services in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.48(a), and meet the performance 
accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. 
 
  
Analysis: MRS did not have sufficient internal controls to prevent deficiencies and inaccuracies 
with the collection and reporting of its performance and fiscal data. While many of the functions 
and responsibilities of MRS’ internal controls were thoroughly defined in its policy and 
procedure, the MRS system of internal controls was not sufficient enough to detect or prevent the 
following:

28 
 
• MRS did not accurately report the reason for exit for hundreds of cases each year that 
resulted in the exclusion of participants from WIOA performance calculations and 
inflated several performance indicators.  
• MRS did not maintain appropriate supporting documentation for MSGs and credentials 
earned.  
• MRS did not report pre-employment transition services for any of the students served 
through two TPCAs. 
• MRS did not report some VR services, such as customized employment, job placement 
services, and supported employment services each time the service was provided or 
purchased, leading to inaccurate data. 
 
MRS’ use of the reason for exit option, “ineligible after determined eligible” is significant due to 
the number and percent of case records attributed to this exit reason and its effect on MRS’ 
performance over the three years of review. In PY 2020 through PY 2022, the number and 
percentage of individuals’ case records closed attributed to “ineligible after determined eligible” 
increased from 1,900 individuals (16.1 percent) to 2,569 individuals (18.7 percent) of all 
individuals who exited the VR process. Inaccurately applying and reporting this exit reason 
resulted in removing individuals from the performance calculation for three of the five 
performance indicators. Despite monitoring protocols in place, the procedures for review failed 
to detect the inaccurate reporting attributed to a significant number of participants. RSA 
determined the inaccurate reporting likely improved the agency’s performance for post-exit 
employment rates in the second and fourth quarters by up to 10 percent per year and likely had a 
positive effect on the credential attainment rate.  
 
In addition, RSA conducted a review of 30 case service records while on-site to identify the 
required supporting documentation for three select areas:  reason for exit, measurable skill gains, 
and credential attainment (each case service record was reviewed for one of the three identified 
areas). The review team determined the necessary supporting documentation was not present or 
sufficient for 22 of the 30 cases selected. For the ten service records reviewed to identify the 
appropriate use of the reason for exit, MRS’ policies require an explanatory administrative note

appropriate administrative note. For the ten service records reviewed for measurable skill gains

Finding 2.2 Failure to Exercise Sole Control over the Expenditure of VR Program Funds  
  
Issue: Does MRS maintain sole responsibility for the expenditure of VR funds, as required by 34 
C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2). 
 
Requirement: The VR program regulations in 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1) specify minimum 
activities that are the responsibilities of the DSU. Specifically, MRS, as the DSU for the VR 
program in Michigan, must be solely responsible for: all decisions affecting the eligibility for VR 
services; the nature, scope and provision of services; determinations to close service records; 
policy formulation and implementation; the allocation and expenditure of VR funds; and 
participation of the VR program as a partner in the one-stop service delivery system (see also 
Section 101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(V) of the Rehabilitation Act). MRS may not delegate any of these 
functions or responsibilities to another entity or individual (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(2)).  
 
Analysis: The Michigan Combined State plan, approved on May 29, 2024, identified LEO as the 
DSA and MRS as the DSU to provide the VR services as the designated division primarily 
concerned with VR services, along with BSBP. In submitting the VR services portion of the 
State plan, MRS assured that the VR program would “operate and is responsible for the 
administration” in accordance with applicable Federal requirements and regulations and provided 
the assurance that all “funds made available to States under Section 111(a) of the Rehabilitation 
Act are used solely for the provision of VR services and the administration of the VR services 
portion of the Unified or Combined State Plan.” Further, LEO and MRS assured that MRS is 
responsible, at a minimum, for the functions outlined in 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1). These 
functions are the sole responsibility of the DSU and may not be delegated to another individual 
or agency (34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(2)).   
MRS has been using the same CMS for over two decades and has been responsible for the 
maintenance and operation of the system, including the personnel hired with the skills required 
to provide the technical assistance needed to manage the operation of its CMS as a vital 
component of the VR agency to meet Federal requirements for the collection, management and 
reporting of its data and fiscal expenditures. Following the enactment of WIOA and 
implementing Federal regulations published in 2016, MRS identified the need for additional 
personnel to manage its CMS due to the added requirements and the need to expand the system’s 
capacity to collect and report students with disabilities and the need to track and report post-exit 
data for the new performance indicators. Changing requirements, identified needs, technical 
support issues, additional report requirements, and continuous monitoring of case records 
supported the need for six staff to effectively manage the CMS with one employee dedicated to 
conducting training and managing system upgrades and enhancements to prepare all MRS staff 
on how to effectively use the CMS as part of MRS quality assurance procedures. 
In PY 2021, MRS learned that the personnel employed by MRS to maintain its CMS would be 
relocated to Enterprise Information Management (EIM) unit under a recently formed E&T

32 
 
Operations agency, located organizationally between LEO and MRS. This transfer of employees 
was intended to centralize information technology (IT) functions to reduce unnecessary costs and 
redundancy. MRS reported that E&T has assumed full control of the supervision, assignment of 
duties, and establishment of priorities for the EIM staff assigned to MRS’ CMS and allocated to 
MRS’ award. MRS also reported that since implementation of this change (approximately at the 
start of PY 2022), MRS has continued to be charged the full salaries of these staff now 
supervised under the administration of E&T Operations.  
Section 101(a)(2)(B)(ii)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(b)(1)(i) require that 
MRS, as the DSU, be responsible for the administration of the VR program. As the DSU, MRS 
must maintain the non-delegable authority, as detailed at 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c), that requires 
certain functions be reserved solely to the staff of the DSU, meaning that staff not employed by 
MRS cannot assign or otherwise delegate the duties to any other agency or individual. These 
“non-delegable” functions relate to decisions affecting, among other things, the allocation and 
expenditure of funds. 
 
RSA has long interpreted
1 these provisions to require that the specified functions and activities 
be carried out by the DSU’s own staff (60 FR 64475, 64482 (December 15, 1995) and 62 FR 
6307, 6316 (February 11, 1997)). Further, RSA has long believed, that the non-delegation 
provisions of 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c) “[strengthen] the role of the State unit by requiring that the 
unit have a substantial role in all decisions affecting the administration of the VR program 
whenever management functions within the State agency are centralized” (60 FR at 64482). 
Retaining these non-delegable functions within MRS: 
 
Ensure[s] that State agencies that consolidate staff to administer multiple State and 
federally funded programs do not entrust these key VR programmatic decisions to 
individuals who lack experience in meeting the needs of individuals with 
disabilities…[T]h e benefits derived from DSU retention of these functions – enhanced 
program efficiency and effectiveness – outweigh any costs that may be associated with 
the non-delegation requirements in the final regulations (62 FR at 6316). 
Because the State has centralized certain financial management functions within LEO, RSA’s 
monitoring activity assessed the manner in which MRS, as the DSU, exercises its non-delegable 
functions by providing effective input into the policy, planning, operations or other related 
decisions of those processes. While it is permissible for LEO to perform certain administrative 
functions related to financial management, centralization of these functions is impermissible if it 
results in interference with MRS’ decision-making capacity to administer the VR program.  
 
To assess the ability of MRS to carry out its non-delegable responsibility for the allocation and 
expenditure of VR program funds, RSA reviewed the position descriptions for the six EIM IT 
specialists, which closely mirrored the position descriptions previously under MRS. In 
comparing the assigned duties of both sets of position descriptions, assigned duties include the

Non-Delegable Responsibilities of the Designated State Unit for the Vocational Rehabilitation Program.” TAC-12-
03 provides comprehensive guidance on Federal requirements governing the organizational structure of the DSA 
and DSU and the non-delegable functions of the DSU for the VR program.

maintenance, technical support and training of staff involving the CMS. Duties further include 
the development of reports, testing, responding to technical support requests, and developing and 
conducting training for MRS staff. RSA also reviewed a sample of the timesheets for EIM IT 
staff, including a description of the work accomplished. While only a small sample of timesheets

In addition, MRS has reported staff employed by E&T charge direct personnel costs to MRS for 
“operational costs” that include, for example, IT, personnel and human resources (HR), and 
communication. LEO has an approved Departmental Cost Allocation Plan (CAP) and Indirect 
Cost Rate plan approved through its cognizant Federal agency, the U.S. Department of Labor. 
The CAP allocates costs primarily across three categories: Direct Executive Direction Personnel 
Costs; Executive Direction Personnel Costs; and Administration Personnel and Non-Personnel

costs of centralized functions. For example, LEO maintains units such as HR, Communications

encourage targeted populations identified as underserved and unserved, according to its

101(a)(15) and (25) of the Rehabilitation Act and 34 C.F.R. § 361.29(a) of the VR regulations), 
other communication and other similar functions can be centralized at the DSA level; however, 
the duties and different functions must be coordinated with the DSU and be consistent with 
agreed upon procedures. E&T has submitted to LEO Finance direct charges by its staff in 
addition to LEO’s indirect costs. MRS has reported that despite numerous requests, it has not 
been able to verify these services charged to the VR award or verify the services are benefiting 
the VR program and ensure there are no duplication of effort or charges for administrative 
functions provided indirectly and directly across LEO, LEO-E&T, and MRS. Furthermore, E&T 
submits personnel charges it assigns to MRS to staff within LEO Finance, without directly 
providing the charges to MRS to review such costs to ensure they are necessary, reasonable, 
allocable, and allowable charges to the VR award prior to drawdown by LEO Finance staff. 
MRS indicated this results in accounting adjustments after the drawdown to correct any 
misallocation of funds. As these examples make clear, MRS is not solely responsible for the 
allocation and expenditure of VR program funds for personnel costs pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 
361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2). MRS must maintain its ability to exercise non-delegable responsibility 
over the financial management of VR program funds with respect to expenditures made for 
direct personnel and other charges. 
 
Although it is permissible for LEO to centralize certain functions traditionally performed by the 
DSU, it is not permissible to centralize those functions that are reserved exclusively to the DSU 
pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c), such as the expenditure and allocation of funds. In this case, it 
is permissible for LEO and the State to centralize certain fiscal functions, including drawing 
down Federal VR funds from the Department’s GMS and accounting; however, the

34 
 
centralization of these fiscal tasks must not minimize the non-delegable functions of MRS with 
respect to the allocation and expenditure of VR funds in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 
361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2). In other words, regardless of which entity is responsible for performing 
the administrative parts of the fiscal tasks, all decisions about the spending of VR funds rests 
solely with MRS. For this reason, MRS must fully engage with all tasks that affect VR program 
funds and receive updates of administrative actions taken with respect to those funds; only in so 
doing can MRS know with certainty the amount of funds remaining in GMS, that its VR 
program funds are spent for allowable costs, and that all Federal requirements are satisfied (i.e., 
those for match, maintenance of effort, and the reservation of funds for the provision of pre-
employment transition services). The fact that MRS does not have a formal policy for the review 
of personnel charges and drawdowns of funds before they are made indicates that MRS is not 
involved with the allocation and expenditure of VR funds, as it must be in accordance with 34 
C.F.R. § 361.13(c)(1)(iv) and (2).  
Conclusion: RSA found that MRS was not solely responsible for certain functions of the VR 
program, namely the allocation and expenditure of funds as required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.13(c). 
RSA determined that regardless of whether the functions are centralized at the DSA level or 
through another State agency, the activities that prohibit MRS’ non-delegable functions cannot 
be provided outside the DSU, and MRS must exercise sole control over the expenditure and 
allocation of the VR funds.

Finding 3.1 Period of Performance Policies and Procedures 
Issue: Does MRS meet obligation and expenditure requirements in 2 C.F.R. § 200.1 and 34 
C.F.R. § 76.707. Does MRS assign and liquidate obligations such that expenditures align with 
the correct fund source and Federal award in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.12, 2 C.F.R. §§ 
200.1, 200.302, 200.303(a), 200.309, and 34 C.F.R. § 76.702. 
Requirement: As a recipient of Federal VR and Supported Employment program funds, MRS 
must have procedures that ensure the proper and efficient administration of its VR and Supported 
Employment programs and enable MRS to carry out all required functions, including financial 
reporting (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). In accordance with the Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 
200.302(a), a State’s financial management systems, including records documenting compliance 
with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient 
to permit the preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and 
conditions; and the tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such 
funds have been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions 
of the Federal award. The Uniform Guidance requires the financial management system of each 
non-Federal entity to provide for the identification, in its accounts, of all Federal awards received 
and expended and the Federal programs under which they were received (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)). 
In addition, Education Department General Administrative Regulations (EDGAR) at 34 C.F.R. § 
76.702 require States to use fiscal control and fund accounting procedures that ensure proper 
disbursement of and accounting for Federal funds.  
Each grant award has a defined “period of performance” that is the time during which the non-
Federal entity may incur new obligations to carry out the work authorized under the Federal 
award (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). A non-Federal entity may only charge to the Federal award allowable 
costs incurred during the period of performance (2 C.F.R. § 200.309, see also EDGAR 34 C.F.R.

46 
 
§§ 76.703 and 76.709). Grantees must implement internal controls necessary to ensure 
obligations and expenditures for a Federal award are assigned, tracked, recorded, and reported 
within the applicable period of performance for that Federal award, thereby ensuring the grantees 
are managing the award in compliance with Federal requirements (2 C.F.R. § 200.303(a)). The 
proper assignment of Federal and non-Federal funds to the correct period of performance is 
necessary for MRS to correctly account for VR and Supported Employment program funds so 
that RSA can be assured the agency has satisfied requirements for, among other things, match 
(34 C.F.R. § 361.60), MOE (34 C.F.R. § 361.62), and the reservation and expenditure of VR 
funds for the provision of pre-employment transition services (34 C.F.R. § 361.65(a)(3)). 
Financial obligations mean “orders placed for property and services, contracts and subawards 
made, and similar transactions during a given period that require payment by the non-Federal 
entity during the same or a future period" (2 C.F.R. § 200.1). For expenditures to be allowable 
under the Federal award, agencies must demonstrate that the obligation occurred within the 
period of performance of the Federal award. EDGAR regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 76.707 define 
when a State incurs an obligation for various kinds of services and property. Expenditures must 
be for payment of actual obligations. Obligations must be charged to a Federal award and must 
occur within the appropriate period of performance. Therefore, to properly account for and 
liquidate expenditures, grantees must be able to assign an obligation to a Federal award or other 
available fund source based upon the date the obligation was made (34 C.F.R. §§ 76.703 and 
76.709). 
Analysis: During the review, RSA and MRS discussed the agency’s ability to track and report 
obligations and liquidations in its systems and account for expenditures on Federal reports. In 
preparation for the review, RSA transmitted a document request to learn more about the agency’s 
policies and procedures for the topic areas identified in the Monitoring and Technical Assistance 
Guide. A review of the documentation the agency submitted in response to the period of 
performance topic area included various policies such as the State of Michigan Financial 
Management Guide (Chapter 15 Encumbrances and Establishment/Liquidation), LEO RSA-17 
SOP Federal Financial Reporting, an administrative policy for tracking obligations and 
encumbrances and one for processing invoices outside of the case management system (ADM 
0408), as well as several MRS policies from its Rehabilitation Services Manual addressing topics 
such as authorization for services, fee schedule and rates of payment, billing-payment for 
services, competitive bids and price quotations, and approval authority. Combined, the policies at 
times address elements of period of performance, but do not provide a robust, comprehensive 
policy addressing how all financial systems manage period of performance and what internal 
controls are established to ensure all period of performance requirements are met.  
Discussions with staff provided more detailed information about the need to establish internal 
control structures to ensure obligations are assigned to the appropriate fund sources, then 
tracked, liquidated and reported. RSA noted the agency demonstrated and described processes 
that were beyond the scope of policies and procedures provided, and discussed the importance of 
developing comprehensive policies demonstrating how LEO and MRS processes ensure all cost 
categories in EDGAR 34 C.F.R. § 76.707 meet the period of performance requirements.

Issue: Does MRS maintain effective internal control over the Federal award to provide

50 
 
Requirements: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or

efficient administration of the VR program. These methods of administration (i.e., the agency’s 
internal controls) must include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial 
accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12).  
 
“Internal controls” means a process, implemented by a non-Federal entity, designed to provide 
reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in the following categories: 
  
• Effectiveness and efficiency of operations;  
• Reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and  
• Compliance with applicable laws and regulations (2 C.F.R. § 200.1).  
 
In accordance with the Uniform Guidance, 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, among other things, a 
non-Federal entity must—  
 
• Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in

• Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings.  
 
In its guidance, The Role of Internal Control, Documenting Internal Control, and Determining 
Allowability & Use of Funds, the Department made clear to grantees that internal controls 
represent those processes by which an organization assures operational objectives are achieved 
efficiently, effectively, and with reliable, compliant reporting. Therefore, an internal control 
deficiency would exist when the design or operation of a control does not allow management or 
employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent or correct 
processes that might lead to noncompliance with Federal and State requirements.  
 
Analysis: RSA requested documentation from MRS as part of its review activities, including

the terms and conditions of Federal awards; and 
• Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings.  
Additionally, provisions at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a) require that a State’s financial management 
systems, including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the 
terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the—  
• Preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and conditions; and 
• Tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have 
been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of

59 
 
the Federal award.  
Furthermore, provisions at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b)(4) require that the financial management 
system of each non-Federal entity must ensure effective control over, and accountability for, all 
funds, property, and other assets. The non-Federal entity must adequately safeguard all assets 
and assure that they are used solely for authorized purposes. 
In its guidance The Role of Internal Control, Documenting Internal Control, and Determining 
Allowability & Use of Funds, the Department made clear to grantees that internal controls 
represent those processes by which an organization assures operational objectives are achieved 
efficiently, effectively, and with reliable, compliant reporting. Therefore, an internal control 
deficiency would exist when the design or operation of a control does not allow management or 
employees, in the normal course of performing their 42 assigned functions, to prevent or correct 
processes that might lead to noncompliance with Federal and State requirements. 
Analysis: RSA found areas of concern, listed below, that fall within the internal control focus 
area and specifically relate to the VR agency’s contracts and service agreements.  
A. Lack of Internal Controls 
RSA reviewed sample administrative contracts with MRS staff as well as the contracting process 
used to ensure compliance with the Federal award. Although MRS was able to demonstrate the 
oversight and contracting process used for administrative contracts housed in SIGMA, MRS did 
not produce evidence of any contracting process or oversight over for purchased service 
agreements. The Uniform Guidance requirements at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a) require that a State’s 
financial management system, including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the 
preparation of reports required by general and program specific terms and conditions, and the 
tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have been used 
according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award. 
The agency explained that the process for vendor registration for VR purchased service 
agreements are handled at the district level. In order to provide VR services, vendors are required 
to register in SIGMA through the Vendor Support Service web portal. However, staff in MRS 
local district staff members must also place the vendors into the CMS to permit the authorization 
of VR services at the district level. MRS staff further explained that there is not a centralized or 
consistent statewide process to ensure that the vendors agree to meet the Federal requirements 
(i.e. Uniform Guidance requirements) and State requirements (e.g., licensure, fingerprinting, 
etc.). RSA clarified that vendor service agreements and the authorizations generated from the 
CMS are considered contracts and are subject to State and Federal requirements. Without a 
mechanism to ensure the vendor registration and service agreement process follow consistent 
policies and procedures established for all vendors providing services in all districts, MRS 
cannot ensure all State and Federal contract requirements have been met. Agency staff further 
described that MRS is in the beginning phase of a pilot to centralize the vendor reregistration 
process to require liability insurance for vendors and introduce additional mechanisms to ensure 
consistent licensure and credential requirement verification centrally. Additionally, MRS staff 
explained although this process will require administrative rulemaking which can take time, it

60 
 
expects to begin its pilot with 45 vendors.  
B. Contract Monitoring  
The Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.303(c) requires grantees to implement internal controls 
sufficient to ensure the grantee evaluates and monitors the agency’s activities to ensure 
compliance with Federal requirements. In addition, 2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a) requires MRS to be 
responsible for the oversight of the operations of all grant-supported activities. VR program 
implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. § 361.12 require MRS to employ methods of 
administration necessary to ensure the proper administration of the VR program and to carry out 
all functions under the VR portion of the Unified or Combined State plan. These methods 
include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial accountability. As such, MRS 
must monitor and evaluate grant-supported activities to ensure compliance for all activities 
performed under the VR program.  
As stated above, the VR purchased service agreements are considered contracts, and the agency 
uses service agreements or contracts as the mechanism to purchase eligible services for VR 
clients by issuing authorizations. Also as described above, each district enters into contracts with 
vendors to provide eligible VR services within their district. MRS staff explained that if 
monitoring is occurring, it occurs at the district level and districts have discretion regarding the 
content of monitoring and frequency. For administrative contracts, MRS staff described a 
monthly process that employs a combination of risk assessments and desk reviews of invoices, as 
well as a comparison of information housed in SIGMA to obligations. Staff further explained 
that administrative contract suspension and debarment checks are conducted in this process as 
well. RSA staff was able to review the monitoring procedures and protocols used for 
administrative contracts. Conversely, MRS confirmed that there is no formalized monitoring 
protocol for the VR purchased service agreement contracts.   
Discussions with the MRS staff during the review and the MRS response to the document 
request confirmed the agency did not have any current evidence of completed monitoring efforts 
for VR purchased service agreements or the vendors that provide themselves at the time of the 
review. The agency staff acknowledged the need to develop a written contract monitoring policy 
and procedure protocol, and to conduct contract monitoring in a more structured and formal 
manner, incorporating programmatic elements along with legal and fiscal requirements. 
Conclusion: RSA found that MRS does not maintain effective internal controls over the Federal 
awards necessary to provide reasonable assurances that it is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, as 
required by 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.3(a) and 361.12, and 2 C.F.R. §§ 200.302 and 200.303. MRS did 
not satisfy the requirements in 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.3 and 361.12, and 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a) and 
(b)(4) that require a State’s financial management systems to be sufficient to permit the tracing 
of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have been used 
according to the Federal requirements, and that funds are spent solely on authorized VR 
activities, because an internal control deficiency exists for developing contracts, monitoring 
contracts, reviewing and approving invoices and supporting documentation, which does not 
permit MRS to ensure all costs charged to the contracts are reasonable, necessary, allocable and

61 
 
allowable under the VR program, as required by Federal cost principles in Uniform Guidance.  
MRS does not have internal control mechanisms to monitor and reconcile all contracts 
reimbursed with Federal funds, to ensure that all expenditures reported are accurate, allocable 
and allowable, in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 200.405.  
Corrective Action 3.4 RSA requires that MRS –  
3.4.1  By the second quarterly update after approval of the CAP, develop, and implement 
written policies and mechanisms governing the development of contracts to ensure 
contracts, including VR purchased service agreements, follow a clear process in which 
vendors are registered uniformly to provide VR services. Such procedures should ensure 
vendors are informed and agree to all State and Federal licensing and certification, 
education, insurance, contract provisions (Uniform Guidance Appendix II), and Federal 
reporting requirements. Each vendor agreement must clearly outline a scope of the 
specific services the contractor will provide, measurable goals, objectives and expected 
outcomes, and tracking and reporting requirements of the contractor to ensure MRS has 
data sufficient to meet VR service provision and reporting requirements; and  
3.4.2  By the second quarterly update after approval of the CAP, develop and implement written 
policies and procedures governing the oversight of the operations of all grant-supported 
activities, as required by 2 C.F.R. § 200.329(a), particularly with respect to—
 
• Establishing contract monitoring steps for VR contracts, including VR purchased 
service agreements, to ensure all contractors are monitored. The written protocol 
should identify monitoring schedules and frequency, risk assessment rubrics, and 
instruments to evaluated whether programmatic and fiscal performance expectations 
of contracts are achieved, and procedures developed to identify contractual 
performance deficiencies and establish corrective action plans for all contractors; 
• Establishing and requiring ongoing monitoring of specific State and MRS VR vendor 
requirements including, but not limited to, licensure and certification requirements, 
education, fingerprinting and insurance liability requirements, as well as Uniform 
Guidance Appendix II contract provisions; and 
• Staff members’ review of VR service provider contract monitoring for activities 
including confirmation that supporting documentation sufficiently demonstrates the 
contractor is meeting all required program and State deliverables, performance 
expectations and requirements of the contract in accordance with the Federal award, 
and data made available to MRS central office staff, including PED, to ensure 
compliance with the provisions of 2 C.F.R. § 200.329.
