SECTION 1: THE SCOPE OF THE REVIEW 
A. BACKGROUND 
Section 107 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended by Title IV of the 
Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), requires the Commissioner of the 
Rehabilitation Services Administration (RSA) to conduct annual reviews and periodic onsite 
monitoring of programs authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act to determine whether a 
vocational rehabilitation (VR) agency is complying substantially with the provisions of its State 
Plan under Section 101 of the Rehabilitation Act and with the evaluation standards and 
performance indicators established under Section 106 of the Rehabilitation Act subject to the 
performance accountability provisions described in Section 116(b) of WIOA. In addition, the 
Commissioner must assess the degree to which VR agencies are complying with the assurances 
made in the State Plan Supplement for Supported Employment Services under Title VI of the 
Rehabilitation Act. In conducting the annual reviews, RSA must consider, at a minimum, budget 
and financial management data (Section 107(a)(2)(I) of the Rehabilitation Act); and, pursuant to 
Section 107(a)(4)(B) of the Rehabilitation Act, RSA must examine the provision of services, 
including pre-employment transition services. 
RSA works closely with its Federal partners at the U.S. Department of Labor’s Employment and 
Training Administration (ETA) and the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Career, 
Technical, and Adult Education (OCTAE) to share monitoring and technical assistance activities, 
especially as they relate to the joint provisions under WIOA. Although the VR program is one of 
the six core programs in the workforce development system, it is unique in that State VR 
agencies provide services directly to individuals with disabilities. Thus, the nature and scope of 
RSA’s monitoring process and report may appear different from the monitoring ETA and 
OCTAE conduct with their grantees.  
In fiscal year (FFY) 2024, RSA conducted an on-site monitoring review of the State Vocational 
Rehabilitation Services program (VR program) and the State Supported Employment Services 
program (Supported Employment program) administered by Colorado Division of Vocational 
Rehabilitation (DVR). The nature, scope, and focus of this review and the process by which RSA 
carried out its activities from January 30, 2024 through February 1, 2024, was defined by 
information regarding the VR agency’s use and expenditure of Federal funds, documents, and 
data submitted by DVR, taking into account the goals, unique circumstances, and technical 
assistance needs of the VR agency.  
RSA—  
● Assessed the performance of the VR and Supported Employment programs, including 
how the VR agency is implementing strategies and practices to achieve continuous 
improvement in the delivery of VR services, including pre-employment transition 
services, leading to quality competitive integrated employment and supported 
employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities;   
● Reviewed the VR agency’s financial management of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs, which supports and maximizes the use of Federal funds and resources to

quality employment outcomes; 
● Identified strategies, including innovative practices, the VR agency uses or may employ 
to improve competitive integrated employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities,

● Provided recommendations leading to continuous improvement in the number and quality 
of outcomes achieved by individuals with disabilities through the VR and Supported

● Identified compliance findings and corrective actions to improve processes and

● Provided technical assistance to assist and support the VR agency in developing strategic

funds.

SECTION 2: FOCUS AREA – PERFORMANCE OF THE STATE 
VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION SERVICES AND STATE SUPPORTED

A. PURPOSE 
 
Through this focus area, RSA assessed the VR agency’s VR and Supported Employment 
programs’ performance in assisting individuals with disabilities, including those with the most 
significant disabilities, with the necessary services and supports to achieve quality competitive 
integrated employment. During this focused review, RSA analyzed VR program data, policies 
and internal controls, and agency practices in implementing VR services within the context of 
maximizing the use and expenditure of funds to improve program performance. Where 
appropriate, RSA provided technical assistance and identified findings requiring corrective 
actions. RSA also provided recommendations, which the agency may consider as it develops 
strategic actions to improve performance and maximize the use and expenditure of Federal funds 
to achieve quality employment outcomes.  
 
B. ANALYSIS OF PERFORMANCE  
 
RSA’s analysis of the VR agency’s performance of the VR and Supported Employment 
programs incorporated a review of data reported by DVR on the Case Service Report (RSA-911) 
and the WIOA Statewide Performance Report (ETA-9169) for program years (PYs) 2020, 2021, 
and 2022, as well as the review and discussion of internal controls and policies and requested 
documents. A review of the VR agency profile and data related to the VR process, VR services, 
quality of employment outcomes, and pre-employment transition services, as well as discussions 
with DVR of potential factors influencing program performance informed the analysis and 
helped to identify technical assistance needs and recommendations to improve the performance 
and management of the VR and Supported Employment programs.  
 
Summary of Factors Influencing Performance 
 
Through the analysis of data and documents provided by the VR agency, information from 
related sources, and discussions with DVR during the review, RSA identified various factors, 
which may be contributing either positively or negatively to the VR agency’s performance 
leading to employment outcomes for individuals with disabilities and the use and expenditure of 
Federal funds. In addition to providing technical assistance, as appropriate and in response to 
DVR’s requests, RSA also developed recommendations for consideration by the VR agency as it 
develops strategies to ensure continuous improvement in program performance and to maximize 
the use and expenditure of available Federal funds to assist individuals with disabilities in 
achieving quality employment outcomes. 
 
Increase in Staff Attrition 
 
During the on-site review, DVR reported the turnover rate for staff was at eight percent during 
the period of review, with the primary reason for departure being low pay compared to other

7 
State positions. Despite a five percent pay increase in 2022, most VR counselors still leave for 
better-compensated roles within the State. The State union has recommended salary increases for 
all State employees to address this issue. These challenges are further exacerbated by Colorado’s 
high cost of living, and the lingering effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. 
 
DVR identified its turnover rate of VR personnel as one of the primary factors contributing to its 
high individual plan for employment (IPE) extension rate, which was 35.8 percent in PY 2020, 
26.5 percent in PY 2021, and 25.9 percent in PY 2022. To address staffing challenges, DVR 
implemented a strategy of hiring interns. While this alleviated some of the difficulties caused by 
personnel turnover, it also introduced complexities that contributed to the IPE extension rate. 
Interns and VR counselors with only bachelor’s degrees were required to have their work and 
processes approved by supervisors. Although this layer of oversight was essential for 
maintaining quality and consistency in service delivery, it added extra steps that prolonged the 
IPE process. Consequently, although recruiting interns and implementing rigorous review 
protocols for less-experienced staff enhanced the agency’s operational capacity, these actions 
may have inadvertently prolonged the process of finalizing IPEs, leading to a notable increase in 
the IPE extension rate in PY 2020 with an average of 29.4 percent over the three-year review 
period.  
 
RSA noted that DVR has taken several steps to mitigate staff attrition. These include the 
development of a training academy to provide professional development to new and current staff, 
a newsletter (DVR Broadcast) to keep staff apprised of key agency developments, and an 
initiative (Wellness Wednesdays) to help staff manage stress and maintain a healthy work-life 
balance. 
 
Technical Assistance 
 
● Staff attrition can significantly impact the performance of DVR and makes it essential to 
regularly analyze turnover rates and their underlying causes. High attrition disrupts 
continuity in service delivery, delays participant progress, and increases the burden on 
remaining staff which potentially leads to burnout. Additionally, frequent turnover may 
hinder the development of strong relationships and engagement between staff and 
participants, which are critical for achieving successful outcomes. By assessing attrition 
patterns, DVR can identify factors contributing to staff departures, such as workload 
demands, insufficient training, or organizational culture, and implement strategies to 
address them. Proactive measures to reduce attrition foster a more stable workforce, 
enhance program efficiency, and improve service delivery.  
● The Vocational Rehabilitation Technical Assistance Center for Quality Management 
(VRTAC-QM) developed a training that might assist DVR with hiring practices: 
Onboarding: Lessons Learned and a Path Forward
. The training is intended for 
supervisors and managers in State VR agencies to provide an overview of onboarding for 
new employees to enhance their knowledge and skills, including methods to prepare new 
employees to function effectively in their new roles. The VRTAC-QM also compiled a 
collection of 
recruitment and retention strategies and resources from VR programs and 
other stakeholders from various sources.

Recommendations 
 
● Survey current staff about staff attrition, as this can be a helpful way to gather 
information and feedback on why employees are leaving the agency and help identify 
areas where improvements can be made to reduce turnover.  
● Review DVR’s recruitment process and identify areas where delays commonly occur and 
simplify the process to reduce time-consuming steps. 
● Clearly define the job requirements, roles and responsibilities, and candidate 
qualifications in job postings and during interviews.  
● Involve critical stakeholders in the recruitment process, such as hiring managers, district 
managers, and VR counselors, to ensure that qualified candidates are interviewed for job 
openings.  
● Establish partnerships with local educational institutions to promote job opportunities and 
internships to help attract new talent and create a pipeline of qualified candidates.  
 
Participant Attrition 
Although DVR reported increases in the number of applicants (4,776 applicants in PY 2020 to 
5,515 applicants in PY 2022) and eligible individuals (4,181 in PY 2020 to 5,097 in PY 2022) in 
the VR program, a large percentage of individuals are exiting prematurely or without an 
employment outcome. In PY 2022, 22.3 percent of individuals exited the VR program after an 
eligibility determination, but prior to an IPE, and the percentage of individuals who exited the 
program after an IPE without an employment outcome was 34.5 percent. When reviewing the 
reported reasons for exit during the period under review, an average of 33.7 percent of 
individuals, or a third of those who applied for services, were reported as exiting the program 
because they were no longer interested in receiving VR services.  
DVR has not yet conducted a comprehensive analysis to uncover additional information about 
this increase in participant attrition. Although the agency reviews the participant attrition rate 
annually, this practice has only been in place for the past two years, highlighting the need for a 
more detailed and historical examination of attrition trends and their underlying causes. Once 
DVR conducts these comprehensive analyses and reviews, it can address those factors within its 
control.  
 
To mitigate participant attrition, DVR uses advanced labor market software to collect detailed 
data across Colorado, including active job postings, occupations (e.g., technicians and 
machinists), openings by city and employers, certifications (e.g., commercial driver’s license), 
hard skills (e.g., the ability to lift 51-100 pounds), soft skills (e.g., customer service and 
communication), job titles (e.g., machine operator), education levels (e.g., bachelor’s degree), 
programs (e.g., engineering), and job types (e.g., remote or full-time). The agency noted that this 
software benefits the agency participants by providing targeted job opportunities that match their 
skills and qualifications, increasing their chances of securing quality employment. For VR staff, 
the software offers valuable insights into current labor market trends, which enables them to 
tailor their guidance and support VR participants more effectively. DVR noted that this 
technology will likely enhance the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the job placement 
process, which may assist in efforts to mitigate participant attrition.

9 
Further, DVR has implemented VR counselor floating positions. These floating counselors are 
assigned to manage caseloads in areas lacking VR counselors, ensuring that cases are handled 
promptly and do not remain vacant for extended periods. This strategy also has proven helpful in 
mitigating participant attrition. By fostering equity across the State with respect to consistent 
caseload sizes, this practice can support the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the VR 
program. 
 
DVR reported a notable decrease in community rehabilitation providers (CRPs) during the 
COVID-19 pandemic. To address this issue, the agency has adjusted payment rates for CRPs to 
rebuild and stabilize its provider network, ensuring the continued delivery of comprehensive VR 
services to participants. In addition, DVR has adopted a strategic shift in participant engagement 
and IPE plan development. Emphasizing flexibility and adaptability, the agency focused on early 
and rapid engagement with participants. This is part of a broader cultural shift, which includes 
encouraging VR counselors to accept that IPEs may evolve and can be amended, as appropriate. 
By starting with smaller, achievable steppingstones, DVR is seeking to make IPEs more fluid 
and responsive to participant needs and facilitate progress toward improved performance and 
outcomes. 
 
Technical Assistance 
RSA discussed the importance of systematically tracking participant attrition rates. This practice 
can assist DVR in identifying specific areas requiring improvement. For instance, if participants 
exit the program after eligibility determinations, it may be necessary to provide additional 
support or resources during the early stages of the VR program. Similarly, if participants leave 
after developing their IPEs, providing more timely service delivery is likely crucial to 
maintaining engagement. Furthermore, analyzing attrition can help identify if reportable 
individuals, such as students with disabilities, are at particular risk of exiting the VR program. 
The technique enables agency administrators to target individuals for additional support, or 
address systemic issues, through policy and procedural revisions. 
 
Recommendations 
 
Building on this technical assistance, RSA also provided the following recommendations to 
DVR: 
 
● Schedule periodic analyses of attrition data to identify emerging patterns or changes, and 
whether certain groups are more prone to disengage from the VR program;  
● Develop a standardized exit interview questionnaire and conduct interviews with 
participants who leave the program in person or via an online survey; 
● Organize bi-monthly focus group discussions with current participants to gather insights 
about their challenges and experiences; 
● Hold training sessions for staff to equip them with skills to identify and support at-risk 
participants; 
● Conduct an annual review of DVR’s structure to ensure it aligns with participant needs 
and industry trends;

10 
● Regularly review program policies and procedures frequently to ensure they do not 
inadvertently contribute to attrition; and 
● Seek technical assistance from VRTAC-QM and explore additional support and 
resources. Leveraging a variety of expertise will enhance DVR’s effectiveness and ensure 
comprehensive service delivery as it works to mitigate participant attrition.  
 
Policies and Procedures Limiting Training Services 
 
Colorado offers a range of statewide initiatives to expand education and job opportunities. 
However, the number of VR participants receiving training services remained notably low from 
PY 2020 to PY 2022. Data show that in PY 2020, 227, or 2.2 percent, of these participants 
received bachelor’s degree training, slightly declining to 211 participants or 2.1 percent, in PY 
2021 and a slight increase to 214, or 2.2 percent, in PY 2022. Similarly, the number of 
participants receiving junior or community college training declined from 310 (3.0 percent) 
participants in PY 2020 to 240 (2.4 percent) participants in PY 2022. The number of participants 
in occupational and vocational training showed slight fluctuations. In PY 2020, there were 73 
participants, accounting for 0.7 percent of the total, slightly decreasing to 56 participants or 0.6 
percent in PY 2021. However, in PY 2022, there was a slight increase, with the number of 
participants rising to 65, mirroring the initial percentage in PY 2020. 
 
In exploring the reasons behind the low enrollment of participants in training programs, it is 
evident that systemic barriers play a significant role. DVR acknowledged that although VR 
counselors are encouraged to guide participants toward postsecondary education and training 
opportunities, the process is challenging. A key issue involves the rigorous procedure for formal 
training, which requires extensive paperwork and justification. This requirement adds to the 
workload of the VR counselors and introduces a level of hesitancy due to the significantly 
burdensome procedural requirements. Adding to the complexity of the matter is the financial 
needs analysis accompanying the training enrollment process. Such policies, while intended to 
ensure the appropriate allocation of resources, can be restrictive and time-consuming. VR 
counselors must navigate these policies and procedures, along with supervisory approval 
processes, which adds another layer of complexity to their roles. These administrative hurdles 
may significantly impact the VR counselors’ ability to efficiently support participants’ 
enrollment in training programs, highlighting a critical area for review and potential reform to 
improve VR service delivery and performance. 
 
Technical Assistance 
 
DVR has communicated to VR counselors a framework for assessing VR services through the 
lens of necessity, appropriateness, and least cost. Recognizing the importance of being prudent 
stewards of State and Federal funds, RSA stressed that this messaging might need reassessment. 
The emphasis on the “least cost” criterion risks creating a perception among VR counselors that 
they should minimize the range or quality of services offered to participants, potentially limiting 
the support available to those in need. This could inadvertently lead to a reduction in VR services 
provided, as the term ”least cost” might be interpreted as favoring low-cost or no-cost options, 
which may not always align with the best interests of participants. Furthermore, RSA advised a 
strategic review of the 2023 Colorado Talent Pipeline Report and the continuous analysis of the

11 
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook to ensure that VR services stay 
aligned with employer needs, enhancing the relevance and effectiveness of training programs. 
Refining the balance in messaging on VR service provision criteria and leveraging data-driven 
insights allows DVR to support participants’ immediate and long-term career goals more 
effectively. 
 
RSA also provided DVR with the following guidance and strategies to increase the number of 
individuals participating in postsecondary education or vocational training to support quality 
employment outcomes: 
 
• RSA encourages, and WIOA expressly permits, VR agencies to provide VR services, 
encouraging qualified, eligible individuals to pursue advanced science, technology, 
engineering, mathematics, medicine, law, or business training; 
• RSA advised DVR that conducting an internal audit of existing services and programs 
could help to identify gaps in providing postsecondary education opportunities. This 
includes reviewing current resources, staff expertise, and the capabilities of the VR 
agency; 
• By assessing staff members’ training needs DVR can ensure they have the necessary 
skills and knowledge to support participants in accessing postsecondary education. 
Examples might include career counseling training, understanding educational systems, 
knowledge of financial aid processes, and labor market analysis; and 
• Developing or updating policies and procedures that specifically address supporting 
participants in postsecondary education and training, including application processes, 
support services, and follow-up protocols can promote better performance and outcomes 
for participants. 
 
Recommendations 
 
• RSA recommends that DVR seek technical assistance from VRTAC-QM and explore 
additional support and resources. Leveraging a variety of expertise will enhance DVR’s 
effectiveness and ensure comprehensive service delivery as it works to increase its 
participant training rate. Note that in PY 2022, DVR provided graduate degree training to 
0.8 percent, bachelor’s degree training to 2.2 percent, and junior college or community 
college training to 2.4 percent of all participants served. 
• RSA stressed that DVR’s policies and procedures contain information that may have 
been put in place prior to the passing of WIOA and its amendments to the Rehabilitation 
Act. RSA strongly recommends that DVR update its policies and procedures to ensure it 
provides individuals with disabilities the services they need to compete for and achieve 
high-quality employment in the 21
st century global economy. 
• DVR should assess the strength and effectiveness of existing partnerships with 
educational institutions and explore the potential for new collaborations. 
• DVR should examine its current budget and financial planning to ensure funds are 
allocated to support the expansion into postsecondary education. 
• RSA recommends that DVR incorporate the goal of providing postsecondary education 
opportunities into the agency’s broader strategic plan, ensuring alignment with the overall

mission and objectives (e.g., DVR will increase support of postsecondary education by 
20 percent by 2026). 
 
Policies and Procedures that Delay Processing Referrals and Applications 
During the period under review, DVR showed a 15.6 percent increase in the number of 
individuals applying to the VR program (4,776 applicants in PY 2020 to 5,515 applicants in PY 
2022). DVR attributed this increase to the implementation of an online referral system, but it 
reported that it does not have a process or procedures in place in the event an individual 
expresses the intent to apply for services at the time of referral. Once a referral for DVR services 
has been received with enough information to contact the individual, DVR’s policy states that a 
meeting with DVR staff must occur within 30 calendar days. The agency’s policy states that it 
accepts applications during an initial appointment and the 60-day timeline for eligibility 
determination begins upon submission of the signed application form to DVR unless the initial 
meeting is held remotely, and the signature is pending. In this instance, DVR will accept the 
individual’s verbal or written statement expressing their interest in applying to determine the 
date of application.  
At the time of the review, other than the exception related to initial meetings being held 
remotely, DVR shared that it did not have procedures in place to process completed applications 
for VR services received by telephone, email, online, or by sending a letter to the VR agency or 
by completing a common intake application form at a one-stop center as required under 34 
C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2).   
 
Technical Assistance 
 
RSA clarified that referrals need to include only the information necessary to contact an 
individual (e.g., address, phone number, email, etc.) in order for the agency to provide 
information on how to submit an application (34 C.F.R. § 361.41(a)). In the event a referral 
expresses the intent to apply for services (e.g., a self-referral) that constitutes an application, the 
60-day timeframe for eligibility determination begins at that point (34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(1)).  
 
Consistent with 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2), individuals with disabilities may apply for VR services 
in a variety of ways (e.g., completing a common intake application, telephone, etc.). There is no 
requirement that applications be submitted in writing or that they be signed. RSA informed the 
agency that it must develop written procedures for individuals, who would like to apply for VR 
services, by telephone, email, online, or by sending a letter to the VR agency or by completing a 
common intake application form at a one-stop center. For RSA-911 reporting purposes, the 
application date should be the date on which the application is submitted to the agency. 
 
Recommendations 
 
● While DVR has established policies for the VR program, there is a notable lack of 
accompanying procedures. Currently, DVR uses a public facing policy manual, an 
intranet site, and its fee schedule to disseminate policies and procedures to the public and 
its staff. The agency reported that policy changes are incorporated into the existing policy

13 
manual during periodic revisions organized by DVR’s policy advisory committee, 
however, there was no process in place during the period of review. DVR has a training 
team and business services team that disperses training material to new employees and

● RSA encouraged DVR to develop a process to disseminate easily accessible procedures 
aligned with their policies to improve program implementation and ensure consistency, 
including changes to its referral and application policy (Chapter 5.2 Applicants and 
Applications) to ensure it is compliant with 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2). Changes to these 
processes will increase participation in the VR program, ensure the delivery of high-
quality services, and enhance the reporting of valid data. 
● RSA observed a staffing vacancy in the key role of policy administrator in the agency, 
which has led to gaps in ensuring DVR staff have the tools needed to perform duties in 
the most efficient manner. During the period of review, DVR acknowledged the need to 
prioritize hiring for this position as it updates its policies and procedures. 
Application of Financial Participation Policies  
 
RSA discussed with DVR its use of financial participation policies and how these requirements 
relate to the agency’s ability to expend VR funds and maximize services leading to quality 
employment outcomes. DVR policy uniformly references applying “the least possible cost” for 
services and RSA did not find exception processes in DVR’s policy manual as required by 34 
C.F.R. § 361.50(c)(2)(ii). Additionally, RSA noted examples of policies and procedures related 
to the application of financial participation to individuals receiving SSI or SSDI. Although 
DVR’s policy manual states that individuals receiving SSI or SSDI benefits are exempt from 
financial needs testing, DVR reported applying policies uniformly to individuals receiving 
services, including SSI or SSDI recipients, that require their participation in the cost of services. 
For example—    
 
10.5 Financial Participation—Individuals eligible to receive SSI or SSDI benefits for 
disability or blindness who pursue self-employment are subject to DVR policies 
addressing individual contribution of start-up costs.  
In this example, start-up costs are routinely needed services for a self-employment plan. This 
policy, as written, is not in compliance with Federal requirements and effectively limits the 
opportunity for these individuals to pursue self-employment. When identified on an IPE, these 
service costs must be paid by the VR agency for individuals determined eligible for Social 
Security benefits under Titles II or XVI of the Social Security Act.  
Services and goods to meet the VR needs of an individual must be individualized, ensure 
informed choice, and determined jointly by the VR counselor and the eligible individual 
resulting in the development of the IPE. The agency must establish policies governing the 
provision of services in accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.50 and may not require participation in 
the cost of services on an IPE for individuals who have been determined eligible for Social 
Security benefits under Titles II or XVI of the Social Security Act (34 C.F.R. §361.54(b)(3)(ii)). 
The VR agency, after applying comparable benefits, must pay the cost of services on an IPE for 
these individuals.

14 
Understandably, an agency when exercising cost containment measures, may choose to apply a 
principle of “least cost” to services. However, this practice may interfere with an individual 
exercising informed choice or may result in a participant receiving services of lesser quality or 
receiving services from a provider that has been poorly evaluated by former participants. As 
written, the policies below do not appear to allow for variances in the quality of services and 
appear subjective when determining if the services or goods meet the needs of the individual.   
 
12.1.1(d) Exceeding Least Possible Cost to Meet Vocational Rehabilitation Needs— 
All services and goods provided to an individual shall meet the vocational rehabilitation 
needs of the individual and be at the least possible cost to DVR. When an individual 
chooses a more expensive service or good from among alternatives that all fully meet the 
individual’s vocational rehabilitation needs, DVR shall not be responsible for those costs 
in excess of the least costly alternative. 
 
3.6 Necessary and Appropriate Services at Least Possible Cost—The DVR counseling 
team shall ensure that the individual and, as appropriate, the individual’s authorized 
representative, understand that the informed choice process shall occur within the 
parameters of the DVR policy requirements that all vocational rehabilitation goods and 
services be necessary and appropriate. Once the necessity and appropriateness of a 
service or good is determined, DVR shall procure the service/good at the least possible 
cost to DVR. 
The Rehabilitation Act does not require individuals to participate in the cost of services—that is 
entirely up to the VR agency, which must develop written policies to implement financial needs 
tests and cost participation. In the policy excerpt below, there is no mention of assisting an 
individual in exploring comparable benefits or securing alternative financial assistance or 
services to meet the chosen goal. The interruption in the provision of services may not occur 
pending an appeal.   
10.5.2(a) Individual’s Failure to Contribute Required Resources—If the individual’s 
financial situation, as per the financial need determination, has not changed when the 
time for the individual’s financial participation arrives and the individual does not have 
the money they have agreed to pay under the plan or is no longer willing to contribute 
toward the payment of goods or services, there shall be an interruption in provision of 
planned goods and services until: 
  The individual can acquire resources to make the required contribution. 
The individual and DVR Counselor can redevelop the plan in terms of its 
employment outcome and/or necessary goods and services so that a financial 
contribution is not required. 
 
Whenever the DVR counseling team cannot support an individual’s choice, the 
individual’s record shall reflect the assistance and support offered to develop alternative 
goals and/or plans and clearly document the reasons for the counseling team’s decisions.

To maximize employment and self-sufficiency for individuals with disabilities, VR agencies may

12.2.6(g) Advanced Degrees (Graduate School)— If the vocational goal can be attained

opportunities for individuals with disabilities.   
 
Technical Assistance 
 
● RSA reviewed RSA TAC 22-03, Prohibition Against Applying Financial Needs Tests or 
Requiring Cost Participation for SSI/SSDI Recipients. The TAC states: 
Regulations in 34 C.F.R. § 361.54(b)(3)(ii) prohibit agencies from applying a financial 
needs test or requiring financial participation as a condition for providing VR services to 
individuals with disabilities eligible to receive SSI or SSDI benefits. In other words, if a 
State VR agency maintains written policies for applying a financial needs test or 
requiring client financial participation in the cost of VR services, SSI and SSDI recipients 
are exempt from both under 34 C.F.R. § 361.54(b)(3)(ii). This means that participants in 
receipt of SSI or SSDI are not required to participate financially for any service on the 
IPE even if a State has a policy requiring financial participation at private or out-of-state 
colleges. Exceptions must be granted, so that services are not limited, and services must 
be consistent with what the agency and the individual agree to include on the IPE 
following the mandatory procedures at 34 C.F.R. § 361.45(d).  
● RSA encouraged DVR to eliminate perceptions of strict and inflexible budgetary 
limitations and stressed VR program requirements in 34 C.F.R. § 361.50(a), which states 
that an agency’s policies must ensure that the provision of services is based on the 
rehabilitation needs of each individual, as identified in the IPE and consistent with 
informed choice. While DVR must administer its VR program in an efficient manner by 
ensuring that the costs to meet the unique rehabilitation needs of each individual are both 
necessary and reasonable under 2 C.F.R. § 200.403(a), RSA indicated that cost should 
not be the only factor in determining appropriate services to the individuals DVR serves. 
DVR is required by 34 C.F.R. § 361.50 to develop policies on the provision of services to 
individuals that ensure an appropriate balance between fully meeting the rehabilitation 
and employment needs of the individual and ensuring that the associated costs are 
reasonable and necessary. 
● 34 C.F.R. § 361.50(c) outlines the regulatory provisions for payment of services. Fee 
schedules are allowable as long as their levels are not so low as to effectively deny an 
individual a necessary service and are not absolute, i.e., they incorporate provisions to

16 
ensure that the rehabilitation needs of the individual are met, notwithstanding the fee 
schedule level for a particular service. Likewise, VR agency policies cannot establish 
absolute inflexible dollar limits on specific service categories or on the total services 
provided to an individual and must have exception processes in place. As with other cost 
containment strategies, a policy with an exception provision is allowable only to the 
extent that it provides the necessary safeguards to ensure the effective implementation of 
the exception, so that the individual's rehabilitation needs are met. VR agencies may not 
require an individual to use loans to offset costs of services. A policy that effectively 
precludes the provision of out-of-State services is prohibited under 34 C.F.R. § 
361.50(b). 
● Policies that restrict or place limitations on the type, degree, and costs of support 
provided to individuals attending both in-State and out-of-State postsecondary 
institutions can deter opportunities for individuals with disabilities to maximize their 
employment and careers. The full investment in postsecondary training offers VR 
agencies an avenue for continuous improvement in performance and the quality of 
employment outcomes for the individuals they serve.

Finding 2.3 Insufficient Data Validation Policies, Procedures, and Internal Controls 
 
Issue: Did DVR maintain effective internal controls over the Federal award to provide a 
reasonable assurance that it was managing the Federal award in compliance with Federal 
statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award in accordance with 2 C.F.R. § 
200.303 and the requirements at 34 C.F.R. § 361.47(a). 
 
Requirement: Pursuant to 2 C.F.R. § 200.303, VR agencies are required to develop an internal 
controls process to provide reasonable assurances regarding the effectiveness and efficiency of 
operations, reliability of reporting for internal and external use, and implemented as a measure of 
checks and balances to ensure proper expenditure of funds, including the evaluation, and 
monitoring of compliance with statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of Federal 
awards. Furthermore, a State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified 
or Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. Specifically, 2 C.F.R. § 200.303 requires a non-
Federal entity to—  
 
● Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award. These internal controls should comply with guidance in Standards for Internal 
Control in the Federal Government issued by the Comptroller General of the United 
States and the Internal Control Integrated Framework issued by the Committee of 
Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO);  
● Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards; 
● Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statutes, regulations, and 
the terms and conditions of Federal awards; and 
● Take prompt action when instances of non-compliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings. 
 
An internal control deficiency would exist when the design or operation of a control does not 
allow management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to 
prevent or correct processes that might lead to non-compliance with Federal and State 
requirements.  
 
Pursuant to 34 C.F.R. § 361.47(a), VR agencies must maintain for each applicant and eligible 
individual a record of services that includes, to the extent pertinent, documentation including, but

Requirements: A State VR agency must assure, in the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, that it will employ methods of administration that ensure the proper and 
efficient administration of the VR program. These methods of administration (i.e., the agency’s 
internal controls) must include procedures to ensure accurate data collection and financial 
accountability (34 C.F.R. § 361.12). 
 
“Internal controls” means a process, implemented by a non-Federal entity, designed to provide 
reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in the following categories:   
 
● Effectiveness and efficiency of operations;  
● Reliability of reporting for internal and external use; and 
● Compliance with applicable laws and regulations (2 C.F.R. § 200.61). 
 
Additionally, the Uniform Guidance at 2 C.F.R. § 200.62(a)(3) defines “internal control over 
compliance requirements for Federal awards” as a process implemented by a grantee that 
provides reasonable assurance that, among other things, that transactions are accurately recorded 
and accounted for to demonstrate compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms 
and conditions of the Federal award. In accordance with the Uniform Guidance, 2 C.F.R. § 
200.303, among other things, a non-Federal entity must—  
 
● Establish and maintain effective internal control over the Federal award that provides 
reasonable assurance that the non-Federal entity is managing the Federal award in 
compliance with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
award…;  
● Comply with Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of the Federal 
awards; 
● Evaluate and monitor the non-Federal entity’s compliance with statute, regulations, and 
the terms and conditions of Federal awards; and 
● Take prompt action when instances of noncompliance are identified, including 
noncompliance identified in audit findings. 
 
In accordance with the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a)), a State’s financial 
management systems, including records documenting compliance with Federal statutes, 
regulations, and the terms and conditions of the award, must be sufficient to permit the—

34 
● Preparation of reports required by general and program-specific terms and conditions; 
and 
● Tracing of funds to a level of expenditures adequate to establish that such funds have 
been used according to the Federal statutes, regulations, and the terms and conditions of 
the Federal award. 
The Uniform Guidance, at 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(b), requires the financial management system of 
each non-Federal entity to provide for the following—   
● Identification, in its accounts, of all Federal awards received and expended and the 
Federal programs under which they were received; 
● Accurate, current, and complete disclosure of the financial results of each Federal award 
or program in accordance with the reporting requirements set forth in 2 C.F.R. § 200.3 
Records that identify adequately the source and application of funds for federally funded 
activities, which must contain authorizations, obligations, unobligated balances, assets, 
expenditures, income and interest and be supported by source documentation; and 
● Effective control over, and accountability for, all funds, property, and other assets, and 
must adequately safeguard all assets and assure that they are used solely for authorized 
purposes. In addition, 34 C.F.R. § 76.702 requires States to use fiscal control and fund 
accounting procedures that ensure proper disbursement of and accounting for Federal 
funds.   
In its guidance titled The Role of Internal Control, Documenting Internal Control, and 
Determining Allowability & Use of Funds, the Department states that internal controls represent 
those processes by which an organization assures operational objectives are achieved efficiently, 
effectively, and with reliable, compliant reporting. 
 
Therefore, an internal control deficiency would exist when the design or operation of a control 
does not allow management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned 
functions, to prevent or correct processes that might lead to noncompliance with Federal and 
State requirements. 
 
Analysis: During the review, including discussions with DVR management and review of the 
agency’s policy manuals, RSA found that the agency did not have sufficient policies, procedures, 
and internal controls to ensure compliance with applicable Federal requirements. RSA found that 
written processes either did not exist or did not include the details necessary to provide a 
reasonable assurance that the agency is managing the award in compliance with Federal statutes, 
requirements, and the terms and conditions of the award. While the agency had general policies, 
there were few written procedures or processes for implementing the policies. For example, the 
agency’s documentation did not include the benchmark for ensuring compliance, who was 
responsible for the internal control, how the control would be reviewed periodically to determine 
whether it was effective, and how noncompliance, if identified, would be corrected. 
 
For certain processes, such as internal controls for period of performance and prior approval, 
DVR was able to describe steps taken to address some of these concerns; however, the processes 
were not documented. Consequently, DVR was unable to demonstrate that adequate safeguards

35 
and internal controls were documented, implemented, and in compliance with Federal 
requirements. 
 
Additionally, there are unclear linkages between various components. For example, DVR does 
not have a comprehensive index that clearly identifies all internal controls, processes, and 
procedures. DVR does not have a unifying organizing feature (such as an index) that identifies 
all current required control activities, management directives, and memoranda. As mentioned 
previously, a staffing vacancy exists in the key role of policy administrator in the agency. DVR’s 
policy administrator would play a key role in reviewing and updating the internal controls, and 
processes and procedures. When considering staff turnover in key positions, the lack of 
documented internal control processes may result in loss of continuity necessary to identify and 
correct non-compliance. 
Insufficient Internal Controls for Period of Performance 
The amount reported as obligations must be assigned to the Federal award within the period of 
availability for the award, as required by 2 C.F.R. § 200.302(a), and non-Federal expenditures

Finding 3.3: SWAP Contracts with Unnecessary Requirements and Inaccurate Information 
Issue: Does the VR agency manage contracts and agreements with public schools in accordance 
with VR program requirements regarding use of third-party cooperative arrangements (TPCA) as

44 
a source of match, and the terms and conditions of the Federal award, including compliance with 
applicable State procurement rules.  
Requirement: VR regulations detailing TPCAs in 34 C.F.R. § 361.28 require the DSU to ensure 
services provided by the cooperating agency are not customary or typical, are available only to 
eligible applicants and recipients, are assigned to the VR award under the administrative 
supervision of the DSU and meet requirements of the VR services portion of the Unified or 
Combined State Plan, including a State’s order of selection (OOS). These requirements apply to 
all VR services provided under a TPCA. VR regulations describe TPCA requirements that 
generally fall within the following six areas.  
The Cooperating Agency Must Furnish All or Part of the Non-Federal Share. The third-party 
cooperating agency must be a State or local public agency that furnishes all or part of the 
non-Federal share per 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a) and be under the administrative supervision or 
control of a government other than the Federal government (34 C.F.R. § 77.1). VR agencies 
cannot develop TPCAs with private non-profit or for-profit entities. 
 
New or Modified Services with a VR Focus. The second requirement for TPCAs is “the 
services provided by the cooperating agency are not the customary or typical services 
provided by that agency but are new services that have a VR focus or existing services that 
have been modified, adapted, expanded, or reconfigured to have a VR focus” (34 C.F.R.  
§ 361.28(a)(1)). 
 
Services are Only Available to Applicants for, or Recipients of, VR Services. The third 
regulatory requirement for TPCAs is that “the services provided by the cooperating agency 
are only available to applicants for, or recipients of, services from the designated State unit” 
(34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a)(2)). All individuals must be applicants or determined eligible for the 
VR program to receive any of the VR services provided under 34 C.F.R. § 361.48(b), 
including when those services are provided under a TPCA. 
 
The DSU Maintains Administrative Supervision. The fourth regulatory requirement for 
TPCAs is that “program expenditures and staff providing services under the cooperative 
arrangement are under the administrative supervision of the designated State unit” in 
accordance with 34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a)(3). Staff providing services under the TPCA must not 
be employees of the VR agency. Requirements regarding non-delegable VR program duties 
are outlined in 34 C.F.R. § 361.13. 
 
VR Services Portion of the Unified or Combined State Plan Requirements Apply. The fifth 
regulatory requirement for TPCAs is that “all requirements of the VR services portion of the 
Unified or Combined State Plan, including a State’s order of selection (OOS), will apply to 
all services provided under the cooperative arrangement” (34 C.F.R. § 361.28(a)(4)). If an 
agency is on an OOS, the TPCA program must also adhere to the OOS for the individuals 
that it serves.  
 
Statewideness. If a TPCA does not comply with the statewideness requirement in 34 C.F.R.  
§ 361.25, the State unit must obtain a waiver of statewideness, in accordance with § 361.26.

45 
The VR agency’s internal controls must ensure that all costs under the award are allocable to the 
VR program in accordance with the relative benefits received (2 C.F.R. § 200.405). Importantly, 
the Uniform Guidance requires the State to follow the same procurement rules it follows to 
acquire goods or services paid using State funds when acquiring goods or services paid using 
Federal funds. 
Analysis: The SWAP is DVR’s initiative to provide VR services through contracts with 
cooperating Colorado school districts and BOCES.  
Per SWAP contract boilerplate language, the purpose of SWAP is to assist students and youth 
with disabilities in making the transition from school to employment, provide successful 
employment outcomes, increase community linkages, and provide new patterns of services for 
students and youth with disabilities within all categories of disabilities. The program’s goals 
include (a) building on current transition planning efforts; (b) creating a career pathway through 
pre-employment transition services; (c) filling the case management gap for the target 
population; and (d) supporting collaboration between DVR and local school districts and 
BOCES. 
The RSA team reviewed a sample of DVR’s SWAP contracts, including the processes DVR uses 
to manage oversight. RSA noted several areas of concern regarding how the agency historically 
managed its SWAP contracts, and how it manages the program currently. Below is a summary of 
these concerns.  
 
1. Unnecessary Requirements. Prior to the current review period, DVR operated SWAP 
contracts as TPCAs. DVR acknowledged that during the period under review, it did not 
consider its SWAP contracts to be TPCAs; however, DVR continued to hold partners 
responsible for carrying out activities that would otherwise have been required under 
TPCAs. RSA questions the necessity and reasonableness of costs partners paid to 
facilitate activities required under TPCAs during the period in which DVR no longer 
operated SWAP contracts as TPCAs. 
 
For example, when DVR no longer considered SWAP contracts to be TPCAs, it still 
required SWAP partners to carry out activities of the system DVR previously used to 
certify personnel expenditures, for the time cooperating agency staff spent providing 
direct VR services pursuant to a TPCA meeting the requirements of 34 C.F.R. § 361.28, 
despite DVR having restructured SWAP contracts to generate VR program match 
through direct interagency cash transfers. As a result, school partners under SWAP 
contracts continued to carry out unnecessary and overly administratively burdensome 
processes related to certification of personnel expenditures only necessary under TPCAs 
requiring DVR to certify personnel expenditures. Moreover, it is concerning that partners 
continued to carry out the unnecessary processes after DVR discontinued engaging in 
reviewing or using the substantial volume of documentation partners continued to prepare 
and submit to DVR after DVR no longer needed them. 
 
2. Inaccurate Information. RSA noted additional concerns regarding inaccurate, unclear, 
or outdated language present in SWAP contracts that were effective during the scope of

46 
RSA’s review. For example, SWAP contracts included language self-identifying the 
contracts as TPCAs, which occurred after the point at which DVR had determined SWAP 
contracts were no longer TPCAs. When DVR obligated SWAP contracts effective during 
the period under review, it considered SWAP contracts to be contracts for the provision 
of VR services that included an interagency cash transfer component to generate match 
for the VR program— and not TPCAs requiring certification of personnel expenditures. 
This occurred because DVR generated new or continuation SWAP contracts that retained 
outdated language, carried over and unrevised, from previous contracts that were in place 
when DVR identified SWAP contracts as TPCAs requiring DVR to certify personnel 
expenditures. 
In addition to the unnecessary requirements and inaccurate information identified above, RSA 
noted the following concerns regarding DVR’s SWAP contracts: 
 
● SWAP contracts do not include language sufficient to meet program requirements for 
partners to trace and report pre-employment transition service costs for individual VR 
consumers at the necessary level of detail. (See Section 2, B, Provision of Pre-
employment Transition Services Statewide); 
● While DVR has not implemented agency-wide fee-for-service rates, partners frequently 
extrapolated total contract costs to generate per-service rates calculated according to each 
individual contract’s not-to-exceed allowance divided by the amount of services provided 
per month within the school year. While DVR demonstrated a system for periodic SWAP 
contract performance monitoring, it lacks such a system for monitoring costs-benefit, or 
price value;  
● Such contracts, where partners extrapolated and reported costs on a quasi-fee-per-service 
basis, resulted in costs per services that fluctuated widely month-to-month over the 
course of the school year. Under SWAP contracts where For other contracts partners 
determined costs for service provision using the monthly contract payment (contract 
amount divided by 12), over the period of the contract as the base. This monthly amount 
was divided by the actual number of services provided each month resulting in a wide 
variance in the costs paid per service.  This method of cost allocation undermined basing 
costs on a fee-for-service schedule, which would have established costs on a per-service 
basis according to pre-determined rates and other factors. Under these circumstances, 
resulting costs may have obfuscated the actual cost of service provision, since during 
busy months rates per service rendered were reported well below actual per service rates, 
while during less busy months, rates may have far exceeded actual per service rates;  
● Under some SWAP contracts, costs were required to be 50 percent for pre-employment 
transition services and 50 percent for other VR services. Such a costing structure made it 
unlikely that invoices were based on the actual delivery of services as needed by the 
population being served under the contract, but instead supported cost assignment based 
on the 50-50 split. A contract requiring costs to be divided on such a basis, where costs 
must be split according to the proportionality defined in the contract for types of services 
to be delivered without respect to which services were needed, discourages partners from 
delivering services in proportion to the needs of the population; and 
● Some contracts were unclear regarding how partners would apply indirect costs and on 
what basis or authority such costs were to be recorded and assigned to the Federal award.

RSA is sensitive to DVR’s request for consideration of the needs of SWAP partners with limited 
resources for whom the established rates for services may be insufficient. However, DVR must 
establish a reasonable base cost for the provision of the contracted services to ensure the average 
cost per service is in accordance with the benefit provided to the VR program and contractors are 
paid uniformly for the same service.  
DVR may supplement the base rate for different allowable circumstances. For example, some 
VR agencies pay a base rate per service to all providers. Then, they create a travel adjustment for 
service providers that permit an additional reasonable hourly fee to be paid per hour of travel 
over X number of minutes. This is acceptable because the mileage rate paid to the provider for 
travel does not include personnel time spent traveling. This type of adjustment benefits rural 
providers that may spend considerably more time traveling to provide services. The important 
factor is that the costs are reasonable and in proportion to the benefit received by the VR 
program. The reasonable and proportional costs can be tracked to a function that is directly 
benefiting VR. Even if DVR does not use a fee-for-service structure, it must still have a process 
to determine the reasonableness of rates paid to the providers and ensure the costs assigned to the 
VR program are consistent, reasonable, allocable and necessary. 
Conclusion: Based on the analysis, DVR’s SWAP contracts include unnecessary requirements 
that result in burdensome and costly activities serving little or no purpose in carrying out 
allowable activities under the award. Relatedly, contracts include inaccurate information that 
confuses the scope and purpose of the activities in relation to allowable award activities, as 
required per the terms of the agreement and the award. DVR’s contracts must be internally 
consistent and include correct legal references and requirements. As a steward of Federal funds, 
DVR is responsible for ensuring those funds are used only for allowable VR purposes. 
Inconsistent contract language adds unnecessary risk to DVR’s ability to adequately enforce 
contract provisions and protect Federal funds.

3.3.2  Based on the plan, review all SWAP and other contracts to identify language that needs
